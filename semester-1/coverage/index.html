Published course descriptions

MA 101 · Calculus I

The major themes correspond to the titles of the published description; The instructional order and details are suggested until the current teacher's syllabus is available.

  1. Limits

    8 question types · 3 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • the limit is calculated by direct substitution when it is legitimate, and it is known when it is not.
    • The limit of a fraction in which the substitution gives the form 0/0 is calculated by factoring or multiplying the conjugate.
    • Differentiate between the value of a function at a point and its limit at that point.
    • Rule that the limit does not exist if the two side limits are different.
    • Calculate limits at infinity and trigonometric limits, explain bounds, and interpret the definition of a limit.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Interpret epsilon and delta using a linear example. General proofs in real analysis are beyond this lesson.
    • L'Hopital's rule requires additional conditions and derivation; It is not a substitute for the methods of this lesson.

  2. Continuity

    5 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Check the three conditions for continuity at a point and identify any condition that fails.
    • Classify discontinuities as removable, jump, or infinite.
    • Find a constant that makes a piecewise function continuous where its pieces meet.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Continuity on intervals and uniform continuity are beyond this lesson.
    • The mean value theorem uses continuity and is covered in a later lesson.
    • We do not prove here that elementary functions are continuous on their domains; We use that as a fact.

  3. Derivative as a rate

    5 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Calculate a derivative at a point from its limit definition.
    • Interpret a derivative as tangent slope and instantaneous rate of change.
    • Differentiate between the average rate of change over a interval and the instantaneous rate at a point.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Differentiation rules (power, product, quotient and chain) come later. This lesson uses the definition.
    • Higher derivatives are not in this lesson.
    • We do not prove here that differentiability necessitates continuity; We remember it and use it.
    • The derivation of trigonometric and exponential functions is not here.

  4. Differentiation rules

    10 question types · 3 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • The external operation in the function is determined, and the rule is chosen from it, not by experimentation.
    • The product and division rules are applied along with the arrangement of terms and their signs.
    • The chain rule is applied, separating the inner function from the outer function.
    • Derive trigonometric, exponential, logarithmic, and inverse functions, and use implicit and higher differentiation.

    Lesson boundaries

    • We derive real functions on domains in which they are defined and differentiable; We do not make formulas valid at points where these conditions fail.
    • Rules for hyperbolic functions and general proofs of inverse functions are not required in this entry.

  5. Related rates

    5 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Convert the description of the change of related quantities into an equation and time rates in their units.
    • Differentiate the relationship with respect to time before substituting values at a particular instant.
    • Interprets the rate sign and verifies the consistency of units in geometry and motion.

    Lesson boundaries

    • The lesson assumes fixed geometric shapes and differentiable relationships; Models in which the shape changes require additional equations.
    • The solution of differential equations over a time interval is studied later; Here we calculate the rate at a specific moment.

  6. Linear approximation

    5 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • It builds a linear approximation around a suitable point and calculates a close value.
    • It calculates the differential and approximate relative error and differentiates between the approximation and the exact value.
    • Determines the direction of the concavity approximation error and defines the limits of its use.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Exact error bounds and higher Taylor formulas are studied later; We give no guarantee of accuracy without estimating a second derivative on the interval.

  7. Rolle and Mean Value Theorems

    5 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Check Rolle's conditions and the mean value theorem before using the result.
    • Find a point at which the instantaneous slope equals the average slope and verify its range.
    • Apply the theorem to motion and explain why a failed hypothesis removes its guarantee without necessarily disproving its conclusion.

    Lesson boundaries

    • We show the justification of the theorem using the existence of extrema; The foundational proof of the extreme value theorem is not included in this lesson.

  8. Extrema and curve shape

    5 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Determine the domain, critical numbers, and intervals of increase and decrease.
    • Concavity and inflection points are determined by sign testing.
    • Distinguish local from absolute extrema and combine the information into a consistent graph.

    Lesson boundaries

    • The drawing here is for functions with one variable; Multivariable surfaces are taught in a later course.
    • Numerical graphics aid understanding but do not replace sign and range testing.

  9. Optimization

    5 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • The objective function is written in one variable using the constraint equation.
    • Determine the feasible domain and check its endpoints as well as its critical points.
    • Justify whether the critical point is maximum or minimum instead of assuming that it is.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Optimization with two or more variables and Lagrange multipliers are not in this lesson.
    • Optimization under constraints of inequalities (linear programming) is out of scope.
    • Numerical methods for optimization are not here.
    • Optimization problems that require trigonometric or exponential functions are not in this lesson.

PHY 101 · General Physics I

General Description: Fundamentals of Physics, Laws of Motion, Applications, Laboratory, and Graphing. These eight units are a proposed educational division; A list of the teacher's experiences is not attached.

  1. Units and measurement

    6 question types · 3 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Convert between units in the order of conversion factors so that unwanted units are omitted.
    • Determine the number of significant figures in a multiplication or division result, and round to it.
    • Dimensional analysis is used to judge an equation before solving it.
    • Differentiates between rounding in addition and multiplication and preserves numbers until the end of the calculation.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Initial estimates of uncertainty are connected to the lab lesson and the graph.
    • Detailed statistical analysis and propagation of uncertainty in several variables require further study.

  2. Vectors and motion

    6 question types · 3 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Distinguish distance from displacement, and speed from velocity.
    • You read the position curve and the velocity curve: slope, area, and what they indicate.
    • You choose the equation of motion with constant acceleration from the data and the unknown.
    • It solves the motion of a projectile with two components and explains the independence of horizontal and vertical motion.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Calculations here for motion with constant acceleration; Movement with changing air resistance requires an additional model.

  3. Newton laws and force diagrams

    5 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • You first identify the body and then draw the external forces on it alone in a free-body diagram.
    • ΣF = ma is applied to each axis, and calculates acceleration, force, or mass.
    • Distinguish a third-law pair, which acts on two different bodies, from balanced forces acting on one body.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Friction and the inclined plane are the topic of the next lesson; Here we assume surfaces are smooth unless otherwise stated.
    • Moments and rotational equilibrium are not covered in this lesson.
    • Accelerated systems as frames of reference (imaginary forces) are out of scope.
    • Air resistance is neglected in all of the examples in this lesson.

  4. Friction and inclined surfaces

    5 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Differentiate between static and kinetic friction and choose the appropriate model for each situation.
    • The force of friction is calculated from the coefficient and the normal force on a horizontal surface.
    • Decompose the weight into two components on an inclined plane and calculate the correct normal force.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Rolling friction and fluid resistance are not covered in this lesson.
    • The dependence of the coefficient of friction on speed, temperature and surface condition is neglected here.
    • Pulley systems and interconnected bodies on ramps are outside the scope of this lesson.
    • Friction in circular motion is the topic of the circular motion lesson.

  5. Work energy and power

    6 question types · 3 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • You calculate the work from the force, the displacement, and the angle between them, and read its sign.
    • Apply conservation of mechanical energy when non-conservative forces do no net work on the chosen system.
    • Power is calculated from work and time, and is distinguished from work itself.
    • Spring energy and frictional work are calculated within the energy budget.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Damped oscillations and relativistic energy are beyond this lesson.
    • We calculate mechanical energy within specific forces and models; Air resistance is negligible unless stated.

  6. Impulse and momentum

    5 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Calculate momentum and impulse, and connect them through the impulse–momentum theorem.
    • It applies momentum conservation to an isolated system while preserving velocity sign.
    • Differentiate between elastic and inelastic collisions in terms of what is saved and what is lost.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Collisions in two dimensions and factored momentum into two components are not in this lesson.
    • The center of mass and its motion are out of scope here.
    • Systems with variable mass, such as rockets, are not in this lesson.
    • The coefficient of restitution is not covered here.

  7. Circular motion

    5 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Centripetal acceleration and centripetal force are calculated from velocity, radius, and mass.
    • Identify the real forces whose radial resultant provides the centripetal force.
    • Relate period to speed on a circular path.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Irregular circular motion (change in speed) is not in this lesson.
    • Rotation of rigid bodies, torque and rotational inertia are out of range.
    • Oblique curves (banking) are not counted here.
    • Movement in rotating frames and imaginary forces are mentioned for correction and are not calculated.

  8. Laboratory graphs and evidence

    6 question types · 3 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • You choose the two axes that make the relationship linear before you calculate any slope.
    • You calculate the slope from two points on the alignment line, and read its unit and physical meaning.
    • It differentiates between a reading within the measurement range and an extrapolation outside it, and does not claim more than what the data supports.
    • It estimates uncertainty and uses the slope of the best line while checking units and bounds.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Weighted regression and detailed significance tests require a statistics course.
    • These are general laboratory skills, not a verified list of your lecturer’s experiments.

CHEM 101 · General Chemistry I

The major themes correspond to the titles of the published description; The instructional order and details are suggested until the current teacher's syllabus is available.

  1. Atomic structure

    6 question types · 3 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Extracts the number of protons, neutrons and electrons from the nuclear symbol and from the charge.
    • Differentiate between atomic number, mass number, and average atomic mass.
    • The average atomic mass is calculated from the masses and abundances of isotopes.
    • Convert between the number of particles, moles, and mass and interpret chemical formulas.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Quantum numbers are covered in the quantum theory lesson. Electron configurations, Hund’s rule and ions are covered in the periodic table lesson.
    • Nuclear binding energy and radioactive decay are beyond this lesson.
    • Polyatomic ions such as sulfates are not here; We limit ourselves to monoatomic ions.

  2. Quantum theory

    6 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Relates wavelength, frequency, and photon energy with unit conversion.
    • Interpret discrete spectra and transitions between energy levels.
    • Determine allowed quantum numbers and electron capacities of shells, subshells and orbitals.
    • De Broglie and uncertainty are used without converting the quantum model to a planetary orbit.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Mathematically solving the Schrödinger equation and multi-electron perturbations is outside this entry.
    • The Bohr numerical level formula here is for hydrogen; It does not generalize to multi-electron atoms.

  3. Periodic table

    6 question types · 3 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • The period and number of valence electrons are determined from the atomic number.
    • The effective nuclear charge is approximately calculated from the atomic number and the number of blocking electrons.
    • Compares two elements in radius and ionization energy based on effective nuclear charge.
    • Write the electron configuration and explain ions and jumps in ionization and magnetism.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Quantum numbers are covered in the quantum theory lesson.
    • Detailed Slater-rule calculations and orbital diagrams for metal complexes are beyond this lesson.

  4. Chemical bonding

    6 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Count valence electrons, draw Lewis diagrams, and verify formal charge.
    • Use electron domains to determine molecular geometry and polarity.
    • Distinguish ionic and covalent bonds and attractive forces between molecules.
    • It relates the bond order to its length and strength and understands the introduction to the molecular orbitals.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Hybridization, Lewis and VSEPR are bounded models; It does not provide a complete quantum description of all compounds.
    • Spectroscopic details and diagrams of advanced metal complexes are studied later.

  5. Reactions in solution

    6 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Write molecular and net ionic equations while conserving atoms and charge.
    • It predicts precipitation and neutralization and identifies spectator ions.
    • The limiting reactant and mass of the precipitate are calculated from the concentration and volume.
    • Identify oxidation and reduction and balance electron transfer in a simple example.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Detailed equilibria of weak acids and calculation of the dissolution product in complex systems require broader equilibrium lessons.
    • The dissolution rules used are indicative for water under usual conditions; They are not a substitute for equilibrium constants at specific concentrations.

  6. Properties of solutions

    7 question types · 3 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Molarity is calculated from the mass of the solute and the volume of the solution, first converting the mass to moles.
    • Use conservation of solute amount to calculate an unknown volume or concentration after dilution.
    • Choose the appropriate concentration scale, and differentiate between molarity and mass percentage.
    • Interpret the solubility and calculate the colligative properties of a dilute solution.

    Lesson boundaries

    • The colligative laws shown are approximations for dilute solutions; Actual Vanthoff factor may vary due to interaction of ions.
    • Multispecies equilibrium calculations and non-ideal concentrated solutions require broader models.
    • Reaction and limiting substance calculations are linked to the lesson on reactions in solution.

  7. Properties of gases

    6 question types · 3 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Convert to Kelvin before any gas calculations, and you will learn why Celsius does not work in ratios.
    • PV = nRT and the two-state law apply in units consistent with the gas constant used.
    • You calculate the partial pressure from the mole fraction, and differentiate it from the total pressure.
    • Use kinetic molecular theory and Graham’s law, and relate gas amounts to reaction stoichiometry.

    Lesson boundaries

    • Real state equations and detailed velocity distributions go beyond preliminary calculations here.

  8. Thermochemistry

    7 question types · 3 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • It defines the system and its surroundings, and applies sign conventions from the perspective of the system.
    • The heat transferred from q = m c ΔT is calculated with an ordered temperature difference.
    • Heat contents are summed by Hess's law while preserving the signs.
    • It links the first law to calorimetry, Hess's law, heats of formation, and bonds.

    Lesson boundaries

    • The heat capacities are approximately constant within the problem domain; Complex thermal transformations require detailed data.
    • We relate Gibbs energy to the lesson on electrochemistry; Extended treatment of entropy is not in this entry.

  9. Electrochemistry

    6 question types · 2 Solved examples · Drawing, table, and interactive experiment

    Lesson objectives and limitations
    • Identify the anode, cathode and electron-flow direction, and distinguish galvanic from electrolytic cells.
    • The cell potential and Gibbs energy are calculated from the reduction potentials.
    • Nernst is used at 25°C with the correct reaction quotient.
    • The amount of substance in electrolysis is calculated from the current and time.

    Lesson boundaries

    • We use equilibrium potentials and activity that approximate the concentration in dilute solutions; Operating voltage differences may be affected by resistance and overvoltages.
    • Industrial battery design, electrode kinetics and detailed corrosion are studied later.
