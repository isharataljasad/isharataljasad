MA 101 · TOPIC 09

Optimization

Build a feasible one-variable model before differentiating.

Book →Pearson →Educator →

The idea

Name the quantity to optimise and the constraint. Use the constraint to reduce the objective to one variable. Identify the allowed domain, find candidates, and justify the winning value by endpoints or derivative signs.

A first example

A rectangle with perimeter 20 m has area A=x(10−x). The derivative 10−2x vanishes at x=5, giving the maximum area 25 m².

Engineering connection. A design optimum is meaningful only for the model and constraints used. Real equipment requires further engineering checks.

What this explanation develops

  • The objective function is written in one variable using the constraint equation.
  • Determine the feasible domain and check its endpoints as well as its critical points.
  • Justify whether the critical point is maximum or minimum instead of assuming that it is.

Definitions & meaning

Objective function
The quantity to be maximized or minimized: area, volume, cost, or distance. It is first written as it comes naturally, even with two variables, and then reduced to one variable.
Constraint
An equation linking the two variables, its source is given in the question: the length of an available fence, or a fixed perimeter, Or required volume. It is your only tool for deleting a variable from the objective function, so derivation is not valid before using it.
Critical point
A critical number c lies in the domain and satisfies f′(c)=0 or has an undefined derivative. It is a candidate for an extremum, not a guarantee. For example, x³ has a stationary point at 0 but no local maximum or minimum there.
Feasible domain
Values that have meaning in the problem, not in algebra alone: lengths are positive, The cut piece is smaller than half the side, and so on. Its definition is not formal: it is what excludes unacceptable roots and identifies the parties that must be examined.
Second derivative test
Suppose f′(c)=0 and the second derivative exists near c. If f″(c)>0, f has a local minimum at c; if f″(c)<0, it has a local maximum. If f″(c)=0, this test is inconclusive. Check the first derivative on both sides or use another argument.

Formulas & conditions

Critical point condition

f′(c) = 0 at inner limit

A necessary, not sufficient, condition: every internal limit is critical, and not every critical limit is an limit.

Second derivative test

f″(c) < 0 ⟹ maximum, f″(c) > 0 ⟹ minor

Faster than checking sign when the second derivative is smoothed out.

The absolute limit

Compare f at critical points and at both ends of the range

On a closed interval, the absolute limit is one of these values.

Why solving f′(x) = 0 is not enough

The saved step is “differentiate and equal to zero,” which gives an incorrect answer in two common cases.

  1. Take f(x) = x² on the interval [1, 4] and ask for the largest value.

    A small example reveals the problem more clearly than general words.

  2. The derivative 2x zeros at x = 0, which is completely outside the interval.

    The memorized method gives nothing here, yet the function definitely has the largest value.

  3. Examine the terminals : f(1) = 1 and f(4) = 16, the maximum is 16 at the terminal.

    The function is increasing over the entire interval, with the largest value at the far right, The derivative does not exist there, nor does it need to exist.

  4. The second case: a root of the derivative lies outside the geometrically permissible range, or on its edge, giving a zero value.

    As in the box problem below: The derivative has two roots, one of which makes the magnitude zero. Algebra does not know that the length is not negative nor that the volume is zero; You are the one who knows.

Steps five and seven are the ones that are skipped the most and are the ones that lead to the most losing grades. The eighth step deserves a pause: many mathematically correct solutions answer another question. Dimension is given where space is required. Read the question again before writing the answer.

Worked examples

Draw the shape and label its dimensions in letters before any calculations. Then write two separate lines: The line “I want to maximize/minimize..” is the goal function, and the line “It is given that..” which is the constraint. Then use the constraint to delete a variable. Then write the domain that is explicitly allowed by the inequality. And then just derive. Finally, return to the question: What exactly was asked?

Example 1 · The largest area with a fixed perimeter

A rectangle with perimeter 40 m. is its largest possible area?

  1. Target: A = x y. and constraint: 2x + 2y = 40, i.e. y = 20 − x.

    Separating the goal from the restriction prevents confusion between them, which is the most common mistake of this type.

  2. Replace : A(x) = x(20 − x) = 20x − x².

    The function now has one variable, and this is the condition for differentiation. The derivation is not valid before this step.

  3. The range : 0 < x < 20 because both lengths are positive.

    The field determines what roots we accept and what edges we examine. Skipping it is the root of most mistakes.

  4. Derive : A′ = 20 − 2x = 0 from x = 10, and A″ = −2 < 0 is maximum.

    The second derivative is negative and constant, so the point is definitely maximum and there is no need to examine the edges here; If left untested, it would be a claim without evidence.

  5. Area A = 10 × 10 = 100 m².

    The question is about space, not distance, so the answer is 100, not 10. Note that the optimal shape is a square, and this is a general result for a fixed perimeter.

Result: A = 100 m²

Example 2 · An open box from a square plate

A square plate with side 12 cm. is cut from its corners with squares of side x, then the ends are folded. Which x gives the largest volume?

  1. Dimensions of base (12 − 2x) in (12 − 2x), height x, The volume is V = x(12 − 2x)².

    It is cut from both ends together, so the decrease is 2x, not x., and this is a frequent error.

  2. The range: 0 < x < 6, because 12 − 2x must remain positive.

    At x = 6 the base is non-existent and the volume is non-existent; This is a limit, not a solution.

  3. Decompose and differentiate : V = 4x³ − 48x² + 144x, so V′ = 12x² − 96x + 144 = 12(x − 2)(x − 6).

    Derivation before derivation is easier than the product rule here, and there are fewer errors.

  4. The two roots are 2 and 6., and the second is the end of the domain and gives V = 0, so it is excluded.

    Algebra gave two roots, and geometry excluded one. If 6 had been accepted, the answer would have been the smallest possible volume, not the largest.

  5. So x = 2 cm, and the volume is 2 × 8² = 128 cm³.

    Examination of both ends confirms: at x → 0 and at x → 6 the volume becomes zero. The internal value is the greatest.

Result: x = 2 cm, V = 128 cm³

Further worked applications

Type 1: The largest area with a fixed perimeter

You use the constraint to delete a variable, and answer what you were asked about.

A rectangle with perimeter 40 m. is its largest possible area in square metres?

y = 20 − x at A = 20x − x², A′ = 20 − 2x = 0 at x = 10. The A″ = −2 < 0 is a maximum, and the space is 10 × 10 = 100 m².

Result: 100 m²

The second type: The smallest sum of two squares

Justify that the point is a minimum, not a maximum.

Two positive numbers whose sum is 12. What is the smallest value of the sum of their squares?

y = 12 − x to S = x² + (12 − x)² = 2x² − 24x + 144. S′ = 4x − 24 = 0 is at x = 6, and S″ = 4 > 0 is minor. The total is 36 + 36 = 72.

Result: 72 Number

The third type: Two roots, one of which is rejected

It excludes the root that falls outside the permitted range.

A square plate with sides 12 cm, whose corners are cut with squares with sides x, then folded to form an open box. What value of x in centimeters gives the largest volume?

V = x(12 − 2x)² and V′ = 12(x − 2)(x − 6), the roots are 2 and 6. The range is 0 < x < 6, so 6 is a term that gives zero volume. So x = 2 cm.

Result: 2 cm

Fourth style · limit at one limit

The terms are checked when the derivative does not exist within the interval.

What is the largest value of the function f(x) = x² on the closed interval from x = 1 to x = 4?

f′(x) = 2x zeros when x = 0 is outside the interval, so there are no critical points inside it. We examine the extremes : f(1) = 1 and f(4) = 16, the maximum is 16.

Result: 16 Number

Fifth style · A restriction with a missing side

The entry is written from form, not from habit.

A rectangular fence is intended next to a wall, so the side adjacent to the wall does not require a fence. Disposable 60 m of fence. What is the largest possible area in square metres?

The entry x + 2y = 60 is in x = 60 − 2y, and the space is A = xy = 60y − 2y². And A′ = 60 − 4y = 0 is at y = 15, then x = 30 and the space is 450 m².

Result: 450 m²

Details that matter

Because the function A = x(P/2 − x) is a parabola whose vertex is at the midpoint of its roots, It is the place where the two dimensions are equal. This is a result of the complete circumference. As for a wall that relieves you of a side, the answer is not square. As in the issue of the fence above.

The second derivative determines whether the point is maximum or minimum **locally**. If the domain is a closed interval, then the absolute limit may be at one limit. It is necessary to compare the values of the function at critical points and at both ends. If the field is open and the function leads to zero at both ends, then the critical point is what is required.

Eliminate it if it represents length or volume, as these are inherently positive quantities. This is the meaning of the permissible field: algebra does not know that the side is not negative, You are the one who imposes this condition. Do not drop the root without mentioning the reason for dropping it.

Useful background

Derivation rules

The force rule is (xⁿ)′ = n x^(n−1), and the product rule is (fg)′ = f′g + fg′. The objective functions in this lesson are mostly polynomials, so the differentiation is straightforward after expanding the parentheses. Expanding the bracket before derivation is easier than applying the product to most problems.

Solve a quadratic equation

By factoring : x² − 8x + 12 = (x − 2)(x − 6) = 0, the roots are 2 and 6. Or by common law x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a. When two roots appear, both are not necessarily acceptable: geometric constraints often exclude one or the other.

Scope of this explanation
  • Optimization with two or more variables and Lagrange multipliers are not in this lesson.
  • Optimization under constraints of inequalities (linear programming) is out of scope.
  • Numerical methods for optimization are not here.
  • Optimization problems that require trigonometric or exponential functions are not in this lesson.
