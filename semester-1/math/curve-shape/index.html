MA 101 · TOPIC 08

Extrema and curve shape

Use derivative signs and endpoints to justify a conclusion.

Book →Pearson →Educator →

The idea

A critical point is a candidate, not automatically an extremum. Use the sign of f′ to identify increasing and decreasing intervals, f″ for concavity, and include interval endpoints when comparing absolute extrema.

A first example

For f(x)=x²−4x on [0,3], the critical point is x=2. Compare f(0)=0, f(2)=−4 and f(3)=−3. The minimum is −4.

Engineering connection. A turning point in a cost model must lie inside the allowed operating range before it can be considered.

What this explanation develops

  • Determine the domain, critical numbers, and intervals of increase and decrease.
  • Concavity and inflection points are determined by sign testing.
  • Distinguish local from absolute extrema and combine the information into a consistent graph.

Definitions & meaning

Start with the domain and intersections before the derivation. In rational functions, examine the denominator; Zero may represent a hole after a factor shortcut or an asymptote if unbounded behavior remains. Do not connect the curve through a point outside the domain. At the far ends, the ends help define the shape of the drawing and the asymptotes.

For the first derivative table separate the axis at the zeros of the derivative and its indefinite points, while retaining the constraints of the original domain. Choose a test point in each interval. A positive derivative means that the function is increasing over an interval where the appropriate conditions are met; This does not mean that the value of the function itself is positive.

The second derivative describes the change in the slope, not the height of the graph. The function may be decreasing and concave upward if the slope is negative but close to zero. So draw two separate mental arrows: the direction of movement on the curve, and the direction of change in its slope. The first derivative table does not replace the second table.

Testing the second derivative at a smooth critical point is fast: f″(c)>0 is a local minimum, and if it is negative, it is a maximum. If it equals zero, the test is inconclusive; The functions x⁴ and x³ at zero are examples of two different results with zero second derivative.

To find the absolute value of [a,b] do not rely on the local maxima alone. Calculate f at each filter and at both ends and compare the numbers. Distinguish the coordinate x at which the extreme occurs from the maximum value of y; Writing one instead of the other is a mistake in understanding what is being asked.

Combine the results in one graph: two extreme points that do not agree with the sign table alert you to an error. Add units if x is a time and y is a temperature or position. The outline becomes a summary of the relationships, not an embellishment next to the solution.

Critical number
The number c within the domain of the function has f′(c)=0 or the derivative does not exist. Not every critical point is a maximum or minimum value. The point at which the function does not exist at all is not called critical for the function, even though it separates the sign table.
Local extremum
f(c) is larger or smaller than adjacent values within the range. Moving from a positive derivative to a negative one indicates a local maximum, and from a negative to a positive derivative indicates a local minimum. If the sign remains the same, there is no maximum value from this test.
Concavity and inflection
Concavity describes how the slope changes: f″ > 0 means the slope increases, and f″ < 0 means it decreases. An inflection point lies on a continuous curve where concavity changes. Solving f″ = 0 gives possible candidates only; check whether the sign of f″ changes.
Absolute extremum
The largest or smallest value over the entire studied field. For a continuous function on a finite closed interval there are two absolute values, and we compare the values ​​of the function at the interior critical points and the extremes. Over an open interval, a maximum value may not be achieved even though there is an upper or lower limit.

Worked examples

Domain → the first derivative and its signs → the second and its signs → point values → plot and check consistency.

Absolute comparison

Find the largest and smallest value of f=x³−3x by [−2,2].

  1. f′=3(x²−1), candidates −1 and 1

    Both are within the interval.

  2. f(−2)=−2، f(−1)=2، f(1)=−2، f(2)=2

    We include both sides in the comparison.

  3. The larger one is 2 and the smaller one is −2

    Absolute value may be achieved in more than one place.

Result: max=2, min=−2

Details that matter

Zero at one point is not enough; x² has zero derivative only at the origin. If the derivative does not exist in a complete interval and the conditions for the average value are met, then the function is constant on it.

Useful background

Factor sign table

For the expression (x−1)(x+1) we separate the axis at −1 and 1 and then test a point in each interval. The sign is positive outside the two roots and negative between them. Knowing the roots is not enough; We need the sign before and after each root.
