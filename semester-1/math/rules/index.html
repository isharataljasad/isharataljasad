Differentiation rules

Choose a rule from the structure of the function.

LearnCheckReferenceImageStep by stepPracticeApply

Bayt Al-Fuad

Before you start

What will you be able to do after this lesson?

  • The external operation in the function is determined, and the rule is chosen from it, not by experimentation.
  • The product and division rules are applied along with the arrangement of terms and their signs.
  • The chain rule is applied, separating the inner function from the outer function.
  • Derive trigonometric, exponential, logarithmic, and inverse functions, and use implicit and higher differentiation.

What this lesson does not cover

  • We derive real functions on domains in which they are defined and differentiable; We do not make formulas valid at points where these conditions fail.
  • Rules for hyperbolic functions and general proofs of inverse functions are not required in this entry.

We mentioned them so that you do not think that you have completed the whole topic.

THE IDEA

Understand the decision

Use the sum rule for sums, the product rule for products, the quotient rule for ratios, and the chain rule for composition. Differentiate the outer function, retain its input, then multiply by the derivative of that input. Trigonometric derivative rules assume radians.

Worked example

For y=(3x+1)², the chain rule gives y′=2(3x+1)·3. At x=1, y′=24.

Engineering connection Rate models often combine an exponential trend with another changing quantity; identify structure before manipulating symbols.

Approach 1 · Reference

Definitions and relationships

Differentiate basic functions only after checking their domains and units. Angles are in radians: the derivatives of sin x, cos x and tan x are cos x, −sin x and sec²x, respectively; tan x requires cos x ≠ 0. The derivative of eˣ is eˣ. For a positive constant a, the derivative of aˣ is aˣ ln a. The derivative of ln x is 1/x for x > 0; the derivative of ln|x| is 1/x for x ≠ 0.

The series is not just about forces. The derivative of sin(x²) is cos(x²)·2x, the derivative of e^(3x) is 3e^(3x), and the derivative of ln(1+x²) is 2x/(1+x²). Choose the outside function first, then leave its inside in place and multiply by the inside derivative. Derivatives of sec x, csc x and cot x are sec x tan x, −csc x cot x and −csc²x within their domains.

Implicit differentiation treats y as a function of x. From x²+y²=25, obtain 2x+2y y′=0, so y′=−x/y where y≠0. At (3,4), the slope is −3/4. The absence of an explicit formula y=f(x) does not make y constant. Differentiate again to obtain 2+2(y′)²+2y y″=0 and solve for the second derivative.

If the exponent and exponent change together like xˣ, the power rule with an exponent constant does not hold. On x>0 we write ln y=x ln x, then y′/y=ln x+1, so y′=xˣ(ln x+1). The derivative of arcsin x is 1/√(1−x²) on |x|<1, and arccos x is the negative of that, and arctan x is 1/(1+x²). The inverse function does not mean the inverse: arcsin x is different from 1/sin x.

The second derivative describes the change in the first derivative. In s(t)=t³ are v=3t² and a=6t. we do not square the derivative to get the second; We derive it again. The units change every time: m, then m/s, then m/s².

Power rule
For f(x)=xⁿ, the derivative is n x^(n−1) wherever the rule is valid. Multiply by the original exponent and reduce the exponent by one. For a general real exponent we use x>0; other domains and endpoints depend on the particular exponent. For general real exponents we assume x>0; The domain extension depends on an exponent, such as a polynomial or a root, and the endpoints are examined separately.
Product rule
‎(f g)′ = f′ g + f g′‎. Differentiate the first and leave the second, then leave the first and differentiate the second, then add. And note that it is not f′ g′; This is the most famous error in the lesson and has a reason that is explained in the derivation below.
Quotient rule
‎(f/g)′ = (f′ g − f g′)/g²‎. The order is important because the subtraction is not commutative: the numerator begins with the derivative of the numerator multiplied by the denominator. The denominator is squared and is not left as it is.
Chain rule
The derivative of f(g(x)) is f′(g(x)) · g′(x). Differentiate the external, keep the internal the same, then multiply by the derivative of the internal. The last factor is the one that is often forgotten, and neglecting it gives a result that seems reasonable but is wrong.

  • (f g)′ = f′ g + f g′The goatsTwo terms are sums, not the product of two derivatives.
  • (f / g)′ = (f′ g − f g′) / g²DivisionAn ordered subtraction, and the denominator is squared.
  • (f(g(x)))′ = f′(g(x)) · g′(x)SeriesDon't forget the last factor: the inner derivative.
  • (sin u)′=cos(u)u′; (eᵘ)′=eᵘu′Trigonometric and compound exponentialRadians in trigonometry.
  • (ln|u|)′=u′/uComplex logarithmu≠0.

Why is the derivative of the product not the product of the two derivatives?

This is every student's first intuition, and therefore deserves an honest response, not just a correction.

  1. Try f(x) = x and g(x) = x, their predecessors are x² and its derivative is 2x.Why: A counterexample is the shortest way to overthrow a proposed rule, and it does not require proof.
  2. But f′ g′ = 1 × 1 = 1, which is only equal to 2x at one point.Why: The proposed rule fell. The remaining question is: Why was the intuition wrong in the first place?
  3. Consider a rectangle with lengths f and g: with area f g. If the two lengths increase slightly, the area increases with two strips: one Δf × g and the other f × Δg.Why: The increase in product comes from two sides, not from one side: the first changes while the second remains, and vice versa.
  4. Divide the increase by Δx and take the limit: f′ g + f g′ remains, The product of the two increments disappears because it is small of the second order.Why: The terms in the base are the two bars, and multiplying the two derivatives corresponds to the small angle That fades away alone. Hence the addition, not the multiplication.

Approach 3 · Step-by-step teaching

From zero to solution

Before you write a letter, ask: If I replace x = 2, what is the last operation I perform? If it is plural, differentiate each term separately. If it is multiplication or division, use its rule. If it is an exponent raised by a magnitude other than x alone, then it is a series. You may need two rules in one problem, so always start with the external ones and then work your way down to the internal ones.

Example 1 · Product rule

Find f′(1) where f(x) = (3x² + 1)(x³ − 2).

  1. The external operation is multiplication in parentheses, so the rule is the product rule.Why: If you substituted a number, you would calculate the parentheses and then multiply them, as multiplication is the last thing you do.
  2. Name f = 3x² + 1 and g = x³ − 2, then f′ = 6x and g′ = 3x².Why: Naming the two parts prevents confusion when substituting, and is a step worth writing down rather than memorizing mentally.
  3. Apply: f′g + fg′ = 6x(x³ − 2) + (3x² + 1)(3x²).Why: Two sum terms: the first by the differentiation of the first, the second by the differentiation of the second, and there is no third for them.
  4. Replace x = 1: 6(1)(1 − 2) + (3 + 1)(3) = −6 + 12 = 6.Why: Substitution after symbolic simplification is less error-prone than substitution at each step.

Output: f′(1) = 6

Example 2 · String rule

Find f′(−2) where f(x) = (2x + 5)⁴.

  1. The inner one is g = 2x + 5 and the outer one is raised to the fourth power.Why: If you substitute a number, you add first and then raise, then the raising is external.
  2. Derive the outer and keep the inner : 4(2x + 5)³.Why: The power rule is applied to the entire expression as if it were a single variable, in this step only.
  3. Multiply by the internal derivative g′ = 2: f′ = 8(2x + 5)³.Why: This factor is what distinguishes the series from the power rule alone, and neglecting it reduces the result by half here.
  4. Replace x = −2: 2(−2) + 5 = 1 with f′ = 8(1)³ = 8.Why: Calculating the internals first simplifies the exponent to 1³ and avoids unnecessarily large numbers.

Output: f′(−2) = 8

Product and series together

Find the derivative of y=x²e^(3x).

  1. External operation hitWhy: Choose the product rule before the derivative of the exponential.
  2. y′=2xe^(3x)+x²(3e^(3x))Why: The derivative of 3x inside the exponent gives the factor 3.
  3. y′=e^(3x)(2x+3x²)Why: Add a common factor to clarify, not because the rule requires it.

Output: e^(3x)(2x+3x²)

Questions often left unexplained

Do I always need the division rule, or can I do without it?

It can often be dispensed with. Write f/g in the form f · g⁻¹ and use the multiplication with the string, The output is one. Some students find this less likely to cause subtraction order errors. But the exam may ask for the rule explicitly, so know it anyway.

How do I handle nested parentheses like ((x² + 1)³ + 2)⁴?

Apply the series once for each layer, from outside to inside, and multiply the results. Differentiate the fourth exponent first, keeping the inside, and then differentiate the inside with the series again. The rule does not change no matter how many layers there are, but the product becomes longer.

Why is the denominator squared in the division rule?

Because it results from writing f/g = f · g⁻¹ and applying the product and the series: The derivative of g⁻¹ is −g⁻² g′, so g² appears in the denominator when the two terms are unified. Square is a mathematical result, not a memorized convention.

Power, constant, and sum rules

Meaning

Power rules replace repeated limit calculations for polynomial, reciprocal, and radical powers wherever the expression is differentiable.

Method

Rewrite roots and reciprocals as exponents, differentiate each term, and retain constants as coefficients. A standalone constant has derivative zero. Check the original domain, since rewriting a negative power does not create a value at x=0.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=3x⁴−2√x+5/x, rewrite as 3x⁴−2x^(1/2)+5x^(−1). Then f′(x)=12x³−x^(−1/2)−5x^(−2), for x>0.

Check yourself

Differentiate 4/x³−7x²+9.

Reveal answer and reasoning

Write 4x^(−3)−7x²+9. The derivative is −12x^(−4)−14x.

Common error. The exponent falls by one after multiplying by the original exponent; it does not simply fall by one.

Product rule

Meaning

Two changing factors contribute separately to the change in their product. A derivative of a product is generally not the product of derivatives.

Method

Label the factors before differentiating. Differentiate one while holding the other, then reverse and add. Expand first only when that makes the algebra simpler; both routes must agree.

Worked reasoning

For y=(x²+1)eˣ, y′=2xeˣ+(x²+1)eˣ=eˣ(x²+2x+1).

Check yourself

Differentiate (x³−2)sin x.

Reveal answer and reasoning

3x² sin x+(x³−2)cos x.

Common error. Both product-rule terms must appear, even if one factor looks simple.

Quotient rule

Meaning

When a numerator and denominator both vary, their effects oppose one another in the derivative.

Method

Name numerator f and denominator g, write “denominator times numerator derivative minus numerator times denominator derivative,” and divide by g². Preserve parentheses in the numerator. If the denominator is a simple power, rewriting as a negative exponent may be shorter.

Worked reasoning

For y=(x²+1)/x, the quotient rule gives [2x·x−(x²+1)]/x²=(x²−1)/x². Rewriting y=x+1/x gives the same 1−1/x².

Check yourself

Differentiate sin x / x, for x≠0.

Reveal answer and reasoning

[x cos x−sin x]/x².

Common error. The denominator is squared as a whole; changing the order in the numerator changes the sign.

Derivatives of trigonometric functions

Meaning

The basic trigonometric derivatives support repeated differentiation of oscillating quantities. These rules require angles measured in radians.

Method

Use the six basic derivatives, then apply product, quotient, or chain rules when trig functions are combined or composed. Derive tan x from sin x/cos x if the sec²x rule is forgotten.

Worked reasoning

For y=3sin x−2tan x, y′=3cos x−2sec²x. For y=sin(5x), the chain rule adds the inner derivative, giving 5cos(5x).

Check yourself

Differentiate cot x+sec x.

Reveal answer and reasoning

−csc²x+sec x tan x.

Common error. The derivative of cos x is negative sin x; degree measure changes the formula.

Chain rule

Meaning

A change in x first changes the inner expression, which then changes the outer function. Multiply those rates.

Method

Mark the outer layer and inner layer. Differentiate the outer form while leaving the inner expression in place, then multiply by the derivative of the inner. For multiple nested layers, repeat from outside inward.

Worked reasoning

For y=(1+3x²)⁵, y′=5(1+3x²)⁴·6x=30x(1+3x²)⁴. The factor 6x comes from the inside.

Check yourself

Differentiate e^(x³) and sin²(2x).

Reveal answer and reasoning

The derivatives are 3x²e^(x³) and 4sin(2x)cos(2x), respectively.

Common error. If an outer derivative looks correct but lacks the inner derivative, the chain rule is incomplete.

Logarithmic and exponential derivatives

Meaning

Exponential differentiation reproduces the exponential while logarithmic differentiation converts products and powers into sums and coefficients.

Method

Use chain rule for an exponential or logarithm of an expression. For y=xˣ with x>0, take natural logs: ln y=x ln x; differentiate implicitly and multiply back by y. For a positive base a≠1, aˣ=e^(x ln a).

Worked reasoning

For y=xˣ, y′/y=ln x+1, so y′=xˣ(ln x+1). For ln(x²+1), the derivative is 2x/(x²+1).

Check yourself

Differentiate 3^(2x).

Reveal answer and reasoning

The derivative is 2 ln(3)·3^(2x).

Common error. The derivative of ln|u| is u′/u only where u≠0; do not discard a chain factor.

Basic and higher derivatives

Idea

Differentiate a sum term by term. The second derivative measures how the first derivative changes and later helps classify concavity and acceleration.

First move

Simplify a polynomial first, apply the power rule to each term, and keep the original variable. Differentiate again only when the question asks for acceleration, concavity, or higher-order change.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=4x³−5x+7, f′(x)=12x²−5 and f″(x)=24x. At x=2 the slope is 43 and the second derivative is 48.

Your turn

Find the velocity and acceleration when s(t)=t³−6t²+2t.

Reveal answer and reasoning

v(t)=3t²−12t+2; a(t)=6t−12. Position, velocity, and acceleration have successive time derivatives.

If it went wrong. The derivative of a constant is zero; do not leave the +7 or its analogue in a derivative.

Products, quotients, and nested functions

Idea

A product has two changing factors, so both contribute. A nested function has an inner rate, which multiplies the outer rate. A quotient has a denominator squared.

First move

Mark the factors and inner expression before differentiating. Do not apply the power rule to an entire product unless it has first been algebraically expanded.

Worked reasoning

For y=x²(3x+1)⁴, treat x² and (3x+1)⁴ as factors. Then y′=2x(3x+1)⁴+12x²(3x+1)³. The 12 comes from 4 times the inner derivative 3.

Your turn

Differentiate y=(x²+1)/(x−1).

Reveal answer and reasoning

y′=[2x(x−1)−(x²+1)]/(x−1)²=(x²−2x−1)/(x−1)², for x≠1.

If it went wrong. If your quotient numerator has a plus sign between the two products, recheck the quotient rule.

Trigonometric derivatives

Idea

These rules assume radians. When a trigonometric expression contains an inner function, multiply by its derivative.

First move

Identify the outer trig function and its argument. Differentiate the argument last. Check signs, especially for cosine.

Worked reasoning

For y=sin(3x²), the outer derivative is cos(3x²) and the inner derivative is 6x, so y′=6x cos(3x²).

Your turn

Differentiate y=cos(2x−1)+tan x.

Reveal answer and reasoning

y′=−2 sin(2x−1)+sec²x.

If it went wrong. The factor 2 is required by the chain rule; cosine also supplies a minus sign.

Exponential and logarithmic rates

Idea

The exponential e^x is its own derivative. A logarithm turns multiplication into addition and has a reciprocal rate. With a changing exponent or argument, the chain rule is still required.

First move

Identify the inner expression and its derivative. For ln|u|, work where u≠0; for ln u, require u>0.

Worked reasoning

For y=e^(2x) ln x on x>0, the product rule gives y′=2e^(2x)ln x+e^(2x)/x=e^(2x)(2ln x+1/x).

Your turn

Differentiate y=ln(x²+1).

Reveal answer and reasoning

y′=2x/(x²+1); the argument is positive for every real x.

If it went wrong. The derivative is not 1/(x²+1); the inner derivative 2x is essential.

Power, product, and quotient rules

Principle

Differentiate sums term by term. A product changes through either factor; a quotient’s derivative has a denominator squared and a subtraction in the numerator.

Why and when

Mark each factor before differentiating. For quotients, preserve parentheses around both numerator products and state where the denominator is nonzero.

Worked solution

For y=(x²+1)/(x−1), y′=[2x(x−1)−(x²+1)]/(x−1)²=(x²−2x−1)/(x−1)², x≠1.

Try it

Differentiate y=x²(x³+1).

Reveal answer and reasoning

y′=2x(x³+1)+3x⁴=5x⁴+2x. Expanding first to x⁵+x² gives the same result.

Common trap. The derivative of uv is not u′v′.

Trigonometric derivatives in radians

Principle

The standard trigonometric derivative rules use radian measure. Combining them with the chain rule handles oscillations whose angle changes at a different rate.

Why and when

Differentiate the outer trigonometric function, keep the original argument, then multiply by the derivative of the argument. Check the negative sign for cosine.

Worked solution

For y=sin(3x²), the outer derivative is cos(3x²) and the inner rate is 6x, so y′=6x cos(3x²).

Try it

Differentiate y=cos(2x−1)+tan x.

Reveal answer and reasoning

y′=−2sin(2x−1)+sec²x.

Common trap. If the angle were in degrees, these derivative formulas would need an extra conversion factor.

The chain rule for nested change

Principle

When an output depends on an intermediate quantity, rates multiply. The outer derivative is evaluated at the inner expression, not at x alone.

Why and when

Name the inner function u=g(x). Differentiate f(u) with respect to u and u with respect to x, then multiply. For several layers, repeat from outside inward.

Worked solution

For y=(1+x²)⁵, set u=1+x². Then dy/du=5u⁴ and du/dx=2x, so y′=10x(1+x²)⁴.

Try it

Differentiate y=√(1+3x²).

Reveal answer and reasoning

y′=(1/2)(1+3x²)^(−1/2)·6x=3x/√(1+3x²).

Common trap. The missing inner derivative is the most common source of a wrong chain-rule answer.

Exponential and logarithmic derivatives

Principle

The natural exponential has derivative equal to itself; the natural logarithm has reciprocal derivative. A changing exponent or argument still contributes its inner derivative.

Why and when

Check the function domain first. Apply chain or product rules as needed; for ln|u| the derivative formula holds where u≠0.

Worked solution

For y=e^(2x)ln x on x>0, y′=2e^(2x)ln x+e^(2x)/x=e^(2x)(2ln x+1/x).

Try it

Differentiate y=ln(x²+1).

Reveal answer and reasoning

y′=2x/(x²+1), defined for all real x.

Common trap. The reciprocal rule applies to the argument, so multiply by its derivative.

The first type is the power base

You subtract the exponent by a factor and subtract it by one.

The second type is the product rule

‎f′ = 2x(x − 3) + (x² + 1)(1) = 3x² − 6x + 1‎. And at x = 2: 12 − 12 + 1 = 1.

The third type is the division rule

Fourth style · Chain rule

Find f′(−2) where f(x) = (2x + 5)⁴.

‎f′ = 4(2x + 5)³ × 2 = 8(2x + 5)³‎. When x = −2 is 2x + 5 = 1, the result is 8 × 1 = 8.
