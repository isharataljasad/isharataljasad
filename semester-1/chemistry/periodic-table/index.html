Prerequisites

  • Atomic number and number of electrons

    You need it because: Everything in this lesson is based on the number and arrangement of electrons, which come from the atomic number.

    The atomic number Z is the number of protons, and is equal to the number of electrons in a neutral atom. Chlorine Z = 17 has seventeen electrons. As for the ion, its number of electrons is Z, with a minus charge.

    Lesson on the structure of the atom

  • Arrangement of electrons in levels

    You need it because: The period is the number of the last level, and the group is determined by the electrons of that level; Whoever does not arrange the electrons does not read the location of the element.

    The first level holds two electrons, the second has eight, and the third has eight in the first elements. For sodium, Z = 11 and the shell arrangement is 2, 8, 1: three occupied shells place it in period 3, And one electron at the end, so it is in the first group. and sulfur, Z = 16, has the shell arrangement 2, 8, 6: period 3 with six valence electrons.

THE IDEA

Understand the decision

Across a period, increasing effective nuclear attraction generally reduces atomic radius and raises first ionisation energy. Down a group, additional occupied shells usually increase size. Exceptions need electron-configuration reasoning.

Worked example

An electron arrangement 2,8,1 has one outer-shell electron. Losing that electron gives a common +1 ion.

Engineering connection Valence behaviour helps predict bonding and reactivity before a reaction is tested.

Approach 1 · Reference

Definitions and relationships

We construct the ground distribution by filling the lowest energy orbitals, with a maximum of two oppositely spind electrons in the Pauli orbit. According to Hund, the electrons of equal energy orbitals occupy single orbitals with parallel spins before pairing. Oxygen 1s²2s²2p⁴ has two lone electrons, so it is paramagnetic; The coupling of all gives diamagnetism.

For iron, Fe: [Ar]3d⁶4s², and when making Fe²⁺, the 4s letter is removed first, so it becomes [Ar]3d⁶; The order of filling is not the order of stripping that is constant for all cases. Chromium [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and copper [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ are two important exceptions to the mechanistic application of simple arrangement, resulting from convergent energies and interaction of electrons.

The second ionization energy removes an electron from the positive ion, not from a new atom. The large jump after the removal of valence electrons indicates the start of the removal of an inner electron; A closed heart has greater attraction. The direction of ionization across the period is general with exceptions such as Be/B and N/O due to the orbit type and pairing of electrons. In an isoelectronic chain, increasing the number of protons often reduces the radius.

Period
A period is a horizontal row of the periodic table. In the main-group examples used here, the period identifies the highest occupied principal shell. Moving across a period changes nuclear charge and valence-electron count.
Group
A vertical column, and its elements are similar in the number of valence electrons and thus in chemical behavior. This similarity is why the table is arranged in the first place: chemistry is governed by valence electrons.
Shielding
The inner electrons shield part of the nucleus's attraction from the valence electrons. The outer electron does not feel all the charge of the nucleus, but rather what remains of it after the inner electrons block it.
Effective nuclear charge (Z_eff)
The net attraction felt by a valence electron is : Z_eff ≈ Z − S, where S is the number of blocking electrons. It is the key that explains all the table trends in this lesson.
Atomic radius
Atomic radius describes atomic size using a stated convention, such as half the distance between two bonded identical atoms. Across a main-group period it generally decreases as effective nuclear attraction increases. Down a group it generally increases as additional shells are occupied.
Ionization energy
First ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous ground-state atom. It generally increases across a main-group period and decreases down a group, with exceptions explained by electron configurations. Radius alone does not determine every comparison.

The relationships you will use

  • Z_eff ≈ Z − SEffective chargeA simple approximation: S is the number of inner electrons.
  • Cross course: Z_eff ↑, radius ↓, IE ↑Horizontal directionThe level is the same, the charge increases, so the attraction increases.
  • Bottom group: n ↑, radius ↑, IE ↓Vertical directionA new level is further away and the blocking is greater, so the attraction weakens.
  • Fe²⁺: [Ar]3d⁶Removal of transition metal electrons4s is removed before 3d in this example.

Why does an atom get smaller as we add electrons through the cycle?

The result appears to be inverted: we add electrons and the volume becomes smaller. The reason is that the addition does not occur alone.

  1. Go from sodium Z = 11 to chlorine Z = 17 in the third cycle.Why: Comparison within a single cycle fixes the number of levels and isolates the effect of charge alone.
  2. Note that the added electrons enter **the same level**, not a new level.Why: The distance from the nucleus does not increase by adding a level; This is the difference from going down in a group.
  3. The number of internal electrons remained ten in both, so the blocking remained almost unchanged. While the protons increased by six.Why: The electrons of the same level mask each other weakly, so the increase in charge appears net in Z_eff.
  4. Z_eff rises from 1 to 7, and gravity pulls the cloud inward, making the radius smaller.Why: volume is not determined by the number of electrons, but by the intensity of attraction on them. This is why the chlorine atom is smaller than the sodium atom, even though it contains more electrons.

Approach 3 · Step-by-step teaching

From zero to solution

Start from the atomic number and distribute the electrons : 2, then 8, then 8. The number of occupied levels is the period, and the number of electrons of the last level is the valence electrons. Then calculate Z_eff = Z − S where S is the sum of electrons in the internal levels. Then every comparison became easy: a larger Z_eff in the same cycle means a smaller atom and greater ionization energy.

Example 1 · The location of an item and its effective charge

For the element Z = 16., determine its period, number of valence electrons, and approximately its effective nuclear charge.

  1. Distribute the electrons: 2, 8, 6.Why: The first level accommodates two, the second eight, leaving six for the third. Distribution is the source of everything that follows it.
  2. The number of occupied levels is three, so the element is in the third cycle.Why: The period is the number of the highest level occupied, neither the number of electrons nor the atomic number.
  3. The last level has six electrons, so they are valence electrons.Why: It determines the chemical behavior and location of the element in the group.
  4. Blocking S = 2 + 8 = 10, Z_eff ≈ 16 − 10 = 6.Why: Inner electrons alone are shielding; The electrons of the same level are weakly shielded and we neglect them.

Output: Period 3, six valence electrons, Z_eff ≈ 6

Example 2 · Comparing two sizes

Which is smaller: the sodium atom Z = 11 or the chlorine atom Z = 17? And why?

  1. They are both in the third period, and each has ten inner electrons.Why: Fixing the cycle isolates the effect of charge; Otherwise, the effect of adding a level would be mixed with the effect of increasing the protons.
  2. Z_eff(Na) ≈ 1 and Z_eff(Cl) ≈ 7.Why: The difference is seven times in net attraction, which is a huge difference that cannot be neglected.
  3. Chlorine is smaller because a stronger attraction pulls the electron cloud inward.Why: This is despite the fact that chlorine contains six more electrons; The volume is determined by the intensity of attraction, not the number of electrons.

Output: Cl is smaller (Z_eff 7 vs 1)

Questions often left unexplained

Why don't we count the electrons of the same level among the blocking?

Because it is located on average at the same distance from the nucleus, not between the electron and the nucleus. Its blocking is weak. The simple approximation neglects it completely. Slater's rules give it a small weight of about 0.35 per electron, which is beyond the scope of this lesson.

Is the ion the volume of its atom?

No. A positive ion is always smaller than its atom: it has lost an electron, so the repulsion is reduced, and perhaps an entire level is lost. The negative ion is larger than its atom: it gained an electron, increasing the repulsion, while the protons remained the same. The same attraction is distributed among more electrons.

Are the trends consistent without exception?

No. There are known exceptions to the ionization energy, such as the ionization energy of aluminum being lower than that of magnesium Even though he is later in the course. It is caused by the structure of the sublevels and not the effective charge alone. Its details are beyond the scope of this lesson. The trend is a general rule, not a law without exception.

Adapted Educator material selected for this topic.

Build electron configurations and ions

Idea. Electrons fill lower-energy orbitals first, subject to the Pauli exclusion principle and Hund’s rule. Valence electrons govern much chemistry. For main-group cations, electrons are removed from the outermost principal shell; transition-metal cations lose ns electrons before (n−1)d.

Decision. Count total electrons from Z and ion charge. Fill subshells in their usual order and check the final count. For an ion, remove electrons from the neutral configuration according to the outermost-shell rule.

Worked case. Neutral Na has 11 electrons: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹. Na+ has ten: 1s²2s²2p⁶. Neutral Fe is [Ar]4s²3d⁶; Fe2+ is [Ar]3d⁶.

Try this before revealing the answer

Give the electron configuration of Cl− (Z=17).

Reveal answer and reasoning

Cl− has 18 electrons, so its configuration is 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶, the same electron count as Ar.

Misconception check. Do not remove a 3d electron before a 4s electron when forming a typical first-row transition-metal cation.

Predict periodic trends with effective attraction

Idea. Across a period, increasing nuclear charge tends to pull valence electrons closer when shielding changes modestly. Down a group, additional occupied shells tend to increase atomic radius. Ionization energy is energy needed to remove an electron from a gas-phase atom; electron affinity describes electron addition.

Decision. Compare positions first, then check electron configurations for exceptions. Cations are smaller than their parent atoms; anions are larger. For an isoelectronic series, the ion with more protons attracts the same electron count more strongly.

Worked case. O2−, F−, Na+, and Mg2+ all have ten electrons. Their radius decreases as Z rises from 8 to 12; Mg2+ is smallest.

Try this before revealing the answer

Which is generally larger: Na or Na+? Which is larger in an isoelectronic pair: F− or Na+?

Reveal answer and reasoning

Na is larger than Na+ because it retains its outer 3s electron. F− is larger than Na+ because F− has fewer protons attracting the same ten electrons.

Misconception check. A periodic trend is a model with exceptions; do not claim every adjacent ionization energy follows a perfectly monotonic pattern.

Fill orbitals before predicting behavior

Question cue. An electron configuration or unpaired-electron count is needed.

Meaning. Subshell capacities follow the number of orbitals: s holds 2, p 6, d 10, f 14 electrons. Hund filling maximizes unpaired electrons before pairing within equal-energy orbitals.

First move. Count electrons from atomic number, adjust for ion charge, fill orbitals in energy order, and remove electrons from the highest principal shell first for common transition-metal cations.

Worked case. Neutral Fe is [Ar]4s²3d⁶; Fe²⁺ loses the two 4s electrons and is [Ar]3d⁶.

Try this before revealing the answer

Write the condensed configuration of Mg²⁺.

Reveal answer and reasoning

Neutral Mg is [Ne]3s²; losing two 3s electrons gives [Ne].

If stuck. Writing a transition-metal cation by removing 3d electrons before 4s often gives the wrong configuration.

Explain a periodic trend with attraction

Question cue. The question ranks atom size or ionization energy.

Meaning. Across a period, increasing effective nuclear charge often pulls valence electrons inward. Down a group, an added shell usually increases atomic radius. Ionization energy generally moves opposite atomic radius.

First move. Identify period and group first. Use shell number for a vertical comparison and nuclear attraction or shielding for a horizontal comparison, then look for known exceptions when precision matters.

Worked case. Across period 3, Na is larger than Cl. The stronger effective attraction in Cl helps hold its valence electrons more tightly.

Try this before revealing the answer

Which neutral atom is larger, K or Na? Which usually has the lower first ionization energy?

Reveal answer and reasoning

K is larger because it has an additional occupied shell and generally has the lower first ionization energy.

If stuck. Do not apply a simple across-period trend to compare an atom with its ion without accounting for electron gain or loss.

Adapted Book material selected for this topic.

Build an electron configuration

Principle. Fill lower-energy orbitals first, obey two electrons per orbital with opposite spins, and place single electrons across equal-energy orbitals before pairing.

Decision. For transition-metal cations, remove electrons from the highest principal shell first.

Worked case. Fe: [Ar] 4s²3d⁶; Fe²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁶.

Check. A configuration predicts valence behavior; it is not a drawing of fixed electron locations.

Predict periodic trends

Principle. Across a period, effective nuclear attraction generally grows: atomic radius falls while first ionization energy generally rises. Down a group, added shells enlarge atoms.

Decision. Account for subshell and electron-pair exceptions rather than treating trends as exact laws.

Worked case. Na is larger than Cl; Cl attracts a bonding electron pair more strongly.

Check. Ionic size must be compared at a specified charge and electron count.

Question types

Each style examines something different. These selected patterns cover the lesson objectives, It is not all that can be asked about the topic.

Type I: Effective nuclear charge

Blocking electrons are subtracted from the atomic number.

Sodium has an atomic number of 11 and a distribution of 2, 8, 1. What is approximately its effective nuclear charge?

The blocking electrons are internal : 2 + 8 = 10. Z_eff ≈ 11 − 10 = 1.

The second type: Determine the course

Count levels, not electrons.

An element with atomic number 16. is in which period it occurs?

Distribution 2, 8, 6 There are three occupied levels, and the element is in the third cycle.

The third type: valence electrons

You read the last level alone.

An element with atomic number 13., how many valence electrons does it have?

The distribution is 2, 8, 3, so there are three electrons in the last level.

The fourth type: Comparing two items

Isolate the effect of charge by stabilizing the cycle.

Sodium Z = 11 and chlorine Z = 17, both in the third period and have ten inner electrons. How much greater is the effective nuclear charge of chlorine than that of sodium?

Z_eff(Cl) = 17 − 10 = 7 and Z_eff(Na) = 11 − 10 = 1, 6. difference This is why the chlorine atom is smaller and its ionization energy is greater, even though it has more electrons.

Type 5: Counting the blocking electrons

Separates the interior from the exterior.

An element with atomic number 15 and distribution 2, 8, 5. How many electrons does it block?

Blocking from internal levels alone: 2 + 8 = 10. The five in the last level are valence electrons, and we neglect to block them in this approximation.
