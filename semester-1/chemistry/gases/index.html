CHEM 101 · TOPIC 07

Properties of gases

Use absolute temperature and state model assumptions.

Book →Pearson →Educator →

The idea

The ideal gas equation PV=nRT relates state variables. Use compatible units and temperature in kelvin. At constant amount and temperature, pressure varies inversely with volume. Real gases depart from ideal behaviour, especially when interactions or molecular volume matter.

A first example

For a fixed ideal-gas amount at fixed temperature, reducing volume from 4 L to 2 L doubles pressure.

Engineering connection. Gas calculations require a stated pressure basis and temperature; a number without conditions is incomplete.

What this explanation develops

  • Convert to Kelvin before any gas calculations, and you will learn why Celsius does not work in ratios.
  • PV = nRT and the two-state law apply in units consistent with the gas constant used.
  • You calculate the partial pressure from the mole fraction, and differentiate it from the total pressure.
  • Use kinetic molecular theory and Graham’s law, and relate gas amounts to reaction stoichiometry.

Definitions & meaning

Pressure results from molecules transferring momentum when they collide with a wall. In the ideal model, the molecules have no intrinsic volume and no attractions between them, and their average translational kinetic energy depends on the Kelvin alone. At the same temperature, the lighter gas is faster on average; Not all particles have the same speed.

Root mean square velocity urms=√(3RT/Mm) Molar mass kg/mol is used if R=8.314 J/(mol·K). Graham's law for flow from a small hole gives r1/r2=√(Mm2/Mm1) at the same temperature and conditions. Real gas deflects especially at high pressure and low temperature, when molecular volume and attraction become important.

When generating a gas by a reaction: balance the equation, calculate the moles of the limiting substance, then the moles of the gas with the coefficients, then PV=nRT. the coefficients are mole ratios, not mass ratios. If the gas is collected above water, subtract the water vapor pressure from the total pressure to get the pressure of the dry gas before using its law.

Absolute temperature
Temperature measured from absolute zero in Kelvin (: K = °C + 273.15.). Its zero is not a term, but rather a physical limit at which translational kinetic energy is non-existent. For this reason alone, it is suitable for ratios and division in the gas laws.
Ideal gas
A model that assumes three things: that the volume of the particles is negligible compared to the volume of the container; There are no forces of attraction between them, and their collisions are completely elastic. It is a good approximation at low pressures and high temperatures, and it gets worse as the gas approaches liquefaction.
Gas constant (R)
A constant that relates pressure and volume to moles and temperature. Its value is 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) or 8.314 J/(mol·K), which are the same value in two different units. Choosing the value forces you to choose the units of the remainder.
Partial pressure
The pressure that a single component in a gaseous mixture would create if it occupied the container alone. It is equal to the mole fraction multiplied by the total pressure: P_i = x_i P_total. The sum of the partial pressures is the total pressure, and this is Dalton's law.
Mole fraction
Moles of a component divided by the total moles of the mixture. A unitless number between zero and one, The sum of the mole fractions of the mixture's components equals one. It is not pressure, so it is not correct to write it as an answer to a question about pressure.

Why is Kelvin mandatory and Celsius not sufficient?

It is said, “Convert to Kelvin” for no reason, and it is forgotten at the first problem. The reason is simpler than one might think.

  1. Take a gas at 27 °C that is heated to 327 °C at constant pressure. If you calculated the percentage in Celsius, you would find it to be 327/27 ≈ 12.

    The ratio is what determines how much the volume is doubled, so its error multiplies the error of the answer twelve times.

  2. And in Kelvin : 600.15 / 300.15 ≈ 2.0, that is, the volume is doubled only once.

    A huge difference between the two answers stems from the choice of the measure alone, not the calculation.

  3. The reason is that zero Celsius is a term: it is the freezing point of water, not the absence of heat.

    The ratio between two measurements is meaningless unless zero is real. Otherwise, “twice ten degrees” would equal twenty, which is belied by the analogy.

  4. Try the threshold: at 0 °C the ratio becomes a division by zero, and at −10 °C it becomes negative.

    A negative or infinite volume is physically impossible, and this alone is proof that the scale is not valid for proportions. As for Kelvin, its zero is unreachable, so this problem never occurs.

Worked examples

Convert the temperature to Kelvin first, even before you read the rest of the question. Then ask: Do I have one condition or two? One case is solved by PV = nRT, The two cases are P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ with the constant deleted from them. If you use PV = nRT, choose the value of R first, then convert the pressure and volume to their units.

Example 1 · is a volume from the ideal gas law

What is the volume 2.00 mol of a gas at 300 K and pressure 1.50 atm? Take R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K).

  1. The temperature is originally given in Kelvin, so there is no conversion. Pressing atm corresponds to the selected R.

    Checking the modules before substitution is cheaper than discovering the error after.

  2. Sort: V = nRT/P.

    What is required is in the numerator and the remainder around it; Arranging the equation before substitution reduces calculator errors.

  3. Replace : V = (2.00 × 0.08206 × 300) / 1.50 = 49.24 / 1.50 = 32.8 L.

    The units are abbreviated : mol × L·atm/(mol·K) × K / atm, leaving only L. This confirms that the order is correct.

Result: V = 32.8 L

Example 2 · Two cases with heat conversion

A gas with volume 1.00 L at 27 °C is heated to 327 °C at constant pressure. What is its new volume?

  1. Convert : 27 °C = 300.15 K and 327 °C = 600.15 K.

    Always the first step. If Celsius had been left out, the ratio would have been 12 instead of 2, and the answer would be six times wrong.

  2. The pressure is constant, so remove it from both ends: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂. remains

    Deleting saves you the pressure value that was not given in the first place and is not needed.

  3. Solve: V₂ = V₁ × T₂/T₁ = 1.00 × 600.15/300.15 = 2.00 L.

    The absolute temperature doubled and the volume doubled. The relationship is direct. Heating with constant pressure increases the volume, not decreases it. This is a quick check of the answer.

Result: V₂ = 2.00 L

What is the volume 3.00 mol of an ideal gas at 400 K and pressure 2.00 atm? Take R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K).

‎V = nRT/P = (3.00 × 0.08206 × 400) / 2.00 = 98.47 / 2.00 = 49.2 L‎.

The third type: Two states with heating

Transform before you divide, and this is where the Kelvin effect appears.

A gas with volume 2.00 L at 27 °C is heated to 327 °C at constant pressure. What is its new volume in litres?

Convert : 300.15 K and 600.15 K. With constant pressure : V₂ = V₁ × T₂/T₁ = 2.00 × 600.15/300.15 = 4.00 L.

Result: 4 L

Type 4 · Moles of the state

Isolates n and checks the reasonableness of the output.

How many moles of an ideal gas occupy 11.2 L at 273.15 K and 1.00 atm pressure? Take R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K).

‎n = PV/RT = (1.00 × 11.2) / (0.08206 × 273.15) = 11.2 / 22.41 = 0.500 mol‎.

Result: 0.5 mol

Type 5: Partial pressure

Multiply the mole fraction by the total pressure and do not stop at the fraction.

A gas mixture containing 0.25 mol from A and 0.75 mol from B, and the total pressure is 4.0 atm. What is the partial pressure of A?

molar fraction x_A = 0.25 / 1.00 = 0.25, The partial pressure is P_A = 0.25 × 4.0 = 1.0 atm.

Result: 1 atm

Useful background

Calculate the number of moles

n = m / M where M is the molar mass of g/mol. Example: 64 g from O₂ has a molar mass of 32 g/mol, so the number of moles is 2.0 mol. Note that O₂ is a diatomic molecule, so its molar mass is twice the atomic mass.

Consistency of units with the gas constant

With R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) the pressure should be atm and the volume should be L And the temperature is in K. With R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), the pressure is Pa and the volume is m³. The practical rule: choose R first and then convert everything to its units, not the other way around.
