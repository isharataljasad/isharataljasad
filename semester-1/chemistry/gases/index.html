Before you start

What will you be able to do after this lesson?

  • Convert to Kelvin before any gas calculations, and you will learn why Celsius does not work in ratios.
  • PV = nRT and the two-state law apply in units consistent with the gas constant used.
  • You calculate the partial pressure from the mole fraction, and differentiate it from the total pressure.
  • Use kinetic molecular theory and Graham’s law, and relate gas amounts to reaction stoichiometry.

Prerequisites

  • Calculate the number of moles

    You need it because: Letter n In the ideal gas law moles do not fines, and many problems give you mass.

    n = m / M where M is the molar mass of g/mol. Example: 64 g from O₂ has a molar mass of 32 g/mol, so the number of moles is 2.0 mol. Note that O₂ is a diatomic molecule, so its molar mass is twice the atomic mass.

    Converting mass to moles in the solutions lesson

  • Consistency of units with the gas constant

    You need it because: The value of R varies with units, so using a value with other units silently gives an incorrect result.

    With R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) the pressure should be atm and the volume should be L And the temperature is in K. With R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), the pressure is Pa and the volume is m³. The practical rule: choose R first and then convert everything to its units, not the other way around.

THE IDEA

Understand the decision

The ideal gas equation PV=nRT relates state variables. Use compatible units and temperature in kelvin. At constant amount and temperature, pressure varies inversely with volume. Real gases depart from ideal behaviour, especially when interactions or molecular volume matter.

Worked example

For a fixed ideal-gas amount at fixed temperature, reducing volume from 4 L to 2 L doubles pressure.

Engineering connection Gas calculations require a stated pressure basis and temperature; a number without conditions is incomplete.

Approach 1 · Reference

Definitions and relationships

Pressure results from molecules transferring momentum when they collide with a wall. In the ideal model, the molecules have no intrinsic volume and no attractions between them, and their average translational kinetic energy depends on the Kelvin alone. At the same temperature, the lighter gas is faster on average; Not all particles have the same speed.

Root mean square velocity urms=√(3RT/Mm) Molar mass kg/mol is used if R=8.314 J/(mol·K). Graham's law for flow from a small hole gives r1/r2=√(Mm2/Mm1) at the same temperature and conditions. Real gas deflects especially at high pressure and low temperature, when molecular volume and attraction become important.

When generating a gas by a reaction: balance the equation, calculate the moles of the limiting substance, then the moles of the gas with the coefficients, then PV=nRT. the coefficients are mole ratios, not mass ratios. If the gas is collected above water, subtract the water vapor pressure from the total pressure to get the pressure of the dry gas before using its law.

Absolute temperature
Temperature measured from absolute zero in Kelvin (: K = °C + 273.15.). Its zero is not a term, but rather a physical limit at which translational kinetic energy is non-existent. For this reason alone, it is suitable for ratios and division in the gas laws.
Ideal gas
A model that assumes three things: that the volume of the particles is negligible compared to the volume of the container; There are no forces of attraction between them, and their collisions are completely elastic. It is a good approximation at low pressures and high temperatures, and it gets worse as the gas approaches liquefaction.
Gas constant (R)
A constant that relates pressure and volume to moles and temperature. Its value is 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) or 8.314 J/(mol·K), which are the same value in two different units. Choosing the value forces you to choose the units of the remainder.
Partial pressure
The pressure that a single component in a gaseous mixture would create if it occupied the container alone. It is equal to the mole fraction multiplied by the total pressure: P_i = x_i P_total. The sum of the partial pressures is the total pressure, and this is Dalton's law.
Mole fraction
Moles of a component divided by the total moles of the mixture. A unitless number between zero and one, The sum of the mole fractions of the mixture's components equals one. It is not pressure, so it is not correct to write it as an answer to a question about pressure.

Why is Kelvin mandatory and Celsius not sufficient?

It is said, “Convert to Kelvin” for no reason, and it is forgotten at the first problem. The reason is simpler than one might think.

  1. Take a gas at 27 °C that is heated to 327 °C at constant pressure. If you calculated the percentage in Celsius, you would find it to be 327/27 ≈ 12.Why: The ratio is what determines how much the volume is doubled, so its error multiplies the error of the answer twelve times.
  2. And in Kelvin : 600.15 / 300.15 ≈ 2.0, that is, the volume is doubled only once.Why: A huge difference between the two answers stems from the choice of the measure alone, not the calculation.
  3. The reason is that zero Celsius is a term: it is the freezing point of water, not the absence of heat.Why: The ratio between two measurements is meaningless unless zero is real. Otherwise, “twice ten degrees” would equal twenty, which is belied by the analogy.
  4. Try the threshold: at 0 °C the ratio becomes a division by zero, and at −10 °C it becomes negative.Why: A negative or infinite volume is physically impossible, and this alone is proof that the scale is not valid for proportions. As for Kelvin, its zero is unreachable, so this problem never occurs.

Approach 3 · Step-by-step teaching

From zero to solution

Convert the temperature to Kelvin first, even before you read the rest of the question. Then ask: Do I have one condition or two? One case is solved by PV = nRT, The two cases are P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ with the constant deleted from them. If you use PV = nRT, choose the value of R first, then convert the pressure and volume to their units.

Example 1 · is a volume from the ideal gas law

What is the volume 2.00 mol of a gas at 300 K and pressure 1.50 atm? Take R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K).

  1. The temperature is originally given in Kelvin, so there is no conversion. Pressing atm corresponds to the selected R.Why: Checking the modules before substitution is cheaper than discovering the error after.
  2. Sort: V = nRT/P.Why: What is required is in the numerator and the remainder around it; Arranging the equation before substitution reduces calculator errors.
  3. Replace : V = (2.00 × 0.08206 × 300) / 1.50 = 49.24 / 1.50 = 32.8 L.Why: The units are abbreviated : mol × L·atm/(mol·K) × K / atm, leaving only L. This confirms that the order is correct.

Output: V = 32.8 L

Example 2 · Two cases with heat conversion

A gas with volume 1.00 L at 27 °C is heated to 327 °C at constant pressure. What is its new volume?

  1. Convert : 27 °C = 300.15 K and 327 °C = 600.15 K.Why: Always the first step. If Celsius had been left out, the ratio would have been 12 instead of 2, and the answer would be six times wrong.
  2. The pressure is constant, so remove it from both ends: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂. remainsWhy: Deleting saves you the pressure value that was not given in the first place and is not needed.
  3. Solve: V₂ = V₁ × T₂/T₁ = 1.00 × 600.15/300.15 = 2.00 L.Why: The absolute temperature doubled and the volume doubled. The relationship is direct. Heating with constant pressure increases the volume, not decreases it. This is a quick check of the answer.

Output: V₂ = 2.00 L

Two gases at the same temperature

Compare the flow rate of He, its molar mass is 4, and O2, its mass is 32.

  1. rHe/rO2=√(32/4)Why: The mass is in the inverse ratio.
  2. √8=2.828Why: We do not use the mass ratio without the root.
  3. Helium is about 2.83 times fasterWhy: It has the lowest molar mass.

Output: 2.828

Questions often left unexplained

Why don't we convert the temperature differences into kelvins?

Because the difference is the same in the two scales: one degree Celsius is equal to one kelvin in magnitude. The height of 10 °C is the height of 10 K. The conversion is necessary for the absolute value, not the difference, because the difference is in the starting point, not in the length of the degree.

When does the ideal gas model break in practice?

At high pressures, the volume of the particles themselves becomes negligible compared to the volume of the container. At low temperatures, the forces of attraction become effective and the gas approaches liquefaction. In these two terms, a correction such as the van der Waals equation is used, which is outside the scope of this lesson.

Is the volume of a mole of oxygen different from a mole of hydrogen under the same conditions?

No, ideally. Avogadro's law says that volume depends on the number of moles alone It does not depend on the type of gas, because the model neglects the volume of the molecule and its forces. In reality, there is a slight difference, which is one of the manifestations of the deviation of real gases from ideal ones.

Use the ideal gas model conditionally

Idea. The ideal gas model treats particles as having negligible volume and no intermolecular attraction between collisions. Pressure, volume, amount, and absolute temperature determine its equilibrium state.

Decision. Convert temperature to kelvins and match the gas constant to pressure and volume units. If the amount is fixed between two states, compare PV/T; at high pressure or low temperature, expect larger deviations from ideal behavior.

Worked case. One mole of ideal gas at 300 K in 24.6 L has pressure near 1.00 atm because R≈0.08206 L·atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Try this before revealing the answer

A fixed amount of ideal gas at constant volume warms from 300 K to 450 K. What is P2/P1?

Reveal answer and reasoning

At fixed n and V, P scales with absolute T. Thus P2/P1=450/300=1.50.

Misconception check. Never insert Celsius temperature directly into the ideal-gas equation or a gas-law ratio.

Add partial pressures and notice nonideal gases

Idea. Each ideal gas in a mixture contributes a partial pressure proportional to its mole fraction. Real gases deviate when intermolecular attractions or particle volume matter; the ideal model improves at lower pressure and higher temperature.

Decision. For a dry gas mixture, use mole fractions. For gas collected over water, subtract water vapor pressure from total pressure before calculating the dry gas amount.

Worked case. A sample collected over water has total pressure 750 torr and water vapor pressure 24 torr. Dry gas pressure is 726 torr.

Try this before revealing the answer

A mixture contains 2 mol He and 3 mol Ne at total pressure 1.0 atm. What is He partial pressure?

Reveal answer and reasoning

He mole fraction is 2/5, so PHe=0.40 atm.

Misconception check. Partial pressure of a wet gas is not the measured total pressure when water vapor is present.

Choose a gas model with its conditions

Question cue. Pressure, volume, temperature, and moles are related.

Meaning. An ideal gas relates P, V, n, and absolute T. It is most reliable when particles are dilute and intermolecular effects are small.

First move. Convert temperature to kelvin, choose consistent pressure and volume units, and use an R value that matches them. State which quantities stay fixed if using a two-state law.

Worked case. At 1.00 atm and 273 K, 1.00 mol ideal gas occupies about 22.4 L with R=0.08206 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Try this before revealing the answer

At fixed n and T, pressure doubles. What happens to volume?

Reveal answer and reasoning

Volume halves because PV=nRT remains constant for the stated model.

If stuck. Never insert Celsius directly into a gas-law temperature ratio.

Separate total gas pressure into components

Question cue. A mixture has partial pressures or mole fractions.

Meaning. For an ideal-gas mixture, each component contributes a pressure proportional to its mole fraction at common T and V. Collected gas over water includes water-vapor pressure.

First move. Find total gas moles and each mole fraction. Multiply by total pressure, or subtract vapor pressure when isolating a dry gas.

Worked case. A mixture with 1 mol N₂ and 3 mol O₂ at 2.0 atm has x(N₂)=1/4 and P(N₂)=0.50 atm.

Try this before revealing the answer

Gas is collected over water at total pressure 760 mmHg; water vapor is 24 mmHg. What is the dry-gas pressure?

Reveal answer and reasoning

736 mmHg, because the total is dry gas plus water vapor.

If stuck. A partial pressure is not the same as the pressure that component would have at a different volume.

Read gas trends from particle motion

Question cue. A question compares gas speed, effusion, or temperature.

Meaning. At equal temperature, gases share mean translational kinetic energy, but lighter molecules have larger root-mean-square speed. Effusion rate has an approximate inverse square-root molar-mass relation.

First move. Compare absolute temperatures for kinetic energy. For rms speed, use molar mass in kg/mol with SI R. For two gases at equal T, compare speeds or effusion rates using molar-mass square roots.

Worked case. At the same temperature, helium (M≈4 g/mol) has about sqrt(40/4)=3.16 times the rms speed of argon (M≈40 g/mol).

Try this before revealing the answer

At equal T, which effuses faster: H₂ or O₂, and by what approximate factor?

Reveal answer and reasoning

H₂; the idealized rate ratio is sqrt(32/2)=4.

If stuck. Equal temperature does not imply equal molecular speed; it implies equal average translational kinetic energy.

molar fraction x_A = 0.25 / 1.00 = 0.25, The partial pressure is P_A = 0.25 × 4.0 = 1.0 atm.
