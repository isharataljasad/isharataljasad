Properties of solutions

Choose a concentration measure and preserve solute amount.

Before you start

What will you be able to do after this lesson?

  • Molarity is calculated from the mass of the solute and the volume of the solution, first converting the mass to moles.
  • Use conservation of solute amount to calculate an unknown volume or concentration after dilution.
  • Choose the appropriate concentration scale, and differentiate between molarity and mass percentage.
  • Interpret the solubility and calculate the colligative properties of a dilute solution.

What this lesson does not cover

  • The colligative laws shown are approximations for dilute solutions; Actual Vanthoff factor may vary due to interaction of ions.
  • Multispecies equilibrium calculations and non-ideal concentrated solutions require broader models.
  • Reaction and limiting substance calculations are linked to the lesson on reactions in solution.

We mentioned them so that you do not think that you have completed the whole topic.

Prerequisites

  • Convert mass to moles

    You need it because: Molarity is defined in moles, not grams, so whoever divides grams by liters gets something else.

    The number of moles is n = m / M, where m is the mass in grams and M is the molar mass in g/mol. The molar mass is collected from the periodic table : M(NaCl) = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol. Example: 11.69 g from NaCl equals 11.69 / 58.44 = 0.200 mol.

    Atomic mass in the lesson on the structure of the atom

  • Convert milliliters to liters

    You need it because: The unit for molarity is mol/L, leaving the volume in milliliters gives a result a thousand times smaller.

    1 L = 1000 mL, the conversion is divided by one thousand: 250 mL = 0.250 L. The sign of error is quick: a typical laboratory solution concentration falls between 0.01 and 10 mol/L, If your output comes out in the hundreds, you probably did not convert the volume.

THE IDEA

Understand the decision

Molarity is amount of solute divided by solution volume in litres. For dilution of the same solute without reaction or loss, the amount stays constant: M₁V₁=M₂V₂. Intermolecular interactions and dissolved particle count influence solution properties.

Worked example

Dissolving 0.20 mol to make 0.50 L of solution gives 0.40 mol/L. Adding solvent lowers molarity without changing the solute amount.

Engineering connection Concentration links the volume of a process stream to the amount of material it carries.

Approach 1 · Reference

Definitions and relationships

Dissolution is a competition between the separation of solute and solvent particles and the formation of new interactions between them. The phrase “similar dissolves like” is rough evidence of these attractions. A saturated solution is in equilibrium with an excess solute at a specific temperature, and an unsaturated solution can dissolve more. Stirring increases the speed at which equilibrium is reached, but does not raise equilibrium solubility alone. The effect of heat on solids depends on the substance; Many gases decrease in solubility when heated.

For a dilute gas at constant temperature Henry's Law c=kH P gives the solubility relationship to the partial pressure of the gas above the solution; Note that some references define Henry's constant as the inverse. Collective properties depend, to a diluted approximation, on the number of particles: A nonvolatile solute lowers the vapor pressure of the solvent, raising its boiling and lowering its freezing.

In ΔTf=iKf m and ΔTb=iKb m, the symbol m is molality in the unit mol/kg of solvent, not molarity. The factor i approximates the number of particles produced: glucose 1 and NaCl approximates 2 in the dilute limit. We do not always assume perfect separation. Osmotic pressure Π=iMRT uses molarity and Kelvin, and describes the pressure needed to prevent net transit of the solvent through a semi-permeable membrane.

Solute and solvent
The solute is the component with the least amount that diffuses into the other, and the solvent the most. The solution is their sum. Most of the errors in this lesson are caused by confusing the “mass of the solvent” And the “mass of the solution” when calculating the mass percentage.
Molarity (M)
Moles of solute in one liter of solution, not of solvent : M = n / V in mol/L. For this reason, the solute is dissolved first, then the volume is brought to the mark, and a liter of water is not added to begin with.
Mass percent
The mass of the solute divided by the mass of the entire solution, multiplied by one hundred. It does not require molar mass or volume, and is useful when the molar mass is unknown Or when the solution is a mixture of uncertain composition.
Molality (m)
Moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. It differs from molarity in the denominator: this is attributed to the solvent by mass, and that to the solution by volume. Molality is preferred when temperature changes, because mass does not expand while volume does.
Dilution
Adding a solvent to a solution. The amount of solute does not change at all, the volume increases, but the concentration decreases. This is the content of the relationship C₁V₁ = C₂V₂.

Where did C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ come from and why is it not suitable for mixing

This relationship is often memorized and used out of place. If you saw where it came from, you would know when it breaks.

  1. Write the moles of solute before dilution: n₁ = C₁ × V₁.Why: This is just a reversal of the definition of molarity C = n/V, not a new law.
  2. Note that adding water does not add or subtract solute, so n₂ = n₁.Why: That's the whole idea: what is preserved is the amount of solute, not the concentration or the volume. The concentration decreases because the numerator is fixed and the denominator is increased.
  3. Write the moles after dilution as n₂ = C₂ × V₂ and equate the two expressions as : C₁V₁ = C₂V₂.Why: Equality is not between the two concentrations nor between the two sizes, but rather between the two products. Because each of them equals the same number of moles.
  4. This is why it breaks when two solutions containing a solute are mixed: then n = n₁ + n₂.Why: Derivation Assume that the additive is free of solute. If there is solute in it, the moles must be added Then divide by the total volume, and this is component balancing, not dilution.

Approach 3 · Step-by-step teaching

From zero to solution

Ask yourself two questions before any calculation: What have I been given, masses or moles? What position is required? Volume of solution or mass of solvent? The answer to them determines the equation alone. In dilution issues, always start with the question: Is the additive free of solute? If there is a solute in it, the issue is balancing the component, not diluting it.

Example 1 · Prepare a solution with a known concentration

What mass of NaCl is needed to prepare 250 mL from a solution of 0.100 M? Molar mass 58.44 g/mol.

  1. Convert volume: 250 mL = 0.250 L.Why: Molarity is in mol/L, so the volume must be in liters before any multiplication.
  2. Calculate the moles : n = C × V = 0.100 × 0.250 = 0.0250 mol.Why: Inverting the definition of molarity gives moles of concentration and volume directly.
  3. Convert to mass: m = n × M = 0.0250 × 58.44 = 1.46 g.Why: The scale weighs grams, not moles, so the last step is translation into what can be measured practically.

Output: 1.46 g

Example 2 · Dilution of a concentrated solution

To what volume do we dilute 25.0 mL from 2.00 M solution so that its concentration becomes 0.500 M?

  1. Check the condition: The additive is water with no solute, so the relationship C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ is valid.Why: The derivation is based on the constant moles; If the additive contained a solute, this condition would be invalid.
  2. Replace : 2.00 × 25.0 = 0.500 × V₂.Why: The units here are mL on both sides, and there is no need to convert as long as they are identical. Because it is deleted from both sides.
  3. Solve: V₂ = 50.0 / 0.500 = 100 mL.Why: The product is larger than the original volume. Here's a quick check: dilution always increases volume.
  4. Water added 100 − 25 = 75 mL, not 100 mL.Why: The question may ask for the final volume or the volume of water added, which are different. Read the requirements carefully before you write.

Output: V₂ = 100 mL (water added = 75 mL)

Anti-freezing solution

Dissolve 0.10 mol glucose in 0.50 kg water. Kf=1.86 °C·kg/mol.

  1. m=0.10/0.50=0.20 mol/kgWhy: The denominator is the mass of the solvent.
  2. i=1؛ ΔTf=1×1.86×0.20=0.372°CWhy: Glucose does not break down into ions.
  3. Tf≈0−0.372=−0.372°CWhy: The drop is subtracted from the freezing point of pure water at the same pressure.

Output: -0.372 °C

Questions often left unexplained

Why do we supplement the volume to the mark and not add a liter of water?

Because molarity is attributed to the volume of the **solution**, not the solvent. Dissolving the solute changes the volume slightly. If you added a full liter of water, it would be the final volume Greater than a liter, the concentration is less than required. This is why volumetric flasks are made with a single mark at the neck.

Does the concentration change if I take half the solution?

No. Concentration is an intensive property: a ratio, not a quantity. Half the solution contains half the moles in half the volume, and the ratio is the same. This distinguishes it from the number of moles, which is actually the middle.

Why mention molality at all if molarity is easier?

Because volume expands with heat, but mass does not expand. A solution whose molarity is 1.00 M at 20 °C decreases in molarity at 80 °C without anything coming out of it. As for molality, it remains the same, which is why it is used in colligative properties that are measured over a temperature range.

Adapted Educator material selected for this topic.

Make and dilute solutions by amount

Idea. Molarity measures solute amount per liter of final solution, not per liter of solvent. When a solution is diluted without losing solute, its moles remain constant while concentration and total volume change.

Decision. Convert final volume to liters for molarity. Use M1V1=M2V2 only when the same dissolved solute amount is retained; chemical reaction or mixing with a reacting solute needs a new mole balance.

Worked case. Diluting 25.0 mL of 2.00 M NaCl to a final volume of 100.0 mL gives 0.500 M NaCl.

Try this before revealing the answer

How much 1.50 M stock is needed to prepare 250 mL of 0.300 M solution?

Reveal answer and reasoning

V1=(0.300 M)(250 mL)/(1.50 M)=50.0 mL; add solvent to a final volume of 250 mL.

Misconception check. Do not add “250 mL of water” to 50 mL stock and assume the final solution volume is exactly 250 mL.

Connect solubility and particle count

Idea. Solubility depends on solute–solvent interactions and conditions. Ideal colligative changes depend mainly on the number of dissolved particles, not their identity. The van ’t Hoff factor i approximates the number of effective dissolved particles per formula unit.

Decision. Distinguish molality m, moles solute per kilogram solvent, from molarity M. For an ideal dilute electrolyte, estimate i from dissociation, then note that real ion pairing can reduce the effective value. Gas solubility at fixed temperature often rises with its partial pressure under Henry’s law.

Worked case. At the same low molality, ideal NaCl with i≈2 lowers freezing temperature about twice as much as a non-electrolyte with i≈1, if both obey the dilute model.

Try this before revealing the answer

At equal molality in the ideal dilute model, which has greater freezing-point depression: glucose or CaCl2?

Reveal answer and reasoning

CaCl2 dissociates ideally into three ions and has i≈3, so its depression is about three times that of glucose with i≈1.

Misconception check. Colligative formulas require the right concentration unit and an appropriate dilute-solution approximation.

Convert solution volume into reaction amount

Question cue. A liquid concentration and volume appear in the question.

Meaning. Molarity is solute moles per liter of solution. Dilution preserves solute moles when no reaction or loss occurs. Mixing with a reacting solute requires stoichiometry after calculating each amount.

First move. Convert mL to L, calculate n=MV, and apply the reaction ratio if there is one. For a pure dilution, set starting and final solute moles equal.

Worked case. 20.0 mL of 0.100 M HCl contains 0.00200 mol HCl. It requires 10.0 mL of 0.200 M NaOH for 1:1 neutralization.

Try this before revealing the answer

What volume of 0.500 M stock contains 0.100 mol solute?

Reveal answer and reasoning

V=n/M=0.100/0.500=0.200 L, or 200 mL.

If stuck. Molarity uses liters of complete solution, not kilograms of solvent.

Choose a concentration scale

Question cue. The problem specifies solution volume, solvent mass, or a mole fraction.

Meaning. Molarity depends on final solution volume and therefore temperature. Molality uses kilograms of solvent; mole fraction is moles of one component divided by all component moles.

First move. Circle the denominator named in the question. Convert mass to moles and volume to liters only where the chosen scale requires them.

Worked case. Dissolving 0.50 mol solute in 2.0 kg solvent gives 0.25 mol/kg molality. Final solution volume was not supplied, so molarity cannot yet be calculated.

Try this before revealing the answer

A solution has 0.20 mol solute and 0.80 mol solvent. What is solute mole fraction?

Reveal answer and reasoning

0.20/(0.20+0.80)=0.20.

If stuck. Molality uses solvent mass; using the mass of the entire solution changes the result.

Count dissolved particles in a colligative effect

Question cue. Freezing or boiling point shifts after adding a solute.

Meaning. Ideal colligative changes depend on the concentration of dissolved particles. An electrolyte may produce several ions per formula unit, summarized approximately by the van ’t Hoff factor i.

First move. Determine the solvent constant, molality, and realistic particle factor. Compare the result with the pure-solvent transition temperature in the correct direction.

Worked case. For an ideal nonelectrolyte at 1.0 m in water, freezing-point depression is about 1.86 °C, so the solution freezes near −1.86 °C.

Try this before revealing the answer

Which ideally makes a larger freezing-point depression at equal molality: glucose or NaCl?

Reveal answer and reasoning

NaCl, because it ideally produces about two dissolved ions per formula unit, whereas glucose remains one molecule.

If stuck. Real electrolytes may have i below the simple ion count because of ion pairing and nonideal behavior.

Reference and worked examples

Adapted Book material selected for this topic.

Use molarity and dilution

Principle. Molarity is solute moles per liter of solution. Dilution changes volume and concentration while conserving solute moles if no reaction occurs.

Decision. Use C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ only for dilution of the same solute.

Worked case. 25.0 mL of 2.00 M stock diluted to 100.0 mL becomes 0.500 M.

Check. Solution volume is not necessarily solvent volume.

Connect forces to properties

Principle. Dispersion exists for all particles; dipole interactions require permanent molecular dipoles; hydrogen bonding needs H attached to N, O, or F and a suitable acceptor.

Decision. For comparable size and shape, stronger attractions usually raise boiling point and lower vapor pressure.

Worked case. Water hydrogen bonds; methane has only dispersion forces.

Check. A polar bond does not automatically make an entire molecule polar.

Practice

Question types

Each style examines something different. These selected patterns cover the lesson objectives, It is not all that can be asked about the topic.

Type I · Molarity of mass

Convert mass to moles before dividing by volume.

5.85 g was dissolved from NaCl and sized to 0.500 L. What is the molarity of the solution? Molar mass 58.44 g/mol.

n = 5.85 / 58.44 = 0.100 mol, then M = 0.100 / 0.500 = 0.200 mol/L.

Type 2 · Concentration after dilution

Apply solute conservation in the correct direction.

25.0 mL was diluted from 2.00 M solution to a final volume of 100.0 mL. What is the final concentration?

‎C₂ = C₁V₁ / V₂ = (2.00 × 25.0) / 100.0 = 0.500 mol/L‎. The volume has become four times its volume and the concentration has become a quarter.

The third type: moles in a known volume

Convert milliliters to liters before multiplying.

How many moles of solute are in 250 mL than 0.400 M solution?

250 mL = 0.250 L, n = 0.400 × 0.250 = 0.100 mol.

Type IV: Mass ratio

Divide by the mass of the solution, not the mass of the solvent.

20.0 g of a substance is dissolved in 180.0 g of water. What is the mass percentage of solute? Write the number without the ratio sign.

Solution mass 20.0 + 180.0 = 200.0 g, The ratio is (20.0 / 200.0) × 100 = 10.0%.

Fifth type: Volume of concentrated solution

A solution to the unknown on the other side of the relationship.

How many milliliters of 6.00 M solution are needed to prepare 500.0 mL from 0.300 M solution?

‎V₁ = C₂V₂ / C₁ = (0.300 × 500.0) / 6.00 = 150 / 6.00 = 25.0 mL‎. The volume is then supplemented with water to 500.0 mL.
