CHEM 101 · TOPIC 05

Reactions in solution

Use balanced amounts and identify reacting species.

Book →Pearson →Educator →

The idea

Balance atoms and charge before applying mole ratios. Strong soluble electrolytes can be represented as ions; spectator ions cancel in a net ionic equation. Available amounts determine the limiting reactant.

A first example

For Ag⁺+Cl⁻→AgCl(s), 0.10 mol Ag⁺ needs 0.10 mol Cl⁻. With only 0.06 mol Cl⁻, at most 0.06 mol precipitate can form.

Engineering connection. Reactant amounts set the maximum material conversion, even when the equation is correctly balanced.

What this explanation develops

  • Write molecular and net ionic equations while conserving atoms and charge.
  • It predicts precipitation and neutralization and identifies spectator ions.
  • The limiting reactant and mass of the precipitate are calculated from the concentration and volume.
  • Identify oxidation and reduction and balance electron transfer in a simple example.

Definitions & meaning

When solutions of AgNO3 and NaCl are mixed, Ag⁺ and Cl⁻ change to form AgCl(s), while Na⁺ and NO3⁻ remain in the solution. The molecular equation writes the names of the compounds, but the net equation focuses on the change. Do not delete an ion just because both sides have the same element; The chemical type must be the same.

Conservation of charge is a condition independent of conservation of atoms. In Ag⁺+Cl⁻→AgCl the total charge is zero on both ends. In oxidation and reduction reactions, we use half-reactions and balance the lost and gained electrons before adding. No electrons are left in the overall equation when you have combined two complete half-reactions.

Before calculating the precipitate, write the equation, then calculate the moles of each reactant in consistent units. Use the stoichiometric ratio, not the volumetric ratio, unless the concentrations and parameters allow this. We convert moles of precipitate to mass in the last step; The molar mass belongs to the precipitate, not the salt we started with.

In the titration of a strong acid with a strong base, the equivalence point is when the stoichiometric equivalents are equal, not necessarily when the volumes are equal. If OH⁻ or H⁺ remains after the reaction, we calculate its excess moles and then divide by the total volume of the solution assuming the volumes add up in the simplified problem. The endpoint of the evidence is an empirical approximation of the equivalence point and is not a single definition.

Electrolyte
A solute that produces mobile ions in water, allowing conduction. The strong ones dissociate or ionize to a greater extent upon dissolution, and the partially weak, non-electrolytes remain mostly neutral molecules. The word strong describes the extent of ionization, not the concentration of the solution.
Net ionic equation
Describes the species that actually change after deleting the ions that appear with the same condition, formula, and parameter on both ends. We dissociate the strong electrolytes dissolved in the ionic equation, leaving the precipitate, gas, water, and weak acids as complete molecules or formulas.
Precipitation
A slightly soluble solid phase is formed when solutions containing appropriate ions are mixed. Many nitrates, alkali metal salts, and ammonium are dissolved. Many chlorides are soluble, with exceptions such as AgCl. Many carbonates are poorly soluble except for alkali and ammonium salts.
Acid–base neutralization
Proton transfer reaction. In a strong acid and a strong base, the net equation is H⁺+OH⁻→H2O. In a weak acid, such as CH3COOH, we keep the acid undissociated on the reacting side. : CH3COOH+OH⁻→CH3COO⁻+H2O.
Limiting reactant
The reactant that allows the smallest number of times for the reaction to occur according to its parameters. We compare the n/ coefficient for each reactant. The smaller one determines the theoretical amount of product, and another reactant may remain after the reaction has finished. Less is not necessarily the determinant.
Oxidation and reduction
Oxidation loses electrons and increases the oxidation number, and reduction gains them and decreases them. The oxidizing agent is reduced, and the reducing agent is oxidized. The oxidation number is a calculation that helps keep track of electrons and does not always equal an actual charge on the atom.

Eliminate spectators while conserving charge

Start with dissolved compounds and precipitate.

  1. AgNO3(aq)+NaCl(aq)→AgCl(s)+NaNO3(aq)

    Balanced molecular equation.

  2. Ag⁺+NO3⁻+Na⁺+Cl⁻→AgCl(s)+Na⁺+NO3⁻

    We only dissolve dissolved salts.

  3. Ag⁺+Cl⁻→AgCl(s)

    We eliminate the two identical types on both sides.

  4. The charge on both ends is zero and the numbers Ag and Cl are conserved

    We review the net equation.

Worked examples

Determine the type of reaction, balance the equation, convert the data to moles, and then examine the limiting reactant and remaining species.

Precipitate mass

Mix 25.0 mL from AgNO3, concentration 0.100 M, with 40.0 mL from NaCl, concentration 0.100 M. M(AgCl)=143.32 g/mol.

  1. n(Ag⁺)=0.0250×0.100=0.00250 mol

    Volume in litres.

  2. n(Cl⁻)=0.0400×0.100=0.00400 mol

    Comparison molar 1:1.

  3. Ag⁺ specified; n(AgCl)=0.00250 mol

    Ag⁺ is not enough to consume all the chloride.

  4. m=0.00250×143.32=0.3583 g

    We turn the product into a mass.

Result: 0.3583 g

Titration

How much mL of NaOH at 0.200 M concentration is equivalent to 20.0 mL of HCl at 0.100 M concentration?

  1. n(H⁺)=0.0200×0.100=0.00200 mol

    We calculate the moles of acid.

  2. n(OH⁻)=0.00200 mol

    Equalizer 1:1.

  3. V=0.00200/0.200=0.0100 L=10.0 mL

    The base is twice the concentration, so you need half the volume.

Result: 10.0 mL

Details that matter

Neutralization, oxidation and reduction, or gas formation may occur. The absence of a precipitate negates only one sign, and does not prove the absence of every chemical change.

Useful background

Mole and concentration

The number of moles is n=mass/mole mass, and in a solution n=M×V if V in liters is. 25.0 mL than 0.200 mol/L contains 0.00500 mol, because 25.0 mL=0.0250 L. the coefficients of the balanced equation give the mole ratios.
