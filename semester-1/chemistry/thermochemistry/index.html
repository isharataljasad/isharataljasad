Prerequisites

  • Malls calculation

    You need it because: Enthalpy is often given per mole, as the relationship between mass and heat goes through moles.

    n = m / M. If ΔH = −890 kJ/mol for methane combustion, Burning 0.50 mol releases 445 kJ. Pay attention to “per mole”: it is part of the unit, not an addition to the phrase.

    Convert mass to moles

  • Order of subtraction in temperature difference

    You need it because: The sign ΔT determines the sign q, so reversing the subtraction changes the meaning of the entire result.

    ΔT = T_final − T_initial, always final first. An increase from 20 °C to 35 °C gives ΔT = +15, and a decrease from 35 to 20 gives −15. Note that the temperature difference is the same in Celsius and Kelvin, so there is no need for conversion here.

THE IDEA

Understand the decision

For a temperature change with no phase transition, q=mcΔT. A system receiving heat has positive q. In an ideal insulated calorimetry balance, heat lost by one part equals heat gained by the other. Reversing a reaction reverses its enthalpy sign.

Worked example

Heating 100 g of a substance with c=4 J/(g·°C) by 5°C requires q=100·4·5=2000 J.

Engineering connection Energy balances begin by specifying which system a sign describes; heat and temperature are different quantities.

Approach 1 · Reference

Definitions and relationships

The first law ΔU=q+w uses the convention that the heat entering a system and the work done on it are positive. In expansion against constant external pressure w=−PextΔV; The system does work and loses energy. When the volume is constant and no work other than expansion is done, it is qv=ΔU, and when the pressure is constant with only expansion working, qp=ΔH. These are two conditional equations and are not a definition of every heat.

Because the enthalpy is a state function, we combine the equations to get the target equation and combine ΔH with it: This is Hess's law. Reversing the reaction reverses the sign of ΔH, and multiplying its coefficients multiplies ΔH. Using standard enthalpies of formation, we calculate the sum of the coefficients of the products multiplied by ΔHf° minus the sum of the reactants. For the element in its standard reference state ΔHf°=0, not for each atom or image of the element.

Breaking a bond requires energy and forming it releases energy. The estimate of ΔH from the bond energies is equal to the sum of the energies of the bonds broken minus the ones formed; Average gas-phase bond energies are an approximation and are not an exact proxy for composition data. In the calorimeter the measuring vessel itself is sometimes included in the : qreaction=−(msolution c ΔT+Ccal ΔT) budget, depending on the system limits and no heat loss.

System and surroundings
The system is the part selected for study; the surroundings are everything outside it. In a reaction calorimetry model, one may choose the reacting chemicals as the system and the water and vessel as surroundings. Always state this choice before assigning heat signs.
Heat (q)
Energy transferred due to a temperature difference, in joules. It is not a property that the body possesses, but rather a transfer that occurs; It is not said “the heat of the body” in the sense of what it contains, but rather the heat transferred to or from it.
Exothermic
An exothermic process transfers heat from the chosen system to its surroundings. At constant pressure under the usual calorimetry assumptions, ΔH<0. The surroundings may warm while the system loses energy.
Endothermic
An endothermic process absorbs heat into the chosen system from its surroundings. At constant pressure under the usual calorimetry assumptions, ΔH>0. The surroundings may cool, for example during some salt-dissolution processes.
Enthalpy change (ΔH)
Enthalpy H=U+pV is a state function. Its change equals heat transferred at constant pressure when only pressure–volume work occurs. Because ΔH depends on the initial and final states, reaction enthalpies can be combined using Hess’s law.
Specific heat capacity (c)
Specific heat capacity relates heat to mass and temperature change: q=mcΔT when c is approximately constant and there is no phase change. Water has c≈4.18 J/(g·°C), so substantial heat can produce a comparatively small temperature change.

Why is ΔH negative in the reaction that heats water?

It seems contradictory: the temperature has risen and the sign is negative. The contradiction disappears once we determine who the regime is.

  1. Identify the system: it is the reactants, not water or the beaker.Why: Water is a measuring instrument, not a subject of study. Every sign is read from the perspective of the system alone.
  2. The reaction released energy from it into the water, so the system **lost** energy.Why: Loss is a decrease, and the decrease is written with a negative sign; The sign describes direction, not quantity.
  3. Write : q_system = −q_water., if the water absorbed by +2000 J is q_system = −2000 J.Why: Energy cannot be destroyed: what the water gains is exactly what the reaction loses, and the two signs are opposite.
  4. The increase in water temperature is evidence that ΔH is negative, not positive.Why: This is where the common mistake lies: water is viewed and its condition is attributed to the system. The practical rule: heat the surroundings, the reaction is repulsive and its sign is negative.

Approach 3 · Step-by-step teaching

From zero to solution

First write: “The system is…”. Then calculate q for water as q = mcΔT with ΔT arranged as final and then as initial. Then invert the sign to get q for the system. If ΔH is required per mole, divide by the number of moles reacting in the experiment. In Hessian problems: arrange the equations so that the intermediates are eliminated, and invert the sign of each equation you reverse.

Example 1 · The temperature of heating water

How much heat is needed to raise the temperature of 250 g of water from 20.0 °C to 45.0 °C? Take c = 4.18 J/(g·°C).

  1. Calculate ΔT = 45.0 − 20.0 = 25.0 °C.Why: The difference is not the final value. If you substituted 45.0, you would calculate heating from absolute zero Celsius, and this does not make sense.
  2. Replace : q = 250 × 4.18 × 25.0.Why: The units are abbreviated : g × J/(g·°C) × °C, leaving only the joule.
  3. The output is 26125 J ≈ 26.1 kJ, and its sign is positive because water has been gained.Why: Water here is what we are studying, so the gain is positive. If the water were surrounding, it would react, and we would invert the sign when moving to the system.

Output: q = 26125 J ≈ 26.1 kJ

Example 2 · from calorimeter measurement to reaction sign

A reaction was carried out in 100.0 g of water and its temperature increased 5.00 °C. What does q interact with? Take c = 4.18 J/(g·°C).

  1. The system is the interaction, and the surroundings is the water.Why: Identifying the system first is what prevents sign error later.
  2. Calculate the temperature of the water: q_water = 100.0 × 4.18 × 5.00 = +2090 J.Why: Positive because the water gained heat and its temperature rose.
  3. Invert the sign to the system : q_reaction = −2090 J.Why: What the water gained is what the reaction lost. The reaction is exothermic, although what you observed was a rise in temperature.

Output: q_reaction = −2090 J (exothermic)

Collection of HS steps

A→B: ΔH=+20 kJ, B→C: ΔH=−50 kJ. Calculate A→C.

  1. We add the two equationsWhy: B is a product then a reactant and is abbreviated.
  2. We collect 20+(−50)=−30 kJWhy: The enthalpy depends on the two sides, not the transfer path.
  3. For the reaction C→A it is +30 kJWhy: Reversing the trend reverses the sign.

Output: -30 kJ

Questions often left unexplained

Why don't we convert the temperature to Kelvin here as we did for gases?

Because q = mcΔT uses a **difference** not an absolute value. The degree length is the same in Celsius and Kelvin, so the difference is the same in them. Gas laws use ratios between absolute values, and the ratio changes with the change of the zero point.

How can ΔH be a state function even though the temperature depends on the path?

The temperature in general really depends on the track. But **when the pressure is constant** the heat transferred is equal The change in enthalpy, which is a function of state. The constraint “pressure constant” is what makes ΔH independent of the path, and thus Hess’s law holds true.

Is an exothermic reaction always spontaneous?

No. Many extruders are automatic, but the decisive factor is not the ΔH alone, but the Gibbs free energy. It combines enthalpy with entropy and heat. The dissolution of some salts is endothermic and occurs spontaneously. Detailing that is beyond the scope of this lesson.

Another explanation

Adapted Educator material selected for this topic.

Separate heat from temperature

Idea. Temperature measures thermal state, while heat is energy transferred because of temperature difference. In a simple insulated calorimeter, energy lost by one part is gained by another. Sign convention depends on which part is called the system.

Decision. Choose system and surroundings. Use the measured temperature change and specific heat for the calibrated material or calorimeter. An exothermic reaction releases heat to surroundings; the solution can warm while reaction q is negative.

Worked case. If 100 g of solution with c=4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ warms by 5.0 K, it gains about +2.09 kJ. If heat loss is negligible, the reaction released about −2.09 kJ.

Try this before revealing the answer

A 50 g water sample cools by 4 K with c=4.2 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. What is q for the water?

Reveal answer and reasoning

qwater=50(4.2)(−4)=−840 J. The water lost 840 J of thermal energy.

Misconception check. A positive temperature rise of the solution corresponds to positive q for the solution, often negative q for an exothermic reaction.

Use enthalpy and Hess law with signs

Idea. At constant pressure with only pressure-volume work, reaction enthalpy tracks heat transferred by the reacting system. Enthalpy is a state function, so reaction steps add even when the physical pathway differs.

Decision. Write the target balanced equation. Reverse a given reaction to reverse ΔH; multiply a reaction to multiply ΔH. For formation enthalpies, use stoichiometric coefficients and subtract reactant total from product total.

Worked case. If A→B has ΔH=+10 kJ and B→C has ΔH=−25 kJ, then A→C has ΔH=−15 kJ. The result depends only on initial and final states.

Try this before revealing the answer

A reaction X→Y has ΔH=−40 kJ. What are ΔH for Y→X and for 2 X→2 Y?

Reveal answer and reasoning

Reversal gives +40 kJ; doubling the reaction gives −80 kJ for the doubled equation.

Misconception check. Reaction enthalpy belongs to the equation as written, including its coefficients and states. Bond energies give estimates, not exact formation-enthalpy results.

More decision practice

Adapted Pearson material selected for this topic.

Use temperature change to find exchanged heat

Question cue. A substance or calorimeter warms or cools.

Meaning. Heat flows because of a temperature difference. A sample with positive ΔT absorbs heat; a reaction that warms its surroundings releases heat to them.

First move. Identify the system and surroundings, use mass and specific heat for the measured body, then invert the sign for the reacting system when assuming negligible losses.

Worked case. 100 g water warming by 5.0 °C absorbs about 100×4.184×5.0=2092 J. The reaction that caused it releases approximately 2.09 kJ under the simple calorimeter model.

Try this before revealing the answer

50 g water cools by 2.0 °C. Is q for that water positive or negative?

Reveal answer and reasoning

Negative: q=50×4.184×(−2.0)≈−418 J. It released heat.

If stuck. A temperature change is not itself a heat quantity; heat also depends on mass and heat capacity.

Reverse and add thermochemical steps

Question cue. A target reaction is assembled from known reactions.

Meaning. Enthalpy is a state function. Reverse a reaction and its ΔH sign changes; multiply coefficients and ΔH scales by the same factor.

First move. Write the target equation. Reverse, scale, and add supplied equations so unwanted species cancel. Add the adjusted enthalpy changes only after the chemical equations match.

Worked case. If A→B has ΔH=−40 kJ and B→C has ΔH=+15 kJ, then A→C has ΔH=−25 kJ.

Try this before revealing the answer

If 2A→2B has ΔH=+60 kJ, what is ΔH for B→A?

Reveal answer and reasoning

Divide by two to obtain A→B at +30 kJ, then reverse: B→A is −30 kJ.

If stuck. Adding enthalpies without first aligning full equations can hide wrong coefficients.

Practice

Question types

Each style examines something different. These selected patterns cover the lesson objectives, It is not all that can be asked about the topic.

Mode 1 · Heat from mcΔT

The difference is used, not the final value.

How many joules would it take to raise the temperature of 200.0 g of water from 25.0 °C to 75.0 °C? Take c = 4.18 J/(g·°C).

ΔT = 75.0 − 25.0 = 50.0 °C, q = 200.0 × 4.18 × 50.0 = 41800 J.

Type 2 · Enthalpy sign

The sign is read from the perspective of the system, not the environment.

Reaction releases 50.0 kJ per mole. What is ΔH and kJ/mol module? Write the number with a sign.

Release means the system has lost power, so the sign is negative : ΔH = −50.0 kJ/mol.

The third type is the arrangement of temperature differences

Subtract the final minus the primary in this order.

The temperature of solution decreased from 24.0 °C to 18.5 °C. What does ΔT indicate?

‎ΔT = T_final − T_initial = 18.5 − 24.0 = −5.5 °C‎. A negative sign means that the solution has lost heat, so the process is absorbent from the perspective of what is dissolved in it.

The fourth type is summation by Hess's law

It is combined using signs and not abstract quantities.

A reaction that takes place in two steps: the first is ΔH₁ = −200 kJ and the second is ΔH₂ = +80 kJ. What is the total ΔH with kJ unit? Write the number with a sign.

‎ΔH = ΔH₁ + ΔH₂ = −200 + 80 = −120 kJ‎. The heat content is a state function, so the sum of the steps is the total change, regardless of the path.

Fifth type: Finding the specific capacity

c is isolated in the right denominator.

A sample with mass 50.0 g absorbed an amount of 500 J and its temperature increased 4.80 °C. What is its specific heat capacity?

‎c = q / (m ΔT) = 500 / (50.0 × 4.80) = 500 / 240 = 2.08 J/(g·°C)‎.
