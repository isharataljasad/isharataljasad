CHEM 101 · TOPIC 04

Chemical bonding

Account for electrons before assigning shape and polarity.

LearnCheckApply
THE IDEA

Understand the decision

Count valence electrons, connect atoms, distribute electrons and check formal charges. Electron-domain geometry and molecular shape are different when lone pairs are present. Molecular polarity depends on both bond polarity and shape.

Worked example

CO₂ has 4+2(6)=16 valence electrons. Two double bonds give a linear arrangement; equal opposite bond dipoles cancel.

Engineering connection Bonding and molecular shape help explain properties used in separation and material selection.

Distinguish ionic and covalent bonding

Idea. An ionic solid is stabilized by an extended network of electrostatic attractions. A covalent bond shares electron density between nuclei. Bond polarity reflects unequal sharing, while molecular polarity depends on both bond dipoles and geometry.

Decision. Identify the particles and structure first. For ionic compounds, balance formula-unit charge and consider lattice behavior; for molecules, draw connectivity and electron pairs. Do not treat a formula unit as an isolated ionic molecule.

Worked case. NaCl is represented by a 1:1 formula unit in an ionic lattice. HCl is a molecular covalent compound with a polar bond. Water has polar O–H bonds and a bent geometry, so its dipoles do not cancel.

Try this before revealing the answer

Why does CO2 have polar C=O bonds but no permanent molecular dipole?

Reveal answer and reasoning

CO2 is linear. The two equal bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel as vectors.

Misconception check. Electronegativity difference alone cannot decide the total polarity of a molecule; geometry matters.

Draw Lewis structures and formal charges

Idea. A Lewis structure allocates valence electrons as bonding pairs and lone pairs. Formal charge is a bookkeeping charge in a chosen electron assignment, not a measured atomic charge.

Decision. Sum valence electrons and adjust for overall charge. Sketch a reasonable skeleton, join atoms with single bonds, complete terminal octets where applicable, place remaining electrons on the center, and create multiple bonds if needed. Compare formal charges and plausible electronegativity placement.

Worked case. For CO2, 16 valence electrons give O=C=O with two lone pairs on each oxygen. Each atom has formal charge zero.

Try this before revealing the answer

How many valence electrons does NH4+ contain, and what is the formal charge on N when it has four N–H bonds and no lone pair?

Reveal answer and reasoning

5+4(1)−1=8 electrons. Nitrogen has FC=5−0−4=+1; each H has zero.

Misconception check. Do not force hydrogen to have an octet. Some central atoms have incomplete or expanded octets, so check the element and electron count.

Use resonance without moving nuclei

Idea. Resonance structures are alternative Lewis electron placements for the same nuclear arrangement. The real electron distribution is not rapidly flipping between drawings; a resonance hybrid can have delocalized bonding. Equivalent contributors often share the same energy.

Decision. Keep atoms and sigma-bond connectivity fixed while moving permissible electron pairs and pi bonds. Preserve the total electron count and overall charge in every contributor.

Worked case. In NO3−, three equivalent drawings put the N=O bond at different oxygen atoms. The measured N–O bonds are equivalent in the resonance hybrid, with average bond order between one and two.

Try this before revealing the answer

When drawing another resonance contributor for a carbonate ion, may the carbon and oxygen nuclei exchange positions?

Reveal answer and reasoning

No. Only the electron placement changes; the atom connectivity and total ion charge remain the same.

Misconception check. Resonance is not the same as chemical equilibrium between different molecules.

Predict shape and polarity with VSEPR

Idea. Electron domains around a central atom repel and arrange to reduce crowding. A multiple bond counts as one domain for the basic VSEPR arrangement. Molecular shape describes atom positions; electron geometry also counts lone pairs.

Decision. Draw a Lewis structure, count bonding regions and lone pairs on the central atom, then name electron geometry and molecular shape separately. Add bond dipole vectors to test net polarity.

Worked case. H2O has four electron domains around O: two bonds and two lone pairs. Its electron geometry is tetrahedral, but its molecular shape is bent; the O–H dipoles do not cancel.

Try this before revealing the answer

Predict the electron geometry, molecular shape, and polarity of NH3.

Reveal answer and reasoning

N has three bonds and one lone pair: tetrahedral electron geometry, trigonal-pyramidal molecular shape, and a net molecular dipole.

Misconception check. The word “tetrahedral” for electron geometry does not mean four atoms surround the center.

Build a Lewis structure that accounts for charge

Question cue. Connectivity, lone pairs, or formal charge are requested.

Meaning. A Lewis structure tracks valence electrons in bonds and lone pairs. Formal charge is a bookkeeping tool for comparing plausible structures, not a measured ionic charge at every atom.

First move. Count total valence electrons including ion charge. Connect atoms, fill outer octets when applicable, place remaining electrons centrally, and use multiple bonds if needed. Calculate formal charges and check total.

Worked case. For CO₂, O=C=O uses 16 valence electrons, gives octets, and has zero formal charge on all atoms.

Try this before revealing the answer

How many valence electrons does NO₃⁻ have before drawing bonds?

Reveal answer and reasoning

N contributes 5, three O contribute 18, and the −1 charge adds 1: total 24 electrons.

If stuck. An octet alone does not prove the best structure; check formal charge, atom placement, and valid exceptions.

Turn electron domains into molecular shape

Question cue. A Lewis structure must predict a three-dimensional shape.

Meaning. VSEPR uses the number of electron domains around the central atom. Single, double, and triple bonds each count as one domain; lone pairs affect the molecular shape and angle.

First move. Draw Lewis first, count bonding and lone-pair domains, name the electron geometry, then describe the positions of atoms alone. Use symmetry to test whether bond dipoles cancel.

Worked case. H₂O has four electron domains around O, two bonding and two lone pairs. Its electron geometry is tetrahedral, molecular shape is bent, and it is polar.

Try this before revealing the answer

What is the molecular shape of NH₃ and why is it polar?

Reveal answer and reasoning

Four domains with one lone pair give a trigonal-pyramidal shape. Its N–H bond dipoles do not cancel.

If stuck. Electron geometry and molecular geometry differ whenever a central lone pair replaces an atom.

Reference and worked examples

Adapted Book material selected for this topic.

Draw Lewis structures

Principle. Count valence electrons (adjust for ion charge), connect atoms, complete outer octets, then form multiple bonds when needed. Minimize unreasonable formal charges.

Decision. For each atom, formal charge = valence electrons − nonbonding electrons − half the bonding electrons.

Worked case. CO₂ has O=C=O and zero formal charges.

Check. Resonance represents a delocalized structure, not atoms rapidly switching bonds.

Use electron regions to find shape

Principle. VSEPR first counts bonding regions and lone pairs around the central atom. Electron-region geometry includes both; molecular shape names positions of atoms only.

Decision. A double bond counts as one region; lone pairs often compress bond angles.

Worked case. CH₄: tetrahedral; NH₃: trigonal pyramidal; H₂O: bent.

Check. Polar bonds can cancel in a symmetric molecule such as CO₂.
