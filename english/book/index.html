Academic IELTS Book Learning Route

Academic IELTS skills, original practice, two complete writing models, and a rapid review index.

Learning loop Understand the task → attempt → check evidence → correct the error

Based on The Official Cambridge Guide to IELTS by Pauline Cullen, Amanda French, and Vanessa Jakeman. Each route is studied independently.

Start studying →

A self-contained revision and practice course built from the Academic skills sequence in The Official Cambridge Guide to IELTS by Pauline Cullen, Amanda French, and Vanessa Jakeman. It teaches how to choose evidence, handle each task type, write clear responses, and correct recurring errors. The explanations, examples, and exercises here are newly written. This file is the book track only.

SECTION 01

How to use this course

Learn Read one principle, then say the decision rule aloud without looking. A strategy is learned only when it changes a new answer.

Attempt Cover every Check line before trying the task. For a full passage or essay, work under time pressure only after an untimed accurate attempt.

Correct For each error, record the question type, the evidence you used, the evidence you missed, and the rule that would have prevented the error. Redo with a changed example.

Measure Use an official full-length practice test and its answer key for a real baseline. The mini tasks in this booklet teach mechanisms; they cannot predict a band score.

The attached guide is a 2014 edition. Its Academic skill instruction remains useful, but administrative details about answer sheets and test delivery can change. Confirm local test-day instructions with IELTS before booking. Authentic Listening practice requires the corresponding recordings; the original script later in this file is a teaching drill, not a substitute for a scored recording.

SECTION 02

Academic test checkpoint

Listening Four parts and 40 questions; hear each recording once. Read the exact answer limit and copy names, numbers, and plurals accurately.

Reading Three Academic passages and 40 questions in 60 minutes. Every answer must be supported by the text, not outside knowledge.

Writing Two tasks in 60 minutes. Task 1 requires at least 150 words; Task 2 at least 250 words and has twice the weight. Reserve time to inspect the prompt and edit the answer.

Speaking Three parts over about 11 to 14 minutes. Part 2 includes preparation time and a longer turn. Answer the examiner, develop a relevant idea, and speak intelligibly.

SECTION 03

Listening

Listening questions test an evolving conversation or talk. Preview the task, anticipate the answer form, follow meaning rather than isolated words, and verify the final response after corrections or contrasts.

Context spelling and numbers

Principle A heading and the nearby question tell you the kind of information to expect: a name, a date, a price, or a noun phrase.

Use Before the audio begins, mark units and answer limits. During the audio, write an abbreviated answer; immediately after, check spelling, singular or plural, and whether the number includes a unit.

Worked example Form: 'Fee per term: £____'. If the speaker says 'seventy-five pounds', enter 75 if the symbol is already printed.

Try Form: 'Contact: Dr ____'. You hear 'My surname is Vale, V-A-L-E.' What belongs in the blank?

Reveal check

Vale. Write the word requested; do not add the title already printed.

Common trap A correct sound with wrong spelling or extra words can lose the mark.

Speakers functions and final decisions

Principle In a conversation, the first option mentioned may be rejected. A question may ask for a decision, a reason, or a speaker's role.

Use Track who proposed each option and listen for changed plans: 'but', 'actually', 'instead', and 'let us'. Answer the question's target, not the first matching word.

Worked example 'We could meet Tuesday. I have a lab then. Wednesday at three works.' The agreed meeting is Wednesday at three.

Try A student says, 'The library is open at nine, but the tutor cannot meet until ten.' Asked for the appointment time, what do you write?

Reveal check

Ten o'clock. Nine is contextual information, not the appointment.

Common trap Treating a mentioned option as a final answer before the speakers agree.

Paraphrase and distractors

Principle The spoken answer often rephrases the printed question. A distractor uses relevant vocabulary but fails the meaning test.

Use Underline the meaning to locate. For multiple choice, eliminate options contradicted by the whole exchange.

Worked example Question: 'What caused the delay?' Audio: 'The equipment arrived on time; the permit was issued late.' Correct idea: a late permit.

Try Options: A faulty equipment, B delayed permit, C staff absence. Which is supported by that audio?

Reveal check

B. Arrival on time rules out A; C was not stated.

Common trap Matching an option because its noun appears, without checking whether it is denied.

Maps processes and lectures

Principle Maps test an ordered route; process questions test sequence; lectures test ideas linked by signposts.

Use On maps, fix your starting point and direction. On a process, listen for 'first', 'after', 'before', and 'finally'. In a lecture, note the claim, support, exception, and conclusion.

Worked example 'From the entrance, walk past the lab and turn left at the stairs; the office is the first door on your right.' The office is after the left turn, not beside the entrance.

Try A lecturer says a method is cheap and fast but produces unreliable readings. What is the main limitation?

Reveal check

Unreliable readings. The advantages are context; 'but' introduces the limitation.

Common trap Losing the current place after missing one answer. Move to the next printed item and recover at the next signpost.

SECTION 04

Academic Reading

Start with the task and the passage structure. Skim to locate the relevant part, then read the evidence closely. Scan for synonyms and relationships, not only exact repeated words.

Skimming and passage structure

Principle Skimming gives a map of purpose: introduction, method, evidence, counterclaim, or result. It is not a speed substitute for careful answer checking.

Use Read title, opening and closing sentences, and visible transitions. Give each paragraph a five-word purpose label; then return to the question.

Worked example Paragraph: 'Early plans focused on cost. Later studies measured repair time and reliability.' Main shift: from cost to performance evidence.

Try Which heading is best: A A complete history of machines, B A change in evaluation criteria, C A list of suppliers?

Reveal check

B. It covers both sentences. A and C are broader or absent.

Common trap Choosing a heading from one eye-catching word rather than the paragraph's overall point.

Detail and completion

Principle A completion question may paraphrase the passage but usually requires an exact word or phrase from it and a strict word limit.

Use Predict the grammar of the blank; find the matching meaning; copy only allowed words and preserve the correct form.

Worked example Text: 'The panels were stored in a dry chamber before testing.' Note: 'Storage location: a ____.' With ONE WORD ONLY, write 'chamber'.

Try Text: 'A second sensor records humidity.' Complete 'The second sensor measures ____' with ONE WORD ONLY.

Reveal check

Humidity. The meaning of 'records' matches 'measures' here.

Common trap Copying too many adjacent words or substituting a plausible word not present in the passage.

True False Not Given and Yes No Not Given

Principle True or Yes means the passage supports the statement. False or No means it directly contradicts it. Not Given means the needed claim is absent.

Use Identify the exact proposition, including quantity and qualification. Find evidence. Do not infer an unstated cause or universal rule. Use Yes/No/Not Given for a writer's views or claims; True/False/Not Given for factual information.

Worked example Text: 'Only some volunteers preferred the new interface.' Statement: 'Every volunteer preferred it.' False: 'only some' contradicts 'every'.

Try Text: 'The trial enrolled 80 volunteers.' Statement: 'Most volunteers were under 30.' Choose True, False, or Not Given.

Reveal check

Not Given. The passage supplies enrollment, not age distribution.

Common trap Marking an unsupported statement False merely because it seems unlikely.

Matching information and features

Principle Matching is a location problem. One paragraph can hold several details; information may appear in a different order from questions.

Use Underline distinctive concepts, scan paragraph functions, then verify the full statement. For people, theories, or inventions, track who actually made each claim.

Worked example If Chen proposes an idea and Malik tests it, a question asking who tested the idea points to Malik, even if Chen is named first.

Try Text: 'Rios designed the filter; Patel compared it with a conventional unit.' Who performed the comparison?

Reveal check

Patel. The verbs distinguish their contributions.

Common trap Matching a nearby name without checking the action linked to it.

Multiple choice writer purpose and opinion

Principle A strong distractor can state a true passage detail while failing the exact question. Purpose asks why a sentence or paragraph appears.

Use Answer mentally before reading the options. Then test each option against the relevant lines, especially degree words such as 'always' and 'only'.

Worked example A paragraph describes a failed trial before a redesigned trial. Its purpose may be to explain why the design changed, not to prove all trials fail.

Try Text: 'The author accepts the cost advantage but doubts that maintenance has been measured adequately.' Does the writer completely reject the device?

Reveal check

No. The view is qualified: one benefit is accepted, while evidence on maintenance is questioned.

Common trap Replacing a qualified argument with an absolute option.

SECTION 05

Academic Writing Task 1

Task 1 reports visual information accurately. Start with what is measured and its units; choose the most important overall pattern; group useful details. Explain only what the visual supplies. The following table and responses are original practice material.

Select an overview before detail

Principle An overview states the dominant pattern, major contrast, or stable feature. It does not list all figures or explain unseen causes.

Use Find the highest and lowest series, the direction of change, any reversal, and the time span. Choose two or three defining features.

Worked example If A rises from 20 to 60 and B stays around 30, say A increases markedly while B is broadly stable.

Try A city library's digital loans rise each year while print loans fall slightly. Give one overview sentence without inventing a cause.

Reveal check

'Overall, digital borrowing increased steadily, whereas print borrowing declined modestly.'

Common trap Saying digital access caused the decline when the data show only concurrent trends.

Compare figures accurately

Principle A comparison must match the measure, unit, and year. Percent and percentage-point changes are not interchangeable.

Use For exact numbers, state start and end or a meaningful gap. For proportions, a change from 20% to 30% is a rise of 10 percentage points, or 50% relative to the starting value.

Worked example 'The share rose from 20% to 30%' is safe; 'rose by 30%' is wrong if you mean a 10-point increase.

Try Station A has 40 visits and B has 60 visits. How much higher is B in visits and relative to A?

Reveal check

20 visits; 50% higher than A. State the basis if you use a percentage.

Common trap Comparing one year's number with another year's percentage or dropping the unit.

Processes and maps

Principle Process diagrams require a start, ordered stages, and an end or cycle. Maps require location and the main change between two times.

Use Describe visible stages with suitable verbs and passive voice when the actor is unimportant. For maps, anchor directions to fixed landmarks and distinguish replacement, addition, and removal.

Worked example 'The raw material is washed, dried, and packed' describes three visible actions without suggesting a chemical change.

Try A map shows a footpath replaced by a cycle lane and a new entrance added to the east. Write a factual summary.

Reveal check

'The footpath was replaced by a cycle lane, and an additional entrance was built on the eastern side.'

Common trap Guessing dates, reasons, traffic effects, or environmental benefits that the visual does not show.

Original Task 1 data practice

The table shows the number of visits, in thousands, to three study facilities in a fictional city. Write a report of at least 150 words. Select an overview and compare key changes.

Facility201820212024
Central library120125130
Digital learning hub3570115
Community study rooms656055

Plan Central library remains the busiest; digital hub grows sharply and nearly catches it; study-room visits decline gently. Group the rising services, then contrast the decline. Use 'thousand visits', not a percentage.

Model response

The table compares visits to three study facilities in a fictional city in 2018, 2021 and 2024. Figures are given in thousands. Overall, the central library remained the most visited facility throughout the period. However, the digital learning hub experienced much stronger growth and came close to the library by 2024. In contrast, use of the community study rooms fell slightly. The central library received 120 thousand visits in 2018. This figure rose gradually to 125 thousand in 2021 and 130 thousand in 2024, an increase of 10 thousand over the full period. Visits to the digital learning hub more than tripled, climbing from 35 thousand to 70 thousand and then to 115 thousand. As a result, the gap between the hub and the library narrowed from 85 thousand visits in 2018 to only 15 thousand in 2024. The community study rooms followed the opposite pattern, declining from 65 thousand visits in 2018 to 60 thousand in 2021 and 55 thousand in 2024.

Inspect The response identifies units, gives an overview, selects key comparisons, and avoids unsupported explanations.

SECTION 06

Academic Writing Task 2

Task 2 is an argument responding to the precise prompt. Build a clear position, support it with reasons and specific examples, acknowledge limits where useful, and edit for language control. A memorised template cannot compensate for missing task response.

Decode the prompt and decide a position

Principle The task words define the answer. 'Discuss both views and give your opinion' needs both views and your own judgement. 'To what extent' needs a degree of agreement.

Use Circle the topic, the claim, and the required operation. Write a one-sentence position before drafting. Choose reasons you can explain with a plausible example.

Worked example Prompt: 'Should universities require internships?' Position: 'A supervised internship is useful for some courses, but a universal requirement would be unfair where placements are scarce.'

Try For 'Some people think public libraries are no longer needed. To what extent do you agree?', write a bounded position.

Reveal check

'I disagree overall: digital resources change how libraries are used, but public access, study space, and guidance remain valuable.'

Common trap Writing about libraries generally without answering whether they are still needed.

Build a paragraph with evidence

Principle A body paragraph needs a claim, a causal explanation, a concrete example, and a link to the question. One relevant developed idea beats several unsupported assertions.

Use Ask: What changes? Why would it change? Under which conditions? What example would make the mechanism visible?

Worked example Claim: 'Supervised practice helps students.' Development: 'Feedback during a real placement exposes errors that a simulated task may miss.'

Try Develop this claim: 'Campus writing centres help students.' Add a mechanism and a specific example.

Reveal check

'A writing centre can show a student how to connect a claim with evidence. For example, feedback on one paragraph can reveal where a quoted result has been asserted rather than explained.'

Common trap Using 'for example' followed by another abstract claim instead of an actual example.

Cohesion vocabulary and correction

Principle Cohesion is the logical connection between ideas, not the number of linking phrases. Precise ordinary words are safer than ornate words used incorrectly.

Use Repeat a key term when clarity requires it. Use 'however' only for a real contrast. In the final check, inspect subject–verb agreement, reference words, articles, and whether every paragraph supports the position.

Worked example Weak: 'This is good. Furthermore, it is good.' Better: 'The programme saves time because students receive feedback while a mistake can still be corrected.'

Try Revise: 'All student needs internships because it always guarantee jobs.'

Reveal check

'Some students benefit from internships, but a placement cannot guarantee employment.' The revision fixes grammar and an unsupported absolute claim.

Common trap Adding stock phrases, invented statistics, or memorised sentences that do not fit the prompt.

Original Task 2 practice

Prompt: Some universities require every student to complete a workplace placement before graduation. Do the advantages of this policy outweigh the disadvantages? Write at least 250 words. Decide what 'every student' changes in the argument.

Plan Position: advantages can be strong when placements are supervised and available, but a universal requirement creates inequity; therefore the disadvantages of a rigid policy outweigh the advantages. Paragraph one explains applied learning. Paragraph two explains unequal access and variable quality. Paragraph three proposes a conditional alternative.

Model response

Workplace placements can help university students connect academic ideas with real tasks. Nevertheless, requiring every student to complete one before graduation is too rigid. In my view, the disadvantages of a universal rule outweigh its benefits unless institutions can provide accessible, supervised placements of comparable quality.

A good placement can strengthen learning. A student may discover that a method that worked neatly in class depends on constraints in a workplace, such as limited time or incomplete information. Supervision also allows the student to receive feedback on communication and judgement. These benefits are most likely when the placement has clear goals and a mentor who can observe the work. Simply spending time in an organisation does not ensure learning.

The universal requirement is problematic because access is uneven. Students who have caring responsibilities or who live far from major employers may have fewer suitable opportunities. Some placements may be unpaid, while others may provide meaningful training and a salary. If graduation depends on securing a placement, the policy could penalise students for circumstances beyond their control. Quality would also vary: one student might analyse a real problem, whereas another might perform repetitive tasks with little feedback.

A better approach is to require evidence of applied learning while allowing several routes. A supervised placement could be one route; a research project, community project, or carefully assessed simulation could be another. Universities would then have to judge what each student learned rather than whether a particular opportunity was available.

For these reasons, placements should be strongly encouraged where their quality can be assured. Making them compulsory for every student, however, risks turning a useful educational experience into an unequal barrier to graduation.

Inspect The answer addresses the full policy, states a consistent judgement, develops both sides, and reaches a conclusion that follows from the reasons. It does not claim that any one example proves a universal result.

SECTION 07

Speaking

Speaking rewards understandable, relevant, sustained communication. The goal is to explain an idea as naturally as possible, not to recite a script or eliminate an accent.

Part 1 familiar questions

Principle Give a direct answer, then one useful reason, example, or contrast. Match the question's time frame.

Use For 'Do you read more now than before?', compare present with past. A short natural extension is enough.

Worked example 'Yes. I now read short research summaries most days, whereas I used to read mainly course notes.'

Try Answer: 'What place do you use for studying, and why?' in two or three sentences.

Reveal check

'I usually study in the library because it is quiet. I can focus for longer there than at home, especially before an exam.'

Common trap An overlong memorised speech that stops answering the examiner's simple question.

Part 2 one minute plan and long turn

Principle Use preparation time to note a route, not to write a speech. The cue points are a structure, and a concrete event gives you material to develop.

Use Note who or what, when and where, what happened, and why it mattered. Speak in connected chunks; if one detail ends, add a consequence or reflection.

Worked example Cue: 'Describe a useful piece of advice.' Notes: tutor; first lab report; separate result from explanation; improved clarity; still useful.

Try Give a 60-second talk about a skill you learned. Use four cue notes and one reflection.

Reveal check

A complete answer names the skill, describes the learning event, explains a difficulty and solution, and closes with why the skill remains useful.

Common trap Listing the cue points as separate fragments without a coherent event or development.

Part 3 abstract discussion

Principle Move from personal experience to a broader pattern while keeping qualifications. Support a claim with a mechanism and an example.

Use Use a compact structure: judgement, reason, condition or counterexample, result. Agree or disagree with the examiner's idea respectfully and directly.

Worked example Question: 'Should all learning be online?' Answer: 'Not entirely. Online lectures widen access, but hands-on tasks need equipment and immediate feedback.'

Try Answer: 'Will universities need physical libraries in the future?' Give a reason and a qualification.

Reveal check

'Yes, although their use may change. Students still need quiet study space and help judging sources; digital collections can reduce the need for some physical storage.'

Common trap Turning a tendency into an absolute prediction without support.

Repair pronunciation and self-monitoring

Principle You may clarify a question or correct a mistake. Intelligibility depends on stress, grouping, pace, and clear contrasts more than imitation of one accent.

Use If you misunderstand, ask for repetition. If you notice an error, make one short correction and continue. Record a sample and check whether a listener can follow each idea.

Worked example 'I visited it last year—sorry, two years ago—when my course began.' The correction is brief and the talk continues.

Try You did not hear a key word in the examiner's question. What should you say?

Reveal check

'Could you repeat the question, please?' If only one term is unclear, ask what that term means.

Common trap Restarting the whole response repeatedly or apologising so much that fluency is lost.

SECTION 08

Integrated practice

Listening mini drill

Ask a partner or text-to-speech tool to read the script once at a natural pace while you hide it. This is an original six-question teaching drill, not an official listening score. Fill each blank with ONE WORD OR A NUMBER.

1 Meeting day: ____
2 Meeting time: ____
3 Room number: ____
4 First material to bring: ____
5 Draft due in ____ days
6 Contact person: Dr ____

Reader script

Student: Is the project meeting still on Tuesday? Tutor: I first booked Tuesday, but the laboratory is closed then. Let us meet on Thursday at two. Student: In room fifteen? Tutor: Room fifty, not fifteen. It is beside the main staircase. Student: Should I bring the survey results? Tutor: Bring the outline first; we can discuss the results after that. Student: When do you need my draft? Tutor: I said five days, but the deadline has moved. You have seven days. Student: If I have a question, should I email Professor Hart? Tutor: Email Dr Reed. She is coordinating the project this week.

Reveal answers

1 Thursday; 2 two or 2; 3 fifty or 50; 4 outline; 5 seven or 7; 6 Reed. If a real test specifies a particular format, follow that instruction. The Tuesday, fifteen, results, five, and Hart references are deliberate distractors.

Reading mini drill

Read the original passage. Then answer without outside knowledge. The purpose is evidence selection, not speed.

A university tested a new booking system for shared study rooms. The former system allowed students to reserve a room for up to four hours, even if they left early. Under the trial, a booking expired after 15 minutes if nobody checked in. The university introduced this rule after staff observed that rooms were sometimes reserved but empty.

During the six-week trial, recorded room use increased from 62% to 76%. The research team warned that this change did not prove the rule alone caused the increase. A revision to the timetable had taken effect in the same month, and the team had not measured how many students worked elsewhere when rooms were unavailable.

Student feedback was mixed. Some students valued the larger number of rooms that appeared available at short notice. Others reported that the check-in screen was difficult to use on older phones. In response, the university added a check-in option at the library desk. It kept the new expiry rule for a second trial, but promised to compare booking records with direct observations before deciding whether to make the policy permanent.

1 Why was the expiry rule introduced?
2 Complete with ONE WORD: A booking expired if nobody ____ in.
3 True, False, or Not Given: The research team concluded that the new rule alone caused the rise in recorded room use.
4 True, False, or Not Given: The research team measured study that took place outside the rooms.
5 Which problem prompted an additional check-in option?
6 What two forms of evidence will be compared before a permanent decision?

Reveal answers

1 Staff had observed reserved rooms standing empty. 2 checked. 3 False: the team explicitly warned that the change did not prove causation by the rule alone. 4 False: the team had not measured it. 5 Difficulty using the screen on older phones. 6 Booking records and direct observations.

Evidence audit Question 3 is not a judgement about whether the rule was helpful; it is a judgement about the passage's causal claim. Question 4 is directly contradicted by 'had not measured'.

SECTION 09

Self-check and error log

Listening For each missed answer, label: lost place; distractor; paraphrase; number or spelling; word-limit error. Replay only after recording the first decision.

Reading Underline the exact supporting or contradicting words. If you cannot identify them, do not keep the answer because it 'sounds right'.

Writing Check task fulfilment first, then structure, support, grammar, and word choice. Rewrite one weak paragraph fully rather than polishing isolated vocabulary.

Speaking Record a fresh two-minute response. Check whether the idea is clear, developed, and easy to follow; mark only a few recurring grammar or pronunciation problems for the next attempt.

Log entry Date | task type | original answer | decisive evidence | corrected rule | new example. Revisit errors after two days and again after one week.

SECTION 10

Quick review index

A Listening option is mentioned then changed Follow the final agreed decision and speaker roles.

A number is heard twice Check whether one number is corrected, compared, or belongs to a different item.

A map or process is confusing Reset at the named starting point or next signpost; follow order.

A Reading heading looks plausible Summarise the whole paragraph before choosing; ignore isolated word matches.

A completion blank is difficult Predict grammar, find a paraphrase, obey the exact word limit.

True or False or Not Given is uncertain Support means True; contradiction means False; missing evidence means Not Given.

Yes or No or Not Given is uncertain Apply the same logic to the writer's view or claim.

A multiple-choice option is partly true Ask whether it answers the question exactly and preserves qualifications.

Task 1 has many numbers State the overview first; group key comparisons; keep units and dates accurate.

Task 1 is a process or map Describe shown stages or spatial changes; do not invent causes.

Task 2 feels broad Circle the instruction and decide a bounded position before drafting.

A body paragraph feels thin Add why, when, and a concrete example that supports the claim.

Speaking answer runs out Add one reason, example, consequence, or qualification.

You lose a word in Speaking Ask briefly for repetition or clarification, then continue.
