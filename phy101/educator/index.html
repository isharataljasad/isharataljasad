PHY 101 · TOPIC 01 OF 8

Units and measurement

Physics is measured quantities with units. Convert carefully, check dimensions, and report results with sensible precision.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

A number without a unit means nothing in physics: “the speed is 25” could be 25 m/s or 25 km/h, a factor of 3.6 apart. Many real engineering failures came from mixed units. Units are also your best error detector: if an equation for a distance produces m/s, it is wrong before you compare any numbers.

Every experiment also has limited precision. Knowing how many digits are meaningful stops you reporting 3.14159 m for a length measured with a ruler.

The idea

Every physical quantity is a number times a unit. The SI system uses seven base units; in mechanics you need three: the metre (m) for length, the kilogram (kg) for mass, and the second (s) for time. All other mechanical units are combinations: speed in m/s, acceleration in m/s2, force in newtons, 1 N = 1 kg·m/s2, energy in joules, 1 J = 1 N·m.

To convert units, multiply by conversion factors that equal 1, such as 1000 m / 1 km or 1 h / 3600 s. Arrange each factor so the unwanted unit cancels, exactly like cancelling numbers in a fraction. Because each factor equals 1, the physical quantity does not change — only its description does.

Prefixes scale units by powers of ten: kilo (k) = 103, centi (c) = 10−2, milli (m) = 10−3, micro (μ) = 10−6. For squared and cubed units the factor is squared or cubed: 1 m2 = (100 cm)2 = 104 cm2, and 1 m3 = 106 cm3 = 1000 L.

Dimensional analysis checks equations: both sides, and every term added together, must have the same dimensions. In x = x0 + v0t + 1 / 2at2, each term is a length: m, (m/s)(s) = m, (m/s2)(s2) = m. You can add metres to metres, never metres to seconds.

Significant figures communicate precision. When multiplying or dividing, keep as many significant figures as the least precise input. When adding or subtracting, keep as many decimal places as the least precise input. Keep extra digits during a calculation and round only the final answer.

Speed conversion

    km and h cancel.

    Multiply the numbers.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: Converting cm² to m² by dividing by 100.

    Correct idea: Square the factor: divide by 10⁴. For volumes divide by 10⁶.

  • Misunderstanding: Mixing units in one formula (km with m, minutes with seconds).

    Correct idea: Convert everything to SI first.

  • Misunderstanding: Rounding at every intermediate step.

    Correct idea: Carry extra digits and round only the final answer, or rounding errors accumulate.

  • Misunderstanding: “Dimensionally consistent means correct.”

    Correct idea: Consistency is necessary, not sufficient; numerical factors like ½ cannot be checked by dimensions.

  • Misunderstanding: Confusing mass (kg) and weight (N).

    Correct idea: Mass is the amount of matter; weight is the gravitational force mg, measured in newtons.

01

Measurements, units, and model limits

Physical idea

A number is a physical answer only when its units and meaning are clear. Dimensional analysis checks whether an equation can be sensible; it cannot prove that the physics model is correct.

First move

Before calculating, identify the system, draw a simple representation, convert to SI units, and state the approximation. Compare dimensions on both sides of an equation and round the final result to a precision justified by the data.

Worked reasoning

A car moves at 72 km/h. Dividing by 3.6 gives 20 m/s. In 5 s at constant speed it travels 100 m. The calculation would fail if the car were accelerating and 72 km/h were only its starting speed.

Additional worked example

A runner covers 150 m in 25 s. Find average speed and check its units.

150/25 = 6.0 m/s. Distance divided by time has dimension L/T.

Common error. A mathematically consistent result can still use the wrong model; write the condition that makes the equation valid.

PHY 101 · TOPIC 02 OF 8

Vectors and motion

Describe motion with position, velocity and acceleration as vectors; with constant acceleration, four equations predict everything, and horizontal and vertical motions are independent.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Kinematics — the description of motion — is the language of all mechanics. Before asking why something moves (forces), you need to describe how it moves: where it is, how fast, in which direction, and how that changes. Braking distances, falling objects, thrown or launched objects and conveyor systems are all kinematics.

The idea

Many physical quantities have a direction as well as a size. Vectors (displacement, velocity, acceleration, force) have magnitude and direction; scalars (distance, speed, time, mass, energy) have magnitude only. In one dimension the direction is shown by a sign after choosing a positive direction: +5 m/s means 5 m/s in the positive direction, −5 m/s means 5 m/s the other way.

Displacement Δx = xf − xi is the change in position, not the distance travelled: walk 3 m east and 3 m back and your displacement is 0 though you walked 6 m. Velocity is the rate of change of position, v = dx/dt; acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, a = dv/dt. Acceleration is not “speed”: a car braking while moving forward has negative acceleration (with forward positive), and a ball at the top of its flight has zero velocity but acceleration −9.8 m/s².

On graphs: the slope of a position–time graph is velocity; the slope of a velocity–time graph is acceleration; the area under a velocity–time graph is displacement.

When acceleration is constant, the four “kinematic equations” below follow from these definitions. Choose the one that contains the unknown and three known quantities. Free fall near the Earth’s surface, neglecting air resistance, is constant acceleration with a = −g = −9.8 m/s² (taking up as positive).

In two dimensions, resolve vectors into perpendicular components. For a projectile with no air resistance, the horizontal and vertical motions are independent: horizontally ax = 0, so vx is constant; vertically ay = −g. The same time t links them. At the top of the path vy = 0, but vx and the acceleration g are unchanged.

An example, explained

Speed after constant acceleration

Problem. A cart starts from rest and accelerates at 3 m/s2 for 4 s. Find its final speed and the distance travelled.

  1. v0 = 0, a = 3 m/s2, t = 4 s.

    List the knowns.

  2. v = v0 + at = 0 + 3 × 4 = 12 m/s.

    Equation containing v, v₀, a, t.

  3. Δx = v0t + 1 / 2at2 = 0 + 1 / 2(3)(16) = 24 m.

    Equation containing Δx.

Result: v = 12 m/s; Δx = 24 m.

What it means: Check with the average velocity: (0 + 12)/2 × 4 = 24 m.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: Treating distance and displacement as the same.

    Correct idea: Displacement is a signed vector change in position; distance is the total path length.

  • Misunderstanding: Deceleration means negative acceleration.

    Correct idea: An object slows down when a and v have opposite signs. Moving in the negative direction and speeding up also gives negative a.

  • Misunderstanding: “At the top, acceleration is zero.”

    Correct idea: Velocity is zero at the top; acceleration is still g downward.

  • Misunderstanding: Using constant-acceleration equations when a changes.

    Correct idea: Split the motion into stages of constant a, or use calculus.

  • Misunderstanding: Using the range formula when launch and landing heights differ.

    Correct idea: Solve the vertical equation for t instead.

  • Misunderstanding: Mixing sin and cos in components.

    Correct idea: cos goes with the side adjacent to the angle. Draw the triangle.

02

Vectors, components, and direction

Physical idea

Scalars have magnitude; vectors also have direction. Components are signed projections on chosen axes. Vector addition operates component by component, not by adding magnitudes unless the vectors are collinear and aligned.

First move

Draw axes and an arrow. Resolve every vector into x and y components using an angle measured from the stated axis. Add components, then find magnitude and quadrant of the resultant. Keep signs attached to components.

Worked reasoning

A 10 N force at 30° above +x has components (8.66, 5.00) N. Added to a 4 N downward force, it gives (8.66, 1.00) N, with magnitude about 8.72 N.

Additional worked example

Add 3 m east and 4 m north. State magnitude and direction north of east.

The displacement is (3,4) m, magnitude 5 m, at arctan(4/3) ≈ 53.1° north of east.

Common error. An angle found from arctan(Ay/Ax) needs a quadrant check when either component is negative.

03

Position, velocity, acceleration, and motion graphs

Physical idea

Displacement is final minus initial position and can be negative. Speed uses distance traveled; velocity uses displacement. On a position-time graph slope is velocity. On a velocity-time graph slope is acceleration and signed area is displacement.

First move

Read axis labels first. For a straight segment, calculate rise over run. For area under v(t), split the region into familiar shapes and preserve area below the time axis as negative.

Worked reasoning

Velocity rises linearly from 0 to 8 m/s in 4 s, then stays at 8 m/s for 2 s. Displacement is triangle 16 m plus rectangle 16 m, totaling 32 m.

Additional worked example

A body moves at +3 m/s for 4 s, then −2 m/s for 3 s. Find displacement and total distance.

Displacement is 12−6=6 m. Distance is 12+6=18 m.

Common error. Area under a velocity graph is displacement, not always distance; use absolute areas for distance.

04

Constant acceleration and free fall

Physical idea

These kinematic relations apply to constant acceleration along one axis. For near-Earth free fall without appreciable air drag, vertical acceleration is approximately −g if up is positive, even while the object rises.

First move

Choose a positive direction, list known and unknown quantities, and select the relation that excludes the variable you do not have. Check whether the acceleration is constant before using these equations.

Worked reasoning

A ball is thrown upward at 20 m/s with g≈10 m/s². At the top v=0, so 0=20−10t and t=2 s. Its rise is 20(2)−5(2²)=20 m.

Additional worked example

A car starts at 5 m/s and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 4 s. Find final speed and displacement.

v=5+2(4)=13 m/s; Δx=5(4)+0.5(2)(4²)=36 m.

Common error. At the top of a vertical throw velocity is zero for an instant; acceleration is still downward.

05

Projectile and relative motion

Physical idea

Ideal projectile motion has constant horizontal velocity and vertical acceleration due to gravity. The two components share time but otherwise solve independently. Relative velocity depends on the chosen reference frame.

First move

Resolve launch velocity, solve the vertical equation for flight time at the specified height, then insert that time in the horizontal equation. For relative motion, subtract the observer velocity vector.

Worked reasoning

From a level surface, launch at 20 m/s and 30° with g≈10 m/s². The vertical component is 10 m/s, so the return time is 2 s. Horizontal component is 17.3 m/s, giving range about 34.6 m.

Additional worked example

A boat moves east at 5 m/s relative to water while the river flows north at 2 m/s. Find its speed relative to shore.

The shore-frame velocity is (5,2) m/s and speed is √29≈5.39 m/s. Its path points north of east.

Common error. The familiar level-ground range formula fails when launch and landing heights differ.

Newton laws and force diagrams

Forces change motion. Isolate one object, draw every force acting on it, and apply ΣF = ma along each axis.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Newton’s laws explain and predict motion from its causes. Every structure, machine and vehicle is designed by balancing or using forces: cables in tension, supports pushing up, engines pushing forward, friction resisting. The free-body diagram is the single most important tool in mechanics; nearly every mistake in force problems is a mistake in the diagram.

The idea

First law (inertia): if the net force on an object is zero, its velocity stays constant — it stays at rest or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed. A force is not needed to keep something moving; it is needed to change its motion.

Second law: the net force equals mass times acceleration, ΣF = ma. It is a vector equation, so it holds separately along each axis: ΣFx = max and ΣFy = may. Mass measures how hard it is to change an object’s velocity. The unit of force is the newton: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

Third law: forces come in pairs. If A pushes on B, then B pushes on A with an equal and opposite force. The two forces act on different objects, so they never cancel each other on a single free-body diagram. The Earth pulls you down (your weight); you pull the Earth up equally.

A free-body diagram (FBD) shows one chosen object and only the forces acting on it: weight (mg, down), contact forces (normal force perpendicular to a surface, friction along it), tensions (along ropes, pulling away from the object), and applied pushes or pulls. Forces the object exerts on other things do not belong on its diagram, and neither does “ma” — ma is the result, not a force.

The normal force is whatever the surface must supply to stop the object sinking in. It equals the weight only in the simplest case (horizontal surface, no other vertical forces, no vertical acceleration). If you pull up on a rope at an angle, the floor pushes less; in an accelerating lift it changes too.

Background you need

Vector components

A force F at angle θ above the +x axis has Fx = F cos θ and Fy = F sin θ (see Vectors and motion).

Weight

Weight is the gravitational force: W = mg, with g = 9.8 m/s² near the Earth’s surface. A 5 kg mass weighs 49 N. Mass (kg) is not weight (N).

An example, explained

Acceleration from the net force

Problem. A 4 kg object has horizontal forces of 20 N to the right and 8 N to the left. Find its acceleration.

  1. Take right as positive: ΣFx = 20 − 8 = 12 N.

    Add forces with signs.

  2. a = ΣF / m = 12 / 4 = 3 m/s2.

    Newton’s second law.

Result: a = 3 m/s2 to the right.

What it means: Only the net force matters; the object could be moving either way at this instant.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: “A moving object needs a net force to keep moving.”

    Correct idea: Constant velocity needs zero net force. Net force causes acceleration, not motion.

  • Misunderstanding: Cancelling third-law pairs on one FBD.

    Correct idea: The pair acts on two different objects. On one object’s diagram only one of them appears.

  • Misunderstanding: Assuming N = mg always.

    Correct idea: Find N from ΣF_y = ma_y. Angled pulls, inclines and accelerating lifts change it.

  • Misunderstanding: Drawing “ma” as a force on the FBD.

    Correct idea: ma is the result of the forces; it is not an extra force.

  • Misunderstanding: Using mass instead of weight (kg instead of N).

    Correct idea: Weight is mg in newtons.

06

Newton laws and free body diagrams

Physical idea

An object accelerates when the vector sum of external forces is nonzero. A free body diagram contains forces acting on one chosen object. Newton third-law partners act on different objects and cannot cancel on one diagram.

First move

Isolate the body, draw only real external forces, choose axes, and write a force equation for each axis. If acceleration is zero, the sum is zero; a moving object can still have zero acceleration.

Worked reasoning

A 2 kg block on a horizontal frictionless surface is pulled right by 6 N. Its weight and normal force cancel vertically, while horizontal acceleration is 6/2=3 m/s².

Additional worked example

A 4 kg object has 18 N right and 6 N left. Find its acceleration.

Net force is 12 N right, so a=12/4=3 m/s² right.

Common error. Do not put the force exerted by the object on its surroundings on the object’s own free body diagram.

08

Connected objects and tension

Physical idea

Objects joined by a taut, light string over an ideal pulley share the same acceleration magnitude. The string tension is the same on both sides only under the ideal string and pulley model.

First move

Draw a separate free body diagram for each mass. Pick one consistent positive direction along the string for the whole system. Write both force equations, then eliminate tension to solve acceleration.

Worked reasoning

For m1=2 kg and m2=3 kg, with g=10, the ideal Atwood acceleration is (3−2)10/5=2 m/s², with m2 downward. From T−m1g=m1a, tension is 24 N.

Additional worked example

For 1 kg and 3 kg masses on an ideal pulley, find acceleration using g=10.

a=(3−1)10/(1+3)=5 m/s²; the 3 kg mass moves down.

Common error. Do not set tension equal to the heavier weight when the system accelerates.

Friction and inclined surfaces

Friction is the contact force along a surface. Static friction adjusts up to a limit; kinetic friction has a fixed size, μₖN, and opposes sliding.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Friction lets you walk, lets car tyres grip and brakes stop, and keeps loads from sliding off conveyors. It also wastes energy in machines. Inclined planes appear in ramps, conveyors, roads and chutes; resolving forces along and perpendicular to a slope is a skill used throughout mechanics.

The idea

When two surfaces are pressed together, the contact force has a part perpendicular to the surface (the normal force N) and a part along it (friction). Friction opposes relative sliding — or, if the surfaces are not sliding, the tendency to slide.

Static friction acts when there is no sliding. It is not a fixed number: it takes whatever value (from 0 up to a maximum μsN) is needed to prevent slipping. Push a heavy box gently and it does not move: static friction exactly matches your push. Push harder than μsN and the box starts to slide.

Kinetic friction acts once sliding occurs: fk = μkN, directed opposite to the velocity of sliding. Usually μk < μs, which is why it is harder to start something sliding than to keep it sliding. The coefficients depend on the pair of materials, not (to a good approximation) on the contact area or the speed.

On an incline of angle θ, choose axes along the slope and perpendicular to it. Then only the weight needs resolving: mg sin θ acts down the slope and mg cos θ acts into the slope. With no other perpendicular forces, N = mg cos θ, not mg.

A block rests on an incline without sliding as long as mg sin θ ≤ μsmg cos θ, that is tan θ ≤ μs. The angle at which it just starts to slide satisfies tan θ = μs; notice that the mass cancels.

Resolving on a slope

The angle between the weight and the perpendicular to the slope equals the slope angle θ. So the components are mg sin θ (along) and mg cos θ (perpendicular). Check with θ = 0: nothing along, all perpendicular.

An example, explained

Kinetic friction on a level floor

Problem. A 20 kg crate is pushed across a floor by a horizontal 80 N force. μk = 0.30. Find the friction force and the acceleration (g = 9.8 m/s2).

  1. N = mg = 20 × 9.8 = 196 N.

    Horizontal push: no other vertical forces, so here N = mg.

  2. fk = 0.30 × 196 = 58.8 N, opposite to the motion.

    Kinetic friction formula.

  3. a = 80 − 58.8 / 20 = 1.06 m/s2.

    Net force divided by mass.

Result: fk = 58.8 N; a ≈ 1.06 m/s2.

What it means: If the push were exactly 58.8 N the crate would slide at constant velocity.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: Always writing f = μN for static friction.

    Correct idea: Static friction is ≤ μₛN. Use the equality only at the point of slipping.

  • Misunderstanding: Taking N = mg on an incline.

    Correct idea: On an incline N = mg cos θ (if no other perpendicular forces).

  • Misunderstanding: Friction always points backwards.

    Correct idea: It opposes relative sliding. A crate on an accelerating truck bed is pushed forward by static friction.

  • Misunderstanding: Swapping sin and cos on the incline.

    Correct idea: Check θ = 0: the along-slope component must vanish, so it is mg sin θ.

  • Misunderstanding: Friction depends on contact area.

    Correct idea: In the simple model it depends only on μ and N.

07

Friction and inclined planes

Physical idea

Static friction adjusts up to a maximum. Kinetic friction has approximate magnitude μkN and opposes slipping. On a plane inclined at angle θ, gravity has a downhill component mg sinθ and a perpendicular component mg cosθ.

First move

Choose one axis along the plane. Determine the direction of actual or impending relative motion before drawing friction. Compute N from the perpendicular force balance if the object has no perpendicular acceleration.

Worked reasoning

A 5 kg block slides down a 30° incline with μk=0.20 and g=10 m/s². Downhill gravity is 25 N; N≈43.3 N and friction≈8.66 N uphill. Acceleration is (25−8.66)/5≈3.27 m/s² downhill.

Additional worked example

A 2 kg block is at rest on a 30° incline with μs=0.8 and g=10. Is rest possible?

Downhill force is 10 N; N≈17.3 N, so maximum static friction≈13.9 N. Rest is possible and actual friction is 10 N uphill.

Common error. Static friction is not automatically μsN; that expression is only its limiting value.

PHY 101 · TOPIC 05 OF 8

Work energy and power

Work transfers energy. Kinetic and potential energy trade places, and the total is conserved unless friction or other forces add or remove energy. Power is the rate of energy transfer.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Energy methods solve problems that are awkward with forces alone: the speed of a roller coaster at the bottom of a hill, the height a spring launches a ball, the power a motor needs to lift a load. Energy is also the currency of engineering: efficiency, losses, fuel and electricity costs are all energy bookkeeping.

The idea

Work is how a force transfers energy to or from an object as it moves: W = Fd cos θ, where θ is the angle between the force and the displacement. Only the component of the force along the motion does work. A force perpendicular to the motion (like the normal force on a level floor) does no work. A force opposing the motion (friction) does negative work: it removes energy.

Kinetic energy K = 1 / 2mv2 is the energy of motion. The work–energy theorem says the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: Wnet = ΔK. Note the v²: doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy, which is why stopping distances grow so quickly with speed.

Some forces — gravity and ideal springs — store the work done against them as potential energy: Ug = mgh (height measured from any chosen reference level) and Us = 1 / 2kx2. These are conservative forces: the work they do depends only on the start and end positions, not the path.

If only conservative forces do work, mechanical energy is conserved: K + U stays constant. If friction or other non-conservative forces act, Ki + Ui + Wother = Kf + Uf; friction’s negative work becomes thermal energy. Energy is never destroyed, only transferred or transformed.

Power is the rate of doing work or transferring energy: P = W/Δt, measured in watts (1 W = 1 J/s). For a force along the velocity, P = Fv. Efficiency compares useful output with input: η = Pout/Pin, always less than 100% in real machines.

Components and the cosine

The component of F along the displacement is F cos θ. cos 0° = 1, cos 90° = 0, cos 180° = −1.

An example, explained

Work by a force at an angle

Problem. A 50 N pull at 37° above the horizontal drags a box 4.0 m across a floor. How much work does the pull do?

  1. W = Fd cos θ = 50 × 4.0 × cos 37°.

    Only the horizontal component does work.

  2. = 200 × 0.799 = 160 J.

    cos 37° ≈ 0.799.

Result: W ≈ 160 J.

What it means: The vertical component (≈ 30 N) does no work because the box does not move vertically.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: “Any force on a moving object does work.”

    Correct idea: Only the component along the displacement does work; perpendicular forces (normal force on a level floor, centripetal force) do none.

  • Misunderstanding: Forgetting the sign of work.

    Correct idea: Friction and braking forces do negative work, reducing kinetic energy.

  • Misunderstanding: Using K = mv² or forgetting to square v.

    Correct idea: K = ½mv².

  • Misunderstanding: Using conservation of mechanical energy when friction acts.

    Correct idea: Include W_friction (negative) or the energy lost to heat.

  • Misunderstanding: Confusing energy (J) with power (W).

    Correct idea: Power is energy per unit time. kW·h is a unit of energy.

15

Work, energy, and power

Physical idea

Work transfers energy when a force acts through displacement. Only the force component along motion contributes work. Power measures the rate of energy transfer, not the amount of energy itself.

First move

Identify the force and displacement direction. For a constant force use W=Fd cosθ. Compare net work with change in kinetic energy and keep joules and watts distinct.

Worked reasoning

A 20 N force acts 3 m at 60° to motion. Work is 20·3·cos60°=30 J. If that work occurs in 2 s, average power is 15 W.

Additional worked example

A 4 kg object speeds up from 2 to 4 m/s. Find net work.

ΔK=0.5·4·(4²−2²)=24 J.

Common error. A force perpendicular to displacement can change direction without doing work.

16

Conservation of energy and losses

Physical idea

Total energy is conserved. Mechanical energy K+U remains constant only when nonconservative work is zero. Gravitational U near Earth is mgh; spring energy is ½kx² relative to the unstretched state.

First move

Choose system and reference height. Mark starting and ending states, then list stored energies and external or dissipative work. If friction acts, its work is typically negative on mechanical energy.

Worked reasoning

A 2 kg object drops 5 m with g=10 and no drag. It loses 100 J of gravitational energy and gains 100 J of kinetic energy. If 20 J is dissipated, final kinetic energy is 80 J.

Additional worked example

A 1 kg object starts from rest 3 m above a reference level. With g=10 and no losses, find speed at the reference level.

mgh=½mv² gives v=√(2gh)=√60≈7.75 m/s.

Common error. “Energy conserved” never means kinetic energy alone is conserved.

Impulse and momentum

Momentum p = mv is a vector. A force acting for a time changes it (impulse). In a collision with no external force, total momentum is conserved.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Momentum is the natural quantity for collisions, impacts and explosions — crash safety, pile drivers, jet and rocket propulsion, the recoil of a gun. During a collision the forces are huge, brief and hard to measure, but momentum lets you predict the outcome without knowing them. Impulse explains why airbags, crumple zones and bent knees reduce injuries.

The idea

Momentum p = mv combines mass and velocity. It is a vector, so direction matters: with right positive, a 2 kg ball at 3 m/s to the left has p = −6 kg·m/s.

Newton’s second law can be written ΣF = Δp / Δt. Rearranged, impulse J = FΔt = Δp: a force acting for a time changes momentum. To stop a moving object you need a definite impulse (its momentum). You can deliver it with a large force for a short time or a small force for a long time. Airbags and crumple zones lengthen the stopping time, so the force on the passenger is smaller.

In a collision between two objects, each pushes the other with equal and opposite forces for the same time (Newton’s third law). So the impulses are equal and opposite and the momentum lost by one is gained by the other. If no external net force acts on the system, the total momentum is conserved: Σpbefore = Σpafter. This is true for every collision, bouncy or sticky.

Kinetic energy is a different story. In an elastic collision kinetic energy is also conserved (ideal billiard balls). In an inelastic collision some kinetic energy becomes heat, sound and deformation. In a perfectly inelastic collision the objects stick together, and the loss of kinetic energy is as large as momentum conservation allows.

The centre of mass of a system moves as if all the mass were there and all external forces acted there. With no external force, the centre of mass moves at constant velocity, even while the parts collide or fly apart.

    Slowing down in the positive direction: negative impulse.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: Ignoring the signs of velocities.

    Correct idea: Momentum is a vector. Opposite directions need opposite signs.

  • Misunderstanding: Conserving kinetic energy in every collision.

    Correct idea: Only in elastic collisions. Momentum is conserved in all isolated collisions.

  • Misunderstanding: Using conservation of momentum when an external force acts over a long time.

    Correct idea: Check the system is isolated, or the interaction brief.

  • Misunderstanding: Treating impulse as a force.

    Correct idea: Impulse is force × time, with units N·s.

Impulse and momentum

Physical idea

Momentum is a vector. Impulse is the change in momentum and equals the signed area under a force-time graph. Extending stopping time reduces average force for a fixed momentum change.

First move

Choose a direction and calculate final momentum minus initial momentum. For variable force, use graph area or an integral. Average force is Δp/Δt over the same interval.

Worked reasoning

A 0.15 kg ball approaches at +20 m/s and rebounds at −10 m/s. Its momentum change is 0.15(−10−20)=−4.5 N·s. If contact lasts 0.03 s, average force on the ball is −150 N.

Additional worked example

A 2 kg cart slows from +5 to +1 m/s in 0.5 s. Find impulse and average force.

Δp=2(1−5)=−8 N·s; Favg=−8/0.5=−16 N.

Common error. For a rebound, the velocity sign changes. Subtracting speed magnitudes loses that direction change.

Collisions and system boundaries

Physical idea

Momentum is conserved for an isolated system during a collision. Kinetic energy is conserved only for an elastic collision. Objects that stick together share one final velocity.

First move

Define both bodies as the system and mark velocity directions. Write momentum conservation first. Add kinetic-energy conservation only when the collision is stated or shown to be elastic.

Worked reasoning

A 2 kg cart at +3 m/s sticks to a 1 kg cart initially at rest. Final speed is (2·3)/(2+1)=2 m/s. Kinetic energy drops from 9 J to 6 J, so the collision is inelastic.

Additional worked example

A 1 kg cart at +4 m/s sticks to a 3 kg cart at rest. Find final velocity.

Total momentum is +4 kg·m/s and total mass is 4 kg, so vf=+1 m/s.

Circular motion

An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating towards the centre, a = v²/r. Some real force (tension, friction, gravity, normal force) must supply the net inward force mv²/r.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Cars rounding bends, centrifuges, rotating machinery, satellites and roller coaster loops all move on curved paths. Designing a safe road bend (friction and banking), a centrifuge (separation force) or a rotating shaft (stresses) needs the relationship between speed, radius and inward force.

The idea

Velocity is a vector. In uniform circular motion the speed is constant, but the direction of the velocity changes continuously (it is always tangent to the circle). A change of velocity is an acceleration, so the object is accelerating even though its speed does not change.

The acceleration points towards the centre (centripetal means “centre-seeking”) and has magnitude ac = v2 / r. Faster motion or a tighter circle means more acceleration: doubling the speed quadruples it.

By Newton’s second law, a net force towards the centre is required: ΣFin = mv2 / r. This is not a new kind of force. It is the net result of real forces: the tension in a string whirling a ball, friction between tyres and road, gravity on a satellite, the normal force from a banked track or loop. On a free-body diagram you draw the real forces only, never a separate “centripetal force”.

If the inward force disappears (a string breaks), the object does not fly outward along the radius; it continues in a straight line along the tangent, as the first law says. The outward “push” you feel in a turning car is your body’s inertia, not a real outward force.

Circular motion is often described with angles: the angular speed ω = Δθ/Δt in rad/s, with v = ωr, and the period T (time for one revolution), with v = 2πr / T and ω = 2π / T.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: “Constant speed means no acceleration.”

    Correct idea: The direction changes, so the velocity changes: a = v²/r inward.

  • Misunderstanding: Adding a separate “centripetal force” to the free-body diagram.

    Correct idea: mv²/r is the required net inward force, supplied by real forces.

  • Misunderstanding: “There is an outward centrifugal force.”

    Correct idea: In an inertial frame there is none; the outward feeling is inertia. If released, objects move along the tangent.

  • Misunderstanding: Using the diameter instead of the radius.

    Correct idea: r is measured from the centre of the circle.

  • Misunderstanding: Using rpm directly as ω.

    Correct idea: Convert: ω (rad/s) = rpm × 2π/60.

Circular motion and inward force

A 1 kg object moves at 6 m/s around a 2 m circle. Find inward acceleration and net radial force.

Angular motion and torque

Physical idea

Angular displacement and angular speed describe how a rigid body turns. Torque measures how strongly a force tends to rotate it, depending on force magnitude, lever arm, and the angle between them.

First move

Use radians in v=rω. For torque, measure r from the chosen pivot to the point where force acts, then use only the component perpendicular to r. Assign positive or negative torque by rotation sense.

Worked reasoning

A 10 N force perpendicular to a 0.30 m wrench produces torque 3.0 N·m. If the same force acts along the wrench, sinφ=0 and torque about the bolt is zero.

Additional worked example

A wheel has radius 0.20 m and angular speed 15 rad/s. Find rim speed.

v=rω=0.20·15=3.0 m/s.

Laboratory graphs and evidence

Plot measured data, draw the best straight line, and read physics from its slope and intercept — with units and a realistic uncertainty.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Laboratory work is part of PHY 101. Physical laws are tested and constants are measured by plotting data and fitting a line. A graph averages out random scatter, shows whether a model fits, and reveals mistakes that a single calculation hides. Engineers use the same skill to calibrate sensors and extract material properties.

The idea

Put the quantity you control (the independent variable) on the horizontal axis and the one you measure in response (the dependent variable) on the vertical axis. Label both axes with the quantity and its unit, and choose scales that spread the points over most of the graph.

If the relationship is linear, y = mx + b, the points should scatter around a straight line. Draw the best-fit line: roughly as many points above as below, following the trend — not simply joining the first and last points, and not forced through every point. Real data always scatter.

The slope m = Δy / Δx has units (y-unit)/(x-unit) and usually means something physical: the slope of position against time is velocity, of velocity against time is acceleration, of force against extension is the spring constant. Calculate it from two points on the line, far apart, not from two data points.

The intercept b is the value of y when x = 0. A theory may predict it (often zero); a non-zero intercept can indicate a systematic error, like a zero offset in the instrument.

Many laws are not linear, but can be made linear. For a falling object, d = 1 / 2gt2: plot d against t2 (not t) to get a straight line with slope g/2. For a pendulum, T = 2π√L/g: plot T2 against L, slope 4π2/g. This is called linearising the data.

Every measurement has uncertainty. Random errors scatter the points and can be reduced by repeating and averaging; systematic errors shift all readings the same way and cannot be removed by averaging. Report results as value ± uncertainty with a unit, and compare with an accepted value by percentage difference.

    Correct idea: Averaging reduces random errors only; systematic errors remain.

Gravity and circular orbits

Physical idea

Gravity acts between masses at center-to-center separation r. In a circular orbit, gravity itself provides the inward acceleration. Orbital speed depends on central mass and orbital radius in the ideal two-body approximation.

First move

Use distance from the central body’s center, not altitude alone. For an orbit, equate GMm/r² to mv²/r. Check that the object is in a circular orbit before using the speed result.

Worked reasoning

If orbit radius doubles around the same planet, the gravitational force falls to one quarter and circular orbital speed falls by a factor of 1/√2.

Additional worked example

At what factor does gravitational force change if separation triples?

It becomes 1/9 of its original magnitude, since force varies as 1/r².

Common error. Doubling altitude does not necessarily double orbital radius; add the planet’s radius first.

Springs and simple harmonic motion

Physical idea

A spring force points toward equilibrium and grows with displacement. In ideal simple harmonic motion, energy alternates between kinetic and spring potential while total mechanical energy stays fixed.

First move

Measure x from equilibrium and keep the restoring-force sign. Use T=2π√(m/k) only for an ideal mass-spring system. At endpoints speed is zero; at equilibrium speed is greatest.

Worked reasoning

For m=1 kg and k=100 N/m, T=2π/10≈0.628 s. If amplitude is 0.20 m, total oscillation energy is ½·100·0.20²=2 J.

Additional worked example

If spring stiffness quadruples while mass stays fixed, how does period change?

T is proportional to 1/√k, so period is halved.

Common error. The object’s acceleration is largest in magnitude at the endpoints, not at equilibrium.

Rotational dynamics and angular momentum

Physical idea

Moment of inertia measures resistance to angular acceleration and depends on mass distribution about a specified axis. With negligible external torque, angular momentum is conserved even if rotational kinetic energy changes.

First move

State the axis, use its correct I, and include all external torques. For conservation questions, compare initial and final Iω; a smaller I requires a larger ω when L stays constant.

Worked reasoning

A rotating system reduces I from 4 to 2 kg·m² while no external torque acts. If ω initially is 3 rad/s, final ω is (4·3)/2=6 rad/s. Rotational energy rises because internal work was done.

Additional worked example

A wheel with I=0.5 kg·m² experiences net torque 2 N·m. Find angular acceleration.

α=τ/I=2/0.5=4 rad/s².

Density, pressure, and buoyancy

Physical idea

Pressure is normal force per area. A fluid pushes harder at greater depth, and a submerged object experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of displaced fluid. Floating equilibrium requires buoyant force to equal the object’s weight.

First move

For buoyancy, use the displaced fluid’s density and displaced volume, not automatically the object’s full volume. Compare buoyant force with weight before deciding whether an object rises, sinks, or floats.

Worked reasoning

A fully submerged 0.002 m³ object in water with ρ=1000 kg/m³ and g=10 receives Fb=20 N. If it weighs 15 N, the net force is 5 N upward before drag.

Additional worked example

A 3 kg object displaces 0.002 m³ of water while fully submerged. With g=10, find its initial net vertical force.

Weight is 30 N down; buoyancy is 20 N up; net force is 10 N down.

Common error. Buoyancy uses fluid density, while the object’s weight uses object mass.

Fluid flow and Bernoulli reasoning

Physical idea

For steady incompressible flow through a pipe, a narrower section has greater speed. Bernoulli’s equation relates pressure, speed, and height along a streamline when viscosity and pump losses are negligible.

First move

Check the flow assumptions first. Apply continuity to obtain speed, then Bernoulli along the same streamline. At the same height, increased speed corresponds to lower static pressure in this ideal model.

Worked reasoning

A pipe narrows from 4 cm² to 2 cm² while flow speed is 1 m/s upstream. Continuity gives 2 m/s downstream. At equal height, ideal pressure falls by ½ρ(2²−1²).

Additional worked example

An ideal horizontal pipe halves its cross-sectional area. What happens to speed?

It doubles because Av stays constant for steady incompressible flow.

Common error. Do not claim fast flow always means low pressure outside the assumptions and streamline comparison of Bernoulli’s equation.

Temperature, heat, phase change, and expansion

Physical idea

Temperature measures thermal state; heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. Sensible heating changes temperature. A phase change can absorb or release energy at nearly constant temperature.

First move

Separate each segment of a heating process: use mcΔT within a phase, mL at a phase boundary, and sum the energy transfers. For thermal expansion use the material’s expansion coefficient and original length.

Worked reasoning

Heating 0.5 kg of water by 10°C with c≈4200 J/(kg·°C) takes 0.5·4200·10=21,000 J, ignoring losses. Melting ice instead requires latent heat and cannot use this temperature-change step alone.

Additional worked example

How much heat raises 2 kg of material with c=500 J/(kg·°C) by 3°C?

Q=2·500·3=3000 J.

Common error. Do not use ΔT in °C as an absolute gas-law temperature; use kelvin there.

Ideal gases and molecular motion

Physical idea

The ideal gas law links pressure, volume, amount of gas, and absolute temperature. Its particle model works best when interactions and molecular volume are small relative to the container and mean separation.

First move

Convert temperature to kelvin. Keep n fixed only if gas does not enter or leave. For a two-state problem divide PV=nRT at each state to cancel constants that truly remain fixed.

Worked reasoning

A sealed rigid container warms from 300 K to 450 K. Since n and V are fixed, pressure rises by factor 450/300=1.5, not by the ratio of Celsius readings.

Additional worked example

A fixed amount of ideal gas at constant pressure warms from 200 K to 300 K. How does volume change?

Volume becomes 300/200=1.5 times its initial value.

Common error. A temperature ratio is meaningful in kelvin, not Celsius.

22

First law and thermal processes

Physical idea

The first law tracks internal energy: heat into the system raises U, while work done by the system lowers it. In a pressure-volume graph, signed area under the path is work by the gas.

First move

Declare the sign convention. For a constant-volume process, Wby=0. For an isothermal ideal-gas process, ΔU=0. For an adiabatic process, Q=0. Use the path to find work; endpoints alone are insufficient.

Worked reasoning

A gas receives 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work on its surroundings. Its internal energy increases by 300 J. If the same 200 J work were done on the gas, the sign of Wby would reverse.

Additional worked example

A gas loses 100 J of heat and 40 J of work is done on it. Find ΔU.

Q=−100 J and Wby=−40 J, so ΔU=−100−(−40)=−60 J.

Common error. Thermal energy moves as heat because of temperature difference; work and heat are transfers, not stored substances.

23

Charge, Coulomb force, and electric field

Physical idea

Charge is conserved. Like charges repel and unlike charges attract. An electric field is force per unit positive test charge, so its direction is defined independently of the sign of a charge later placed in it.

First move

Draw charges and displacement vectors. Add electric fields as vectors, then multiply the net field by the actual test charge to get force. Use the inverse-square distance between charge centers.

Worked reasoning

A +2 μC charge in a rightward field of 300 N/C experiences 6×10⁻⁴ N right. A −2 μC charge in the same field feels the same magnitude left.

Additional worked example

A −3 μC charge sits in a field of 200 N/C pointing east. Find force magnitude and direction.

Magnitude is 3×10⁻⁶·200=6×10⁻⁴ N, directed west.

Common error. Field direction follows the force on a positive test charge; a negative charge moves oppositely under electric force alone.

Electric potential and energy

Physical idea

Electric potential is energy per unit charge and is a scalar. Potential difference determines the change in electric potential energy of a charge. A negative charge reverses the sign of ΔU for a given ΔV.

First move

Keep voltage and energy distinct. Add potentials algebraically from multiple point charges, but add fields vectorially. Use energy conservation to connect electric potential change with kinetic change when no other work occurs.

Worked reasoning

A +2 μC charge moves through a +5 V potential change. ΔU=qΔV=+10 μJ, so its kinetic energy decreases by 10 μJ if electric force is the only force doing work.

Additional worked example

A −1 μC charge moves from 10 V to 4 V. Find ΔU.

ΔV=−6 V, so ΔU=(−1 μC)(−6 V)=+6 μJ.

Common error. Higher electric potential does not always mean higher potential energy for a negative charge.

25

Current, resistance, and electrical power

Physical idea

Current is charge flow rate. Resistance links voltage and current for an ohmic element under fixed conditions. Electrical power measures energy transfer per time; a resistor generally converts electrical energy to thermal energy.

First move

Use conventional current direction consistently. Determine whether voltage, current, or resistance is fixed before predicting power changes. Convert mA, kΩ, and hours to SI units before calculating energy.

Worked reasoning

A 6 Ω resistor across 12 V carries 2 A and dissipates P=12·2=24 W. In 10 s it transfers 240 J of energy.

Additional worked example

A 4 Ω resistor carries 3 A. Find voltage and power.

V=IR=12 V and P=I²R=36 W.

Common error. Increasing resistance lowers power at fixed voltage but raises power at fixed current.

26

Series and parallel circuit decisions

Physical idea

Series elements share current; parallel branches share potential difference. Junction current is conserved, and voltage changes around a closed loop sum to zero.

First move

Mark nodes and branches first. Collapse unmistakable series or parallel groups, then use V=IR. When a connection is not reducible, write Kirchhoff junction and loop equations instead of forcing a series-parallel label.

Worked reasoning

Two resistors 4 Ω and 6 Ω in series across 20 V have equivalent 10 Ω, current 2 A, and drops 8 V and 12 V. In parallel across 20 V, each sees 20 V and their currents differ.

Additional worked example

Two 6 Ω resistors are connected in parallel across 12 V. Find equivalent resistance and total current.

Rp=3 Ω and Itotal=12/3=4 A; each branch carries 2 A.

Common error. An ideal ammeter goes in series and has negligible resistance; an ideal voltmeter goes in parallel and has very high resistance.

Capacitors and RC transients

Physical idea

A capacitor stores separated charge and energy. For an RC circuit, the time constant sets the pace of exponential charging or discharging. After one time constant, charging has reached about 63% of its final voltage and discharging retains about 37%.

First move

Find equivalent capacitance with the rule opposite to resistance: parallel capacitances add; reciprocal values add in series. For a DC steady state, an ideal capacitor branch carries no current.

Worked reasoning

With R=2 kΩ and C=100 μF, τ=RC=0.20 s. Charging toward 10 V gives about 6.3 V across the capacitor after 0.20 s.

Additional worked example

Two capacitors, 2 μF and 3 μF, are in parallel. Find equivalent capacitance and charge at 10 V.

Ceq=5 μF; Q=CeqV=50 μC.

Common error. Do not apply the resistance series rule to capacitors; the combinations reverse.

Magnetic force on charges and wires

Physical idea

A magnetic field acts on moving charge or current perpendicular to both motion and field. Magnetic force on a single particle changes direction of motion but does no work because it is perpendicular to velocity.

First move

Use a right-hand rule for positive charge or conventional current; reverse force for negative charge. Apply the sine factor for the angle between velocity or wire and field.

Worked reasoning

A +1 μC charge moves at 1000 m/s perpendicular to a 0.5 T field. Force magnitude is 1×10⁻⁶·1000·0.5=5×10⁻⁴ N.

Additional worked example

A 0.20 m wire carries 3 A perpendicular to a 0.4 T field. Find force magnitude.

F=ILB=3·0.20·0.4=0.24 N.

Common error. A stationary electric charge feels no magnetic force from a static magnetic field.

Magnetic flux and induction

Physical idea

Changing magnetic flux through a loop induces an emf. The induced current, if a closed path exists, produces a field opposing the change in flux, not necessarily the original field itself.

First move

Define the surface normal to interpret angle φ. Compare initial and final flux, then divide by elapsed time and multiply by turns. Use Lenz’s law to determine direction only after identifying whether the flux is increasing or decreasing.

Worked reasoning

Flux through a 10-turn coil increases from 0.01 to 0.03 Wb per turn in 0.2 s. Induced emf magnitude is 10(0.02/0.2)=1 V.

Additional worked example

A 20-turn coil has flux per turn fall by 0.005 Wb in 0.1 s. Find emf magnitude.

|emf|=20·0.005/0.1=1 V.

Common error. A changing field is not the only way to change flux; changing area or orientation also works.

30

Wave speed, frequency, and energy transport

Physical idea

A wave transfers energy and disturbance without transporting the medium as a whole over the same distance. Frequency is set by the source; wavelength adjusts when speed changes in a new medium.

First move

Identify what oscillates and what travels. Use v=fλ with consistent units. Read amplitude as maximum displacement, wavelength as spatial repeat distance, and period as time for one cycle.

Worked reasoning

A wave with frequency 50 Hz travels at 200 m/s. Its wavelength is 200/50=4 m, and period is 1/50=0.02 s.

Additional worked example

A wave has wavelength 0.5 m and frequency 8 Hz. Find speed.

v=fλ=8·0.5=4 m/s.

Common error. When a wave enters a new medium, its source frequency usually stays fixed while speed and wavelength change.

31

Superposition and standing waves

Physical idea

When waves overlap, displacements add. Interference can be constructive or destructive. A standing wave forms from oppositely traveling waves and has stationary nodes and antinodes.

First move

For a string fixed at both ends, put nodes at endpoints, fit n half-wavelengths in length L, and use f=v/λ. Check boundary conditions; a tube closed at one end has a different harmonic series.

Worked reasoning

A 1 m string has wave speed 100 m/s. Its fundamental wavelength is 2L=2 m, so f1=50 Hz. The next allowed frequency is 100 Hz.

Additional worked example

For the same string, find the third harmonic wavelength.

λ3=2L/3=2/3 m.

Common error. Do not use the fixed-string harmonic rule for a tube closed at one end, where only odd harmonics occur in the ideal model.

32

Sound, Doppler shift, and wave behavior

Physical idea

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave and needs a medium. Relative motion between source and observer changes the frequency received. Reflection, diffraction, and interference depend on wavelength and geometry.

First move

For Doppler questions, first predict whether pitch should rise or fall. Then choose source and observer signs so approaching motion raises the calculated frequency. State that the displayed relation assumes speeds along the line of sight in still air.

Worked reasoning

A stationary source emits 500 Hz and an observer moves toward it at 10 m/s in air where v=340 m/s. Received frequency is 500(350/340)≈515 Hz.

Additional worked example

If a sound source approaches a stationary observer, should received frequency be above or below emitted frequency?

Above; wavefronts are compressed in front of the approaching source.

Common error. Do not use this sound-in-medium equation for light in vacuum.

33

Reflection, mirrors, and image signs

Physical idea

Reflection obeys equal incident and reflected angles measured from the normal. The thin mirror equation links focal length, object distance, and image distance. Magnification sign distinguishes upright from inverted images under a consistent convention.

First move

Sketch the mirror, principal axis, focal point, and object. Apply a stated sign convention; a concave mirror has positive f in the common real-is-positive convention. Check a numerical answer against a simple ray sketch.

Worked reasoning

For a concave mirror with f=10 cm and object at 30 cm, 1/di=1/10−1/30=1/15, so di=15 cm. Magnification is −15/30=−0.5: a smaller inverted real image.

Additional worked example

A plane mirror has an object 2 m in front. Where is its image?

2 m behind the mirror, upright and the same size; it is virtual.

Common error. The angle of incidence is measured from the normal, not from the mirror surface.

34

Refraction, lenses, and total internal reflection

Physical idea

Light changes speed and wavelength when crossing media, while frequency stays fixed. Snell’s law relates angles measured from the normal. A converging lens may make a real or virtual image depending on object position.

First move

Draw the normal and decide whether the ray bends toward it (higher refractive index) or away from it (lower index). For a lens, sketch principal rays before using the thin-lens equation and sign convention.

Worked reasoning

Light travels from n1=1 to n2=1.5 at 30° to the normal. sinθ2=(1/1.5)sin30°=1/3, so θ2≈19.5°, toward the normal.

Additional worked example

For light going from glass n=1.5 to air n=1, can total internal reflection occur?

Yes, above critical angle θc=arcsin(1/1.5)≈41.8°. It cannot occur in the reverse direction.

Common error. Total internal reflection requires travel from higher to lower refractive index and a sufficiently large incidence angle.

35

Interference, diffraction, and wave nature of light

Physical idea

Coherent light waves can reinforce or cancel. A double-slit bright fringe occurs when path difference is an integer number of wavelengths. Diffraction spreads a wave after an opening or edge, especially when its size is comparable to wavelength.

First move

Identify whether the setup is double-slit interference or single-aperture diffraction before choosing a condition. Keep slit separation d distinct from screen distance; small-angle geometry may be needed to convert angle to screen position.

Worked reasoning

For d=0.50 mm and λ=500 nm, first bright fringe satisfies sinθ=λ/d=0.001, so θ≈0.001 rad. Reducing d spreads the interference fringes farther apart.

Additional worked example

If wavelength increases while slit separation stays fixed, how does fringe spacing change?

It increases because the bright-fringe angle scales approximately with λ/d for small angles.

Common error. A diffraction minimum uses a different condition from a double-slit interference maximum.

36

Photons, atomic energy, and wave particle reasoning

Physical idea

Light has wave behavior but exchanges energy in photons. Electrons and other matter also have wavelengths. Discrete atomic energy levels produce or absorb photons whose energy equals the level difference.

First move

In a transition, compute |ΔE| and set it equal to hf. Emission moves to a lower energy state; absorption moves upward. For a particle wavelength use momentum p, not its kinetic energy directly.

Worked reasoning

A photon with frequency 6×10¹⁴ Hz has energy about (6.63×10⁻³⁴)(6×10¹⁴)=4.0×10⁻¹⁹ J. An atom emitting that photon loses the same energy.

Additional worked example

If photon wavelength doubles, what happens to its energy?

It halves because E=hc/λ.

Common error. Greater light intensity adds photons; it does not raise the energy of each photon if frequency is unchanged.

37

Nuclear reactions, decay, and mass energy

Physical idea

Nuclear identity follows proton number; isotopes differ in neutron count. Radioactive half-life describes the fraction of undecayed nuclei remaining. Mass differences in nuclear reactions correspond to large energy transfers.

First move

Balance mass number and charge number across a nuclear reaction. Count elapsed half-lives rather than subtracting a fixed number of nuclei. Distinguish alpha, beta, and gamma changes.

Worked reasoning

After three half-lives, an initial 80 g radioactive sample has 80·(1/2)³=10 g of that undecayed isotope. Daughter products account for the rest, ignoring other losses.

Additional worked example

A sample has 32 mg of an isotope and half-life 5 days. How much remains after 15 days?

Three half-lives pass, so 32/8=4 mg remains undecayed.

Common error. Half-life is a fixed time for halving, not a fixed amount lost each interval.
