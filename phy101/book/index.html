Represent and measure

01

Measurement and dimensional reasoning

When you see

A quantity is reported in unfamiliar units or a proposed equation looks suspicious.

Choose this model

Quantity with dimensions; idealized particle or system only after the units are clear.

Principle

An equation can add only quantities of the same dimension. A unit check can reject an equation but cannot establish that the physical model is true. Keep extra digits until the final reported answer.

First move

Cancel units explicitly, estimate the expected scale, and report precision supported by the given data.

Worked reasoning

72 km/h is 72/3.6 = 20 m/s. At constant speed for 5 s, the distance is 100 m.

Additional worked example

Convert 54 km/h to m/s, then find its constant-speed travel in 4 s.

15 m/s and 60 m. The conversion lowers the numerical speed because one metre per second is a larger unit than one kilometre per hour.

Important distinction. A dimensionally valid equation can still describe the wrong situation; state the physical condition before substitution.

02

Vectors, components, and products

When you see

Several displacements or forces point in different directions, or work and torque appear.

Choose this model

Vector with signed components in a declared coordinate system.

Principle

Vectors add component by component. The dot product selects the component parallel to a second vector; the cross product measures a perpendicular turning effect and has a direction.

First move

Draw the axes and angle first. Check the quadrant before taking an inverse tangent. Keep units attached to each component.

Worked reasoning

A 3 m east displacement followed by 4 m north has components (3,4) m and magnitude 5 m, directed about 53 degrees north of east.

Additional worked example

Combine a 5 N east force and a 12 N north force.

Components are (5,12) N; magnitude is 13 N. The direction is about 67 degrees north of east.

Important distinction. Adding magnitudes would give 17 N and ignore direction. A negative component is not a negative magnitude.

Motion of particles

03

Motion graphs and rates

When you see

The question gives position, velocity, or acceleration as a graph or function of time.

Choose this model

Particle; choose a graph slope for a rate and a signed area for accumulation.

Principle

The slope of a position graph is velocity. The slope of a velocity graph is acceleration. Signed area under velocity gives displacement; absolute area gives distance.

First move

Mark the time interval and axis units. For a piecewise graph, calculate each signed area before adding.

Worked reasoning

A velocity of +4 m/s for 3 s, then -2 m/s for 2 s, gives displacement 12-4 = 8 m and distance 12+4 = 16 m.

Additional worked example

Velocity is -3 m/s for 4 s. Find displacement and distance.

Displacement is -12 m; distance is 12 m. The sign records direction, not the amount of ground covered.

Important distinction. The area under a position-time graph is not generally a displacement.

04

Constant acceleration and free fall

When you see

Acceleration is constant over the entire interval, including ideal vertical motion near Earth.

Choose this model

Particle under constant acceleration.

Principle

These relationships follow from constant acceleration. For upward-positive free fall, a_y = -g even at the top of the path.

First move

Declare the positive axis, write initial and final states, and choose the relation that eliminates the unneeded variable.

Worked reasoning

A cart starts at 2 m/s and accelerates at 3 m/s² for 4 s. It ends at 14 m/s and moves 2(4)+0.5(3)(4²)=32 m.

Additional worked example

A ball is launched upward at 20 m/s. With g = 10 m/s², when does it first reach its highest point?

Set vertical velocity to zero: 0=20-10t, so t=2 s. Acceleration is still -10 m/s² at that instant.

Important distinction. Do not apply a constant-acceleration formula across intervals with changing acceleration.

05

Projectiles in two dimensions

When you see

A body moves after launch while gravity is the only appreciable force.

Choose this model

Particle with independent horizontal and vertical equations sharing one time.

Principle

Ideal projectile motion has horizontal acceleration zero and vertical acceleration -g. A zero vertical velocity at the peak does not make the total velocity zero.

First move

Split launch velocity into components. Solve the vertical condition for time, then use that same time horizontally.

Worked reasoning

A ball leaves a 20 m ledge horizontally at 5 m/s. With g=10 m/s², falling takes 2 s and the horizontal range is 10 m.

Additional worked example

From a 45 m ledge, a ball launches horizontally at 8 m/s. Use g=10 m/s². Find its flight time and range.

Vertical fall gives 45=5t², hence t=3 s. Range is 8(3)=24 m.

Important distinction. The familiar equal-height range formula does not apply when launch and landing heights differ.

06

Relative motion

When you see

The velocity of one object is described from a moving vehicle, river, or observer.

Choose this model

Particle velocities with frame labels; add vectors, not just speeds.

Principle

Relative velocity is a difference between observations in two frames. Frame labels make the chaining rule unambiguous.

First move

Name the moving object and each observer. Draw component arrows before solving for magnitude and heading.

Worked reasoning

A boat moves 4 m/s east relative to water, and water moves 3 m/s north relative to shore. The boat moves 5 m/s relative to shore, northeast.

Additional worked example

A walker moves 2 m/s east on a train moving 10 m/s east relative to ground. Find walker speed relative to ground.

12 m/s east. The two relative velocities add because their directions agree.

Important distinction. A velocity without its reference frame is incomplete.

Forces and constraints

07

Free-body diagrams and Newton laws

When you see

The target is an acceleration, contact force, or equilibrium condition.

Choose this model

One isolated body with only real external forces; particle under net force.

Principle

A net force changes velocity. Zero net force means zero acceleration, not necessarily zero velocity. Third-law partners act on different bodies.

First move

Choose one body, draw gravity and each contact or applied force, then resolve the sum on chosen axes.

Worked reasoning

A 4 kg cart has 18 N right and 6 N left. Its net force is 12 N right and its acceleration is 3 m/s² right.

Additional worked example

A 5 kg object has 25 N east and 10 N west. Find its acceleration.

Net force is 15 N east, so acceleration is 3 m/s² east.

Important distinction. Do not place an equal-and-opposite third-law partner on the same free-body diagram.

08

Friction and inclined surfaces

When you see

A body touches a rough surface or moves along a slope.

Choose this model

One body with normal force and friction; decide rest versus sliding first.

Principle

Static friction adjusts up to a maximum; kinetic friction has approximate magnitude μkN while surfaces slide. The normal force is set by perpendicular force balance, not by a universal N=mg rule.

First move

Choose axes parallel and perpendicular to the slope. Use the impending-motion test before selecting a friction law.

Worked reasoning

For a 2 kg block sliding down a 30-degree slope with μk=0.20 and g=10, N≈17.3 N, downhill gravity is 10 N, friction is 3.46 N uphill, and a≈3.27 m/s² downhill.

Additional worked example

A 3 kg block rests on a horizontal floor with μs=0.5 and g=10. Can static friction hold against a 12 N horizontal pull?

Yes. N=30 N and the maximum static friction is 15 N, but actual static friction is 12 N, opposite the pull.

Important distinction. Setting static friction equal to μsN in every stationary problem overstates it.

09

Circular motion and inward force

When you see

A trajectory bends, even when speed stays constant.

Choose this model

Particle with radial acceleration; identify the real force that supplies net inward force.

Principle

Centripetal acceleration is a direction of net acceleration, not an additional force. Tangential acceleration changes speed; radial acceleration changes direction.

First move

Point the radial axis toward the center. Resolve gravity, tension, friction, and normal force along it.

Worked reasoning

A 2 kg mass at 4 m/s on a 2 m radius needs 16 N net inward force.

Additional worked example

At twice the speed and the same radius and mass, how does required inward force change?

It becomes four times larger because mv²/r depends on speed squared.

Important distinction. A constant speed on a curved path is accelerated motion.

10

Resistive force and terminal speed

When you see

Drag grows with speed and eventually balances a driving force.

Choose this model

Particle with a velocity-dependent force; terminal state has zero net force.

Principle

Drag opposes relative motion through a fluid. Linear and quadratic forms describe different regimes and cannot be interchanged without evidence.

First move

State the drag law. Balance weight and drag only at terminal speed; during approach to that speed acceleration remains nonzero.

Worked reasoning

For a 1 kg falling body with linear drag b=2 N·s/m and g=10, mg=bvt gives vt=5 m/s.

Additional worked example

For linear drag with m=2 kg, b=4 N·s/m, and g=10, find terminal speed.

vt=mg/b=20/4=5 m/s. This is a limiting speed, not the initial speed.

Important distinction. Zero acceleration at terminal speed does not mean that gravity has disappeared.

Work and energy

11

Work by constant and changing forces

When you see

A force acts over a displacement, possibly with a changing magnitude.

Choose this model

System energy transfer by work; integrate only the force component along motion.

Principle

Work is positive, negative, or zero according to the angle between force and displacement. Net work changes kinetic energy.

First move

Choose a system. For constant force use Fd cosφ; for changing force take signed area under F versus displacement.

Worked reasoning

Stretching a spring from 0 to 0.20 m with k=100 N/m stores 0.5kx²=2 J, supplied by 2 J of external quasistatic work.

Additional worked example

A net 12 N force acts along 3 m of motion. How much does kinetic energy change?

ΔK=Wnet=12(3)=36 J. Direction alignment is assumed.

Important distinction. A force perpendicular to instantaneous motion does zero instantaneous work despite changing direction.

12

System choice and energy conservation

When you see

A height, spring compression, or speed changes without a need to find elapsed time.

Choose this model

System energy accounting; isolated for energy only when transfer across its boundary is negligible.

Principle

Energy is conserved overall. Mechanical energy K+U is constant only when nonconservative transfer and other exchange are absent. Potential energy belongs to an interacting system.

First move

Draw initial and final states, choose the system boundary, list transfers, then solve symbolically before inserting numbers.

Worked reasoning

A body released from rest drops 5 m with negligible drag. With g=10, mgh=0.5mv² gives v=10 m/s, independent of mass.

Additional worked example

A body falls 1.8 m from rest with g=10 and negligible drag. Find speed.

v=sqrt(2gh)=sqrt(36)=6 m/s. If drag matters, the mechanical-energy result overpredicts speed.

Important distinction. Do not call mechanical energy conserved when friction converts part of it to internal energy.

13

Power and efficiency

When you see

The question asks how fast energy is transferred or how much input is useful.

Choose this model

Rate of work or energy flow across the chosen system boundary.

Principle

Power is a rate, not an amount of energy. At constant speed the driving power balances dissipative energy transfer at that instant.

First move

Compute output and input over the same interval. Separate joules from watts and state whether force and velocity are aligned.

Worked reasoning

A motor exerts 100 N along a vehicle moving at 3 m/s, so the instantaneous mechanical power is 300 W.

Additional worked example

A device receives 500 J and delivers 350 J of useful work. Find its efficiency.

η=350/500=0.70, or 70%. The remaining 150 J is transferred in other forms.

Important distinction. A force magnitude times speed is not the power if the force is oblique; use the dot product.

Momentum and systems

14

Momentum and impulse

When you see

A force acts briefly or an object rebounds.

Choose this model

Particle or system with momentum change caused by external impulse.

Principle

Impulse is the area under force versus time. It changes vector momentum. A short interaction can produce large average force without a large time interval.

First move

Use signed initial and final velocities. Compute Δp first, then divide by duration for average force if needed.

Worked reasoning

A 0.5 kg ball changes from +6 to -2 m/s. Its impulse is 0.5(-2-6)=-4 N·s.

Additional worked example

If that reversal lasts 0.020 s, what is average force on the ball?

Favg=J/Δt=-4/0.020=-200 N. The negative sign gives direction.

Important distinction. A bounce reverses momentum direction; subtract signed values instead of subtracting speeds.

15

Collisions and center of mass

When you see

Bodies interact strongly with each other over a short time.

Choose this model

Multi-body system with negligible external impulse; kinetic energy requires its own test.

Principle

Internal collision forces cancel in system momentum accounting. Elastic collisions conserve kinetic energy too; bodies that stick generally do not.

First move

Draw before and after velocity arrows. Conserve x and y momentum separately, then test energy only if the collision type warrants it.

Worked reasoning

A 1 kg cart at +4 m/s sticks to a stationary 3 kg cart. Shared velocity is +1 m/s. Kinetic energy falls from 8 J to 2 J.

Additional worked example

A 1 kg mass is at x=0 and a 3 kg mass at x=4 m. Find the center of mass.

xcm=(1·0+3·4)/4=3 m, closer to the heavier mass.

Important distinction. Momentum conservation is a system claim; it fails if external impulse during the event is substantial.

16

Variable-mass motion

When you see

A rocket ejects mass while gaining speed.

Choose this model

Rocket plus exhaust system; compare momentum before and after a short ejection.

Principle

In the ideal rocket model, expelled mass carries momentum opposite the rocket acceleration. The formula assumes constant exhaust speed relative to the rocket and negligible external force over the burn.

First move

Identify initial and final rocket mass, distinguish exhaust speed from rocket speed, and check that mi>mf produces positive speed gain.

Worked reasoning

If a rocket halves its mass and ejects gas at 2 km/s relative to itself, ideal speed gain is 2 ln2≈1.39 km/s.

Additional worked example

If the same ideal rocket reduces mass from 4 units to 1 unit, express its speed gain in terms of ve.

Δv=ve ln4≈1.386ve. A fourfold mass ratio does not yield four times the exhaust speed.

Important distinction. Do not treat mass as constant in F=ma while fuel is being expelled across the system boundary.

Rotation and equilibrium

17

Angular kinematics

When you see

A wheel turns and angular acceleration is given.

Choose this model

Rigid body rotating about a fixed axis.

Principle

Angular position, velocity, and acceleration parallel linear kinematics only when angular acceleration is constant. Points on a rigid wheel share ω but have different linear speeds.

First move

Work in radians, choose positive rotation, and use r to connect angular and tangential quantities.

Worked reasoning

Starting at 2 rad/s with α=3 rad/s² for 4 s, a wheel reaches 14 rad/s and turns through 32 rad.

Additional worked example

At ω=6 rad/s, what is tangential speed at r=0.50 m?

vt=rω=3 m/s. A point at twice the radius would have twice this speed.

Important distinction. Do not put degrees directly into equations derived using radians.

18

Torque and rotational inertia

When you see

An off-center force changes a rigid body’s rotation.

Choose this model

Rigid object under net torque about a specified fixed axis.

Principle

Torque depends on perpendicular lever arm. Moment of inertia depends on how mass is distributed relative to the axis, not just total mass.

First move

Mark the axis, determine each torque sign, and compute the relevant I. The torque equation is about the chosen fixed axis.

Worked reasoning

For a uniform disk with M=2 kg and R=0.50 m, I=0.5MR²=0.25 kg·m². A 1 N·m net torque gives α=4 rad/s².

Additional worked example

What torque is needed to accelerate an I=0.40 kg·m² wheel at 5 rad/s²?

τnet=Iα=2 N·m.

Important distinction. A force directed along the radius has zero torque about the center even if its magnitude is large.

19

Rolling and rotational energy

When you see

A wheel or ball both translates and rotates without slipping.

Choose this model

Rigid object with translation plus rotation; contact point instantaneously at rest relative to ground.

Principle

No-slip rolling links center-of-mass speed to angular speed. Static friction may enable rolling while doing no work at an ideal stationary contact point.

First move

Write both energy terms and the no-slip constraint. Do not replace a rotating body with a particle if its spin energy matters.

Worked reasoning

A uniform disk rolls from rest through a vertical drop h without dissipative loss. With I=0.5MR², Mgh=0.75Mv², so v=sqrt(4gh/3).

Additional worked example

For h=0.30 m and g=10 m/s², find the disk speed.

v=sqrt(4·10·0.30/3)=2 m/s. A frictionless sliding particle would reach sqrt(6) m/s.

Important distinction. The relation v=Rω fails for a wheel that slips.

20

Angular momentum

When you see

Rotation changes as mass moves inward, or an external torque acts for a time.

Choose this model

Rotating system; external torque changes angular momentum about the same axis.

Principle

Angular momentum is conserved when external torque about the selected axis is negligible. A changing moment of inertia can therefore change angular speed.

First move

State the axis and system before using Iiωi=Ifωf. Do not transfer a scalar relation to a problem with changing rotation direction.

Worked reasoning

A skater has I=4 kg·m² and ω=2 rad/s. Pulling arms inward to I=2 kg·m² gives ω=4 rad/s when external torque is negligible.

Additional worked example

If I falls from 6 to 3 kg·m² at initial ω=1 rad/s, find final ω.

ωf=(6/3)(1)=2 rad/s. Rotational kinetic energy rises because the skater does internal work.

Important distinction. Angular momentum can stay constant while rotational kinetic energy changes.

21

Rigid-body equilibrium

When you see

A beam, sign, or ladder remains at rest.

Choose this model

Rigid object with both translational and rotational equilibrium.

Principle

Zero net force prevents translational acceleration; zero net torque prevents angular acceleration. Both are needed for static equilibrium.

First move

Draw every external force and its application point. Choose a pivot that removes unknown forces from the torque equation.

Worked reasoning

A 20 N downward load 2 m right of a pivot is balanced by a 10 N downward load 4 m left: opposite torque magnitudes are each 40 N·m.

Additional worked example

A 30 N load acts 1 m right of a pivot. What downward load 2 m left balances its torque?

15 N because 30(1)=F(2). Force balance still determines pivot reaction.

Important distinction. Balancing torques alone does not guarantee zero net force.

22

Elasticity of solids

When you see

A rod stretches slightly under load.

Choose this model

Linear elastic material within its proportional limit.

Principle

Young modulus links tensile stress to strain. The extension grows with force and initial length but falls as area or modulus increases.

First move

Check that deformation is small and reversible; use the original cross-sectional area and length.

Worked reasoning

A 2 m rod with A=1.0×10⁻⁴ m², Y=2.0×10¹¹ Pa, and F=100 N stretches ΔL=FL/(AY)=1.0×10⁻⁵ m.

Additional worked example

Within the elastic range, what happens to extension when force doubles?

Extension doubles. The material and geometry must remain unchanged.

Important distinction. A modulus is a material property; a spring constant also depends on object geometry.

Gravity and fluids

23

Gravitation and orbital motion

When you see

A body is far from Earth’s surface or moves in an ideal circular orbit.

Choose this model

Two-body gravitational interaction; circular orbit needs inward net force.

Principle

Gravity weakens with center-to-center distance squared. In an ideal circular orbit it supplies the inward force; an orbiting satellite is continuously falling.

First move

Use distance from the attracting body’s center. Match energy reference U=0 at infinite separation, and check whether a circular-orbit assumption is justified.

Worked reasoning

At twice Earth-center distance, gravitational field magnitude is one-quarter as large, ignoring other bodies.

Additional worked example

If circular-orbit radius grows by a factor of 4, how does orbital speed change?

It halves because v scales as 1/sqrt(r) for the same central mass.

Important distinction. Altitude above a surface is not the r in the inverse-square law; add the planet radius.

24

Pressure and buoyancy

When you see

A submerged body or a pressure measurement at depth is given.

Choose this model

Static fluid with pressure increasing downward and upward buoyant resultant.

Principle

Hydrostatic pressure depends on depth, fluid density, and surface pressure, not container shape. Buoyant force equals weight of displaced fluid.

First move

Separate gauge from absolute pressure. For a floating body, balance buoyancy with weight; for sinking or tethering, include all forces.

Worked reasoning

At 5 m depth in water with ρ=1000 kg/m³ and g=10, gauge pressure is 50 kPa. A 0.002 m³ fully displaced volume gives 20 N buoyancy.

Additional worked example

A 1 kg object of volume 0.002 m³ floats in water with g=10. What fraction of its volume is submerged?

Weight is 10 N, so displaced volume is 10/(1000·10)=0.001 m³, half its volume.

Important distinction. Buoyancy uses displaced fluid volume, which equals total object volume only when fully submerged.

25

Flow and Bernoulli conditions

When you see

Fluid moves through a pipe with changing area or height.

Choose this model

Steady incompressible flow; Bernoulli additionally assumes negligible viscous loss along a streamline.

Principle

Continuity is volume-flow conservation for incompressible flow. Bernoulli accounts for pressure, kinetic, and gravitational energy per unit volume under stated conditions.

First move

Solve flow speeds from continuity first. Then compare points on one streamline with consistent height reference.

Worked reasoning

In a horizontal pipe, area halves from 4 to 2 cm² and speed doubles from 1 to 2 m/s. For ideal water, pressure drops by 0.5(1000)(4-1)=1500 Pa.

Additional worked example

A pipe narrows to one-quarter its original area. If initial speed is 0.5 m/s, find new speed for steady incompressible flow.

Speed becomes 2 m/s. The result does not alone determine pressure if elevation or losses are unknown.

Important distinction. Do not use ideal Bernoulli across a pump, substantial frictional loss, or unrelated streamlines without extra terms.

Oscillations and waves

26

Simple harmonic motion

When you see

A restoring force is proportional to displacement from stable equilibrium.

Choose this model

Particle in simple harmonic motion; displacement measured from equilibrium.

Principle

Acceleration points toward equilibrium and is largest at the turning points. Speed is greatest at equilibrium, where spring potential energy is least.

First move

Use amplitude A as maximum displacement. Keep ordinary frequency f=1/T distinct from angular frequency ω=2πf.

Worked reasoning

For k=100 N/m, m=1 kg, and A=0.10 m, ω=10 rad/s, T≈0.628 s, and vmax=ωA=1 m/s.

Additional worked example

At the positive turning point of this motion, what are speed and acceleration?

Speed is zero and acceleration is -ω²A=-10 m/s² toward equilibrium.

Important distinction. At a turning point, zero speed does not imply zero acceleration.

27

Pendulum, damping, and driving

When you see

A small-angle pendulum swings, or oscillations lose energy or receive periodic driving.

Choose this model

Small-angle oscillator; driving and damping alter amplitude response.

Principle

For small swings, the pendulum period depends mainly on length and g, not bob mass. Damping removes energy; near resonance a drive can transfer energy efficiently.

First move

Check that the angle is small, then compare period using a square-root ratio. For driven motion, distinguish natural frequency from drive frequency.

Worked reasoning

Doubling a small-angle pendulum’s length multiplies its period by sqrt(2), not by 2.

Additional worked example

For L≈1 m and g≈9.8 m/s², estimate the small-angle period.

T≈2πsqrt(1/9.8)≈2.0 s.

Important distinction. The simple pendulum formula is less accurate for large amplitudes.

28

Traveling waves and sound

When you see

A disturbance transports energy through a medium.

Choose this model

Traveling-wave model; material points oscillate while the pattern propagates.

Principle

Wave speed is set by medium properties for a given mode of propagation. Frequency comes from the source; wavelength adjusts when speed changes. Sound in air is longitudinal.

First move

Identify frequency, wavelength, and medium. Do not confuse particle speed with the propagation speed of the wave pattern.

Worked reasoning

A string with tension 80 N and linear density 0.20 kg/m has wave speed sqrt(80/0.20)=20 m/s. A 50 Hz wave on it has wavelength 0.40 m.

Additional worked example

A 25 Hz disturbance travels at 10 m/s. Find wavelength.

λ=v/f=0.40 m.

Important distinction. A wave may carry energy while the medium has no net transport along the full path.

29

Interference and standing waves

When you see

Waves overlap, reflect from a boundary, or resonate in a string or air column.

Choose this model

Superposition of waves with boundary conditions.

Principle

At a node, displacement is always zero; at an antinode, amplitude is largest. Allowed wavelengths are fixed by boundary conditions, not arbitrary harmonic labels.

First move

Sketch nodes and antinodes first. Count half-wavelengths along a string fixed at both ends before choosing n.

Worked reasoning

For a 1 m fixed string with wave speed 100 m/s, fundamental frequency is 50 Hz and second harmonic is 100 Hz.

Additional worked example

What is the third-harmonic frequency of the same string?

150 Hz because f3=3f1. The standing pattern contains three half-wavelengths.

Important distinction. An air column closed at one end has different allowed harmonics; do not import the fixed-string formula unchanged.

Thermal physics

30

Temperature and ideal gas states

When you see

Pressure, volume, or temperature of a dilute gas changes.

Choose this model

Ideal gas in equilibrium; temperature must be absolute.

Principle

The gas law relates equilibrium states of a fixed amount of ideal gas. Pressure at fixed volume and mole number scales with kelvin temperature.

First move

Write two states. Use kelvins and keep n constant only if no gas enters or leaves.

Worked reasoning

Heating a sealed rigid vessel from 300 K to 450 K increases ideal-gas pressure by a factor 450/300=1.5.

Additional worked example

A rigid vessel at 200 kPa and 300 K reaches 600 K. Find final pressure if gas amount stays fixed.

400 kPa because P2/P1=T2/T1=2.

Important distinction. Doubling Celsius temperature does not generally double ideal-gas pressure.

31

Thermal energy and calorimetry

When you see

Bodies exchange heat and their temperatures change or a phase transition occurs.

Choose this model

Thermal system; energy transferred by temperature difference.

Principle

Specific heat governs temperature change within one phase. During an ideal phase change at fixed pressure, latent heat changes phase without changing temperature.

First move

Choose the system, set heat lost plus heat gained to zero if insulated, and treat each heating or phase segment separately.

Worked reasoning

Heating 0.50 kg of water by 10 K with c=4200 J/(kg·K) requires Q=0.50·4200·10=21 kJ.

Additional worked example

How much heat is needed to warm 0.20 kg of the same water by 5 K?

Q=0.20·4200·5=4.2 kJ, assuming no phase change or loss.

Important distinction. Use a temperature difference in K or °C, but use kelvins for absolute gas temperatures and entropy formulas.

32

First law and gas work

When you see

Heat enters or leaves a gas while its volume may change.

Choose this model

Thermodynamic system with a declared work sign convention.

Principle

Internal energy changes through heat added and work done by the system. Expansion usually gives positive work by the gas; compression gives negative work by the gas.

First move

State whether W means work by or on the gas. On a P-V graph, signed area under the path is work by the gas.

Worked reasoning

A gas receives 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work. Its internal energy rises by 300 J.

Additional worked example

An adiabatic gas is compressed by surroundings doing 150 J of work on it. Find ΔU.

Q=0 and Wby=-150 J, hence ΔU=0-(-150)=+150 J.

Important distinction. For a cyclic process, ΔU=0 over the whole cycle, though heat and work need not each be zero.

33

Microscopic gas energy

When you see

The question relates temperature to molecular motion or heat capacity.

Choose this model

Classical monatomic ideal gas, with translational degrees of freedom.

Principle

Absolute temperature measures average molecular translational kinetic energy. Internal energy of a monatomic ideal gas depends only on temperature, not separately on pressure or volume.

First move

Check the gas type and model. For a temperature change use ΔU=(3/2)nRΔT.

Worked reasoning

One mole of monatomic ideal gas warmed by 100 K gains about 1.5(8.314)(100)=1.25 kJ of internal energy.

Additional worked example

If two moles of this gas warm by 50 K, what is ΔU?

ΔU=(3/2)(2)(8.314)(50)≈1.25 kJ.

Important distinction. Do not apply the monatomic 3/2 factor to every gas without checking active degrees of freedom.

34

Heat engines and the Carnot limit

When you see

Heat flows between hot and cold reservoirs, sometimes producing work.

Choose this model

Cyclic engine with energy accounting and second-law limit.

Principle

A heat engine cannot turn all intake heat into work while operating cyclically between two reservoirs. Carnot efficiency is the upper limit for reservoir temperatures in kelvins.

First move

Draw heat-input, work-output, and rejected-heat arrows. First use Qh=W+Qc, then compare actual efficiency with the Carnot bound.

Worked reasoning

An engine takes 1000 J and rejects 600 J each cycle. It delivers 400 J and has 40% efficiency. At Th=500 K and Tc=300 K, 40% is the Carnot ceiling.

Additional worked example

If an engine takes 800 J and produces 200 J of work, how much heat does it reject and what is efficiency?

Qc=800-200=600 J and η=200/800=25%.

Important distinction. Carnot efficiency is a maximum under specified reservoir temperatures, not the expected efficiency of every actual engine.

Entropy and refrigeration

When you see

Heat flows between reservoirs or a device moves heat from cold to hot.

Choose this model

Thermal system plus surroundings; assess entropy of the combined isolated whole.

Principle

Entropy can decrease in one part while total entropy of an isolated system does not decrease. A refrigerator requires work to extract heat from a cold region and release still more heat to a warm region.

First move

Track positive heat magnitudes and the work input. For a reversible isothermal heat transfer, use ΔS=Qrev/T with kelvin temperature; for an irreversible process, evaluate entropy change along a reversible reference path.

Worked reasoning

A refrigerator removes 300 J from its cold compartment using 100 J of work. It rejects 400 J to the room and has cold-side COP=300/100=3.

Additional worked example

A refrigerator removes 500 J using 200 J of work. Find heat rejected and cold-side COP.

Heat rejected is 700 J by energy balance; COPc=500/200=2.5.

Important distinction. COP can exceed one because it counts heat moved, not work converted into heat; it is not an engine efficiency.
