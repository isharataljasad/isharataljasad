PHY 101 · TOPIC 01 OF 8

Units and measurement

Physics is measured quantities with units. Convert carefully, check dimensions, and report results with sensible precision.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Definitions

SI base units (mechanics)
metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s). Others include the kelvin (K), mole (mol) and ampere (A).
Derived unit
A combination of base units, e.g. newton N = kg·m/s2, joule J = N·m = kg·m2/s2, watt W = J/s, pascal Pa = N/m2.
Conversion factor
A ratio equal to 1, such as 1000 m/1 km, used to change units without changing the quantity.
Dimension
The type of a quantity: length [L], mass [M], time [T]. Speed has dimensions [L]/[T].
Significant figures
The digits that carry meaning about precision: all non-zero digits, zeros between them, and trailing zeros after a decimal point. Leading zeros (0.004) are not significant.
Accuracy and precision
Accuracy: closeness to the true value. Precision: closeness of repeated measurements to each other (and the fineness of the scale).
Symbols, meanings and units
SymbolMeaningUnit
m, kg, smetre, kilogram, secondSI base units
Nnewton, unit of forcekg·m/s²
Jjoule, unit of energy and workN·m = kg·m²/s²

Formulas and their conditions

Speed conversion

1 m/s = 3.6 km/h; v(m/s) = v(km/h) / 3.6

Conditions and limits: Exact, since 1 km = 1000 m and 1 h = 3600 s.

Area and volume conversions

1 m2 = 104 cm2; 1 m3 = 106 cm3 = 103 L; 1 mL = 1 cm3

Conditions and limits: Square or cube the length factor.

Density

ρ = m / V

Conditions and limits: For a uniform material. 1 g/cm3 = 1000 kg/m3 (water ≈ 1.00 g/cm³ near room temperature).

Dimensional consistency

[left side] = [right side]; only like dimensions can be added

Conditions and limits: Always required. Consistency does not prove an equation correct (pure-number factors such as 1/2 are invisible), but inconsistency proves it wrong.

Rounding rules

× ÷ : fewest significant figures; + − : fewest decimal places

Conditions and limits: Apply to the final answer. Exact numbers (counted objects, defined conversions) do not limit precision.

Why 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h

Convert 1 m/s to km/h with conversion factors.

  1. 1 m / s × 1 km / 1000 m × 3600 s / 1 h.

    Arrange each factor so m and s cancel.

  2. = 3600 / 1000 km / h = 3.6 km/h.

    Multiply the numbers; the units left are km/h.

So to go from km/h to m/s divide by 3.6: 90 km/h = 25 m/s; 36 km/h = 10 m/s.

Worked example

Speed conversion

Problem. Convert 36 km/h to m/s.

  1. 36 km / h × 1000 m / 1 km × 1 h / 3600 s.

    km and h cancel.

  2. = 36 000 / 3600 m/s = 10 m/s.

    Multiply the numbers.

Result: 36 km/h = 10 m/s.

What it means: Quick check: divide by 3.6.

Scope

  • Uncertainty propagation beyond significant-figure rules (standard deviations, combined uncertainties) is introduced in the laboratory topic and developed in laboratory courses.

01

Measurement and dimensional reasoning

When you see

A quantity is reported in unfamiliar units or a proposed equation looks suspicious.

Choose this model

Quantity with dimensions; idealized particle or system only after the units are clear.

Principle

An equation can add only quantities of the same dimension. A unit check can reject an equation but cannot establish that the physical model is true. Keep extra digits until the final reported answer.

First move

Cancel units explicitly, estimate the expected scale, and report precision supported by the given data.

Worked reasoning

72 km/h is 72/3.6 = 20 m/s. At constant speed for 5 s, the distance is 100 m.

Additional worked example

Convert 54 km/h to m/s, then find its constant-speed travel in 4 s.

15 m/s and 60 m. The conversion lowers the numerical speed because one metre per second is a larger unit than one kilometre per hour.

Important distinction. A dimensionally valid equation can still describe the wrong situation; state the physical condition before substitution.

PHY 101 · TOPIC 02 OF 8

Vectors and motion

Describe motion with position, velocity and acceleration as vectors; with constant acceleration, four equations predict everything, and horizontal and vertical motions are independent.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Definitions

Position and displacement
x is location relative to a chosen origin; displacement Δx = xf − xi (vector).
Average velocity
vavg = Δx / Δt; average speed = total distance/time (scalar, never negative).
Instantaneous velocity
v = dx / dt, the slope of the x–t graph.
Acceleration
a = dv / dt, the slope of the v–t graph. Units m/s².
Free fall
Motion under gravity alone: acceleration g = 9.8 m/s² downward, independent of mass (air resistance neglected).
Projectile
An object moving in two dimensions under gravity alone: constant horizontal velocity, constant downward acceleration g.
Symbols, meanings and units
SymbolMeaningUnit
x, yposition coordinatesm

aacceleration (signed, constant here)m/s²
ttime elapseds
gmagnitude of free-fall acceleration, 9.8 m/s² (9.81 in some texts)m/s²

Formulas and their conditions

Constant-acceleration equations

v = v0 + at; Δx = v0t + 1 / 2at2; v2 = v02 + 2aΔx; Δx = v0 + v / 2t

Conditions and limits: Only when a is constant. All quantities signed relative to one chosen positive direction.

Vector components

Ax = A cos θ, Ay = A sin θ; A = √Ax2 + Ay2

Conditions and limits: θ measured from the +x axis. If θ is measured from another direction, sin and cos swap — draw the triangle.

Projectile motion

x = v0 cos θ · t; y = v0 sin θ · t − 1 / 2gt2; vy = v0 sin θ − gt

Conditions and limits: No air resistance; up positive; launch from the origin.

Level-ground results

time of flight T = 2v0 sin θ / g; range R = v02 sin 2θ / g; max height H = (v0 sin θ)2 / 2g

Conditions and limits: Only when launch and landing heights are equal. Otherwise solve the y-equation for t.

Relative velocity

vA relative to C = vA relative to B + vB relative to C

Conditions and limits: Vector addition (Galilean relativity, speeds much less than light).

Where Δx = v₀t + ½at² comes from

For constant a, the velocity–time graph is a straight line from v0 to v = v0 + at.

  1. Displacement = area under the v–t graph.

    Area of (velocity × time) has units of metres.

  2. The area is a rectangle v0t plus a triangle 1 / 2t(v − v0) = 1 / 2t(at).

    Split the trapezium.

  3. Δx = v0t + 1 / 2at2.

    Add the two parts.

  4. Eliminating t with t = (v − v0)/a gives v2 = v02 + 2aΔx.

    Useful when time is not given.

In calculus terms, the equations come from integrating a = dv/dt and v = dx/dt with a constant.

Worked example

Speed after constant acceleration

Problem. A cart starts from rest and accelerates at 3 m/s2 for 4 s. Find its final speed and the distance travelled.

  1. v0 = 0, a = 3 m/s2, t = 4 s.

    List the knowns.

  2. v = v0 + at = 0 + 3 × 4 = 12 m/s.

    Equation containing v, v₀, a, t.

  3. Δx = v0t + 1 / 2at2 = 0 + 1 / 2(3)(16) = 24 m.

    Equation containing Δx.

Result: v = 12 m/s; Δx = 24 m.

What it means: Check with the average velocity: (0 + 12)/2 × 4 = 24 m.

Scope

  • Air resistance is neglected throughout; it is discussed qualitatively in the collection notes (terminal speed).
  • g is taken as 9.8 m/s²; use your course’s value (9.81 m/s²) if required — answers change slightly.

Relative motion and reference frames

A speed is incomplete until you say what it is measured relative to. A person can walk towards the back of a moving train while still moving forwards relative to the platform. Naming the observer removes the apparent contradiction.

Write vA/B for the velocity of object A measured by observer B. Keep one set of axis directions when combining velocities. In one dimension the signs carry direction; in two dimensions add the x-components and y-components separately. Speeds, which are magnitudes, cannot generally be added as scalars.

Position vectors satisfy rA/C = rA/B + rB/C: go from C to B, then from B to A. Differentiating with respect to the same time gives vA/C = vA/B + vB/C. The middle observer B connects the two terms. This is the classical, nonrelativistic rule, valid for speeds far below the speed of light and axes that do not rotate relative to each other.

For two objects observed from the ground G, vA/B = vA/G − vB/G. Reversing the observer reverses the vector: vB/A = −vA/B. If the frames move at constant velocity relative to one another, their measured accelerations agree. An accelerating or rotating frame needs further care when applying Newton’s laws.

For a river or wind problem, distinguish the vehicle’s velocity relative to the fluid from the fluid’s velocity relative to the ground. A boat’s heading is the direction it points through the water; its track is the path seen from the bank. They differ when a current has a sideways component.

Formulas and conditions

Add velocities through a named frame

vA/C = vA/B + vB/C

Conditions and limits: Vectors expressed using consistent axes and the same time; classical speeds.

Observe A from B

vA/B = vA/G − vB/G

Conditions and limits: Both given velocities are measured from the same frame G.

Point straight across a uniform river

t = w/u; downstream drift = ct

Conditions and limits: The boat points perpendicular to the banks. Current is parallel to the banks; u and c are constant.

Land directly opposite

sin α = c/u; vacross = √u2 − c2; t = w / √u2 − c2

Conditions and limits: Aim upstream by α from straight across. A positive crossing speed requires u > c. At u = c, cancellation uses the whole boat speed and the boat cannot cross.

Worked examples for this background

Walking backwards on a moving train

Problem. A train moves east at 12 m/s. A passenger walks west along it at 1.5 m/s relative to the train. Find the passenger’s velocity relative to the platform.

  1. Choose east positive. vP/T = −1.5 m/s and vT/G = +12 m/s.

    The westward walking velocity must have the opposite sign.

  2. vP/G = −1.5 + 12 = +10.5 m/s.

    The train frame cancels between the two terms.

Result: The passenger moves east at 10.5 m/s relative to the platform.

What it means: Walking towards the back of the train does not necessarily mean moving west over the ground.

A faster vehicle approaches from behind

Problem. Car A is 180 m behind car B. Both move east at constant speeds: A at 24 m/s and B at 18 m/s. Find when A reaches B.

  1. vA/B = 24 − 18 = 6 m/s east.

    The gap closes at the relative speed, not at either ground speed.

  2. t = 180/6 = 30 s.

    Initial separation divided by constant closing speed.

  3. A travels 24 × 30 = 720 m; B travels 18 × 30 = 540 m.

    A covers 180 m more, exactly the initial gap.

Result: A reaches B after 30 s.

What it means: Adding 24 and 18 would apply to head-on motion, not to vehicles travelling in the same direction.

Point straight across and accept the drift

Problem. A boat travels at 2.5 m/s relative to water, pointing straight across a 100 m river. The current is 1.5 m/s downstream. Find crossing time, drift and speed relative to the bank.

  1. Across component = 2.5 m/s; downstream component = 1.5 m/s.

    The current changes only the downstream component in this model.

  2. t = 100/2.5 = 40 s; drift = 1.5 × 40 = 60 m.

    Use the across component to cross the width.

  3. Ground speed = √2.52 + 1.52 = 2.92 m/s.

    The perpendicular components form a right triangle.

Result: 40 s, landing 60 m downstream; speed relative to the bank ≈ 2.92 m/s.

What it means: Dividing 100 m by 2.92 m/s would mix a perpendicular width with speed along a diagonal path.

Aim upstream to land directly opposite

Problem. Use the same river and boat. Find the upstream heading and crossing time for zero downstream drift.

  1. Choose the boat’s upstream component to be −1.5 m/s.

    It must cancel the water’s +1.5 m/s downstream velocity.

  2. sin α = 1.5/2.5 = 0.60, so α = 36.9° upstream from straight across.

    The total velocity through water remains 2.5 m/s.

  3. Across component = √2.52 − 1.52 = 2.0 m/s; t = 100/2.0 = 50 s.

    Part of the boat’s velocity is now used to cancel the current.

Result: Aim 36.9° upstream from straight across; crossing takes 50 s.

What it means: Zero drift takes longer than simply pointing straight across. If the current is at least the boat’s water speed, a direct opposite-bank crossing is impossible in this model.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: Adding speeds without directions.

    Correct idea: Add signed components of velocities. Then calculate a magnitude if needed.

  • Misunderstanding: Using diagonal ground speed to cross a perpendicular river width.

    Correct idea: Time uses the velocity component in the direction of that width.

  • Misunderstanding: Using the same velocity for every observer.

    Correct idea: Write both the moving object and the reference frame on each velocity.

References for these concepts: OpenStax University Physics 1 — Relative motion. The explanations and examples above are written for this library.

02

Vectors, components, and products

When you see

Several displacements or forces point in different directions, or work and torque appear.

Choose this model

Vector with signed components in a declared coordinate system.

Principle

Vectors add component by component. The dot product selects the component parallel to a second vector; the cross product measures a perpendicular turning effect and has a direction.

First move

Draw the axes and angle first. Check the quadrant before taking an inverse tangent. Keep units attached to each component.

Worked reasoning

A 3 m east displacement followed by 4 m north has components (3,4) m and magnitude 5 m, directed about 53 degrees north of east.

Additional worked example

Combine a 5 N east force and a 12 N north force.

Components are (5,12) N; magnitude is 13 N. The direction is about 67 degrees north of east.

Important distinction. Adding magnitudes would give 17 N and ignore direction. A negative component is not a negative magnitude.

03

Motion graphs and rates

When you see

The question gives position, velocity, or acceleration as a graph or function of time.

Choose this model

Particle; choose a graph slope for a rate and a signed area for accumulation.

Principle

The slope of a position graph is velocity. The slope of a velocity graph is acceleration. Signed area under velocity gives displacement; absolute area gives distance.

First move

Mark the time interval and axis units. For a piecewise graph, calculate each signed area before adding.

Worked reasoning

A velocity of +4 m/s for 3 s, then -2 m/s for 2 s, gives displacement 12-4 = 8 m and distance 12+4 = 16 m.

Additional worked example

Velocity is -3 m/s for 4 s. Find displacement and distance.

Displacement is -12 m; distance is 12 m. The sign records direction, not the amount of ground covered.

Important distinction. The area under a position-time graph is not generally a displacement.

04

Constant acceleration and free fall

When you see

Acceleration is constant over the entire interval, including ideal vertical motion near Earth.

Choose this model

Particle under constant acceleration.

Principle

These relationships follow from constant acceleration. For upward-positive free fall, a_y = -g even at the top of the path.

First move

Declare the positive axis, write initial and final states, and choose the relation that eliminates the unneeded variable.

Worked reasoning

A cart starts at 2 m/s and accelerates at 3 m/s² for 4 s. It ends at 14 m/s and moves 2(4)+0.5(3)(4²)=32 m.

Additional worked example

A ball is launched upward at 20 m/s. With g = 10 m/s², when does it first reach its highest point?

Set vertical velocity to zero: 0=20-10t, so t=2 s. Acceleration is still -10 m/s² at that instant.

Important distinction. Do not apply a constant-acceleration formula across intervals with changing acceleration.

05

Projectiles in two dimensions

When you see

A body moves after launch while gravity is the only appreciable force.

Choose this model

Particle with independent horizontal and vertical equations sharing one time.

Principle

Ideal projectile motion has horizontal acceleration zero and vertical acceleration -g. A zero vertical velocity at the peak does not make the total velocity zero.

First move

Split launch velocity into components. Solve the vertical condition for time, then use that same time horizontally.

Worked reasoning

A ball leaves a 20 m ledge horizontally at 5 m/s. With g=10 m/s², falling takes 2 s and the horizontal range is 10 m.

Additional worked example

From a 45 m ledge, a ball launches horizontally at 8 m/s. Use g=10 m/s². Find its flight time and range.

Vertical fall gives 45=5t², hence t=3 s. Range is 8(3)=24 m.

Important distinction. The familiar equal-height range formula does not apply when launch and landing heights differ.

06

Relative motion

When you see

The velocity of one object is described from a moving vehicle, river, or observer.

Choose this model

Particle velocities with frame labels; add vectors, not just speeds.

Principle

Relative velocity is a difference between observations in two frames. Frame labels make the chaining rule unambiguous.

First move

Name the moving object and each observer. Draw component arrows before solving for magnitude and heading.

Worked reasoning

A boat moves 4 m/s east relative to water, and water moves 3 m/s north relative to shore. The boat moves 5 m/s relative to shore, northeast.

Additional worked example

A walker moves 2 m/s east on a train moving 10 m/s east relative to ground. Find walker speed relative to ground.

12 m/s east. The two relative velocities add because their directions agree.

Important distinction. A velocity without its reference frame is incomplete.

Newton laws and force diagrams

Forces change motion. Isolate one object, draw every force acting on it, and apply ΣF = ma along each axis.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Definitions

Force
A push or pull on an object due to an interaction with another object; a vector measured in newtons (N).
Net force ΣF
The vector sum of all forces acting on the object.
Equilibrium
ΣF = 0, so a = 0: the object is at rest or moving at constant velocity.
Normal force N
The contact force perpendicular to a surface, preventing objects from passing through it.
Tension T
The pulling force exerted by a rope or cable, directed along it away from the object. For an ideal (massless) rope over an ideal pulley, the tension is the same throughout.
Free-body diagram
A sketch of one isolated object with arrows for every external force acting on it, plus chosen axes.
Symbols, meanings and units
SymbolMeaningUnit
ΣFnet (vector sum of) forceN
mmasskg

W = mgweightN
N, Tnormal force, tensionN

Formulas and their conditions

Newton’s second law

ΣFx = max, ΣFy = may

Conditions and limits: In an inertial (non-accelerating) reference frame, for a body of constant mass. Sum only forces acting on the chosen body.

Equilibrium

ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0

Conditions and limits: Object at rest or moving with constant velocity.

Weight

W = mg

Conditions and limits: Near the Earth’s surface, g ≈ 9.8 m/s².

Third law

FA on B = −FB on A

Conditions and limits: Always; the two forces act on different bodies and are of the same type (both gravitational, both contact, …).

Apparent weight in a lift

N = m(g + a)

Conditions and limits: Vertical acceleration a, up positive. N is what a bathroom scale reads.

Why the normal force is not always mg

Use the block in the figure: m = 5 kg, pulled by T = 20 N at 30° above horizontal, on a frictionless floor.

  1. Vertical forces: N up, T sin 30° = 10 N up, mg = 49 N down.

    Resolve every force along y.

  2. The block does not leave the floor, so ay = 0: N + 10 − 49 = 0.

    ΣFy = may with ay = 0.

  3. N = 39 N, which is less than mg = 49 N.

    The rope carries part of the weight.

  4. Horizontally: T cos 30° = 17.3 N = max ⇒ ax = 3.46 m/s².

    ΣFx = max.

N is found from the equations, never assumed. On an incline, or with an upward pull, or in a lift, it differs from mg.

Worked example

Acceleration from the net force

Problem. A 4 kg object has horizontal forces of 20 N to the right and 8 N to the left. Find its acceleration.

  1. Take right as positive: ΣFx = 20 − 8 = 12 N.

    Add forces with signs.

  2. a = ΣF / m = 12 / 4 = 3 m/s2.

    Newton’s second law.

Result: a = 3 m/s2 to the right.

What it means: Only the net force matters; the object could be moving either way at this instant.

Scope

  • Friction and inclined planes are in the next topic; circular motion (net inward force) in its own topic.
  • Ropes and pulleys are ideal (massless, frictionless) unless stated. Rotational dynamics (torque) is beyond this topic.

07

Free-body diagrams and Newton laws

When you see

The target is an acceleration, contact force, or equilibrium condition.

Choose this model

One isolated body with only real external forces; particle under net force.

Principle

A net force changes velocity. Zero net force means zero acceleration, not necessarily zero velocity. Third-law partners act on different bodies.

First move

Choose one body, draw gravity and each contact or applied force, then resolve the sum on chosen axes.

Worked reasoning

A 4 kg cart has 18 N right and 6 N left. Its net force is 12 N right and its acceleration is 3 m/s² right.

Additional worked example

A 5 kg object has 25 N east and 10 N west. Find its acceleration.

Net force is 15 N east, so acceleration is 3 m/s² east.

Important distinction. Do not place an equal-and-opposite third-law partner on the same free-body diagram.

PHY 101 · TOPIC 04 OF 8

Friction and inclined surfaces

Friction is the contact force along a surface. Static friction adjusts up to a limit; kinetic friction has a fixed size, μₖN, and opposes sliding.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Definitions

Static friction fs
Friction when surfaces do not slide: 0 ≤ fs ≤ μsN, with whatever direction and size prevents slipping.
Kinetic friction fk
Friction during sliding: fk = μkN, opposite to the direction of sliding.
Coefficient of friction μ
A dimensionless number describing a pair of surfaces; μs for static, μk for kinetic.
Angle of repose
The steepest incline angle on which an object stays at rest: tan θ = μs.
Symbols, meanings and units
SymbolMeaningUnit
μs, μkstatic and kinetic friction coefficientsno unit
Nnormal forceN (newton)
fs, fkstatic and kinetic friction forcesN
θincline angle above horizontal°

Formulas and their conditions

Static friction

fs ≤ μsN

Conditions and limits: An inequality: use fs = μsN only at the point of slipping. Otherwise find fs from ΣF = 0.

Kinetic friction

fk = μkN

Conditions and limits: Surfaces sliding. Direction opposite to the relative velocity. Approximately independent of speed and contact area.

Incline components

along the slope: mg sin θ; perpendicular: mg cos θ

Conditions and limits: θ measured from the horizontal.

Sliding down with friction

a = g(sin θ − μk cos θ)

Conditions and limits: Block sliding down, no other forces. If the result is negative, the block decelerates (and eventually stops).

Onset of sliding

tan θmax = μs

Conditions and limits: Block at rest on an incline with no other forces along the slope.

Acceleration of a block sliding down a rough incline

Axes: x down the slope (the direction of motion), y perpendicular to it.

  1. y: N − mg cos θ = 0 ⇒ N = mg cos θ.

    No acceleration perpendicular to the slope.

  2. Friction: fk = μkN = μkmg cos θ, pointing up the slope.

    Opposes the sliding.

  3. x: mg sin θ − μkmg cos θ = ma.

    Down-slope component of weight minus friction.

  4. a = g(sin θ − μk cos θ).

    The mass cancels.

With θ = 30° and μₖ = 0.20: a = 9.8(0.500 − 0.173) = 3.20 m/s². Heavy and light blocks of the same material slide down with the same acceleration.

Worked example

Kinetic friction on a level floor

Problem. A 20 kg crate is pushed across a floor by a horizontal 80 N force. μk = 0.30. Find the friction force and the acceleration (g = 9.8 m/s2).

  1. N = mg = 20 × 9.8 = 196 N.

    Horizontal push: no other vertical forces, so here N = mg.

  2. fk = 0.30 × 196 = 58.8 N, opposite to the motion.

    Kinetic friction formula.

  3. a = 80 − 58.8 / 20 = 1.06 m/s2.

    Net force divided by mass.

Result: fk = 58.8 N; a ≈ 1.06 m/s2.

What it means: If the push were exactly 58.8 N the crate would slide at constant velocity.

Scope

  • Coulomb’s friction model (constant μ) is an approximation. Rolling resistance, lubrication and air drag need other models.

08

Friction and inclined surfaces

When you see

A body touches a rough surface or moves along a slope.

Choose this model

One body with normal force and friction; decide rest versus sliding first.

Principle

Static friction adjusts up to a maximum; kinetic friction has approximate magnitude μkN while surfaces slide. The normal force is set by perpendicular force balance, not by a universal N=mg rule.

First move

Choose axes parallel and perpendicular to the slope. Use the impending-motion test before selecting a friction law.

Worked reasoning

For a 2 kg block sliding down a 30-degree slope with μk=0.20 and g=10, N≈17.3 N, downhill gravity is 10 N, friction is 3.46 N uphill, and a≈3.27 m/s² downhill.

Additional worked example

A 3 kg block rests on a horizontal floor with μs=0.5 and g=10. Can static friction hold against a 12 N horizontal pull?

Yes. N=30 N and the maximum static friction is 15 N, but actual static friction is 12 N, opposite the pull.

Important distinction. Setting static friction equal to μsN in every stationary problem overstates it.

PHY 101 · TOPIC 05 OF 8

Work energy and power

Work transfers energy. Kinetic and potential energy trade places, and the total is conserved unless friction or other forces add or remove energy. Power is the rate of energy transfer.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Definitions

Work W
Energy transferred by a force acting through a displacement: W = Fd cos θ (constant force). Unit: joule, 1 J = 1 N·m. Scalar, can be negative.
Kinetic energy K
K = 1 / 2mv2; never negative.
Gravitational potential energy
Ug = mgh near the Earth’s surface, with h measured from a chosen reference level. Only changes ΔU matter.
Elastic potential energy
Us = 1 / 2kx2 for an ideal spring stretched or compressed by x from its natural length.
Mechanical energy
E = K + U.
Power P
Rate of energy transfer, P = W / Δt; unit watt (W). 1 kW·h = 3.6 × 106 J.

Formulas and their conditions

Work by a constant force

W = Fd cos θ

Conditions and limits: Force constant in size and direction over the displacement d; θ is the angle between F and the displacement. For a varying force, W is the area under the F–x graph.

Work–energy theorem

Wnet = ΔK = 1 / 2mvf2 − 1 / 2mvi2

Conditions and limits: Always true for a particle; W_net is the work of all forces together.

Conservation of mechanical energy

Ki + Ui = Kf + Uf

Conditions and limits: Only conservative forces (gravity, ideal springs) do work. Otherwise include Wother: Ki + Ui + Wother = Kf + Uf.

Spring force and energy

F = −kx; Us = 1 / 2kx2

Conditions and limits: Ideal spring within its elastic limit (Hooke’s law).

Power

P = W / Δt; P = Fv

Conditions and limits: P = Fv for a force parallel to the velocity; instantaneous power.

Speed at the bottom of a frictionless slope does not depend on its shape

An object starts from rest at height h and slides without friction to the bottom.

  1. Ki + Ui = Kf + Uf: 0 + mgh = 1 / 2mv2 + 0.

    Only gravity does work (the normal force is perpendicular to the motion).

  2. v2 = 2gh ⇒ v = √2gh.

    The mass cancels.

A steep slope and a gentle one of the same height give the same final speed (the gentle one just takes longer). Energy methods ignore the path; that is their power.

Worked example

Work by a force at an angle

Problem. A 50 N pull at 37° above the horizontal drags a box 4.0 m across a floor. How much work does the pull do?

  1. W = Fd cos θ = 50 × 4.0 × cos 37°.

    Only the horizontal component does work.

  2. = 200 × 0.799 = 160 J.

    cos 37° ≈ 0.799.

Result: W ≈ 160 J.

What it means: The vertical component (≈ 30 N) does no work because the box does not move vertically.

Scope

  • Gravitational potential energy mgh assumes heights small compared with the Earth’s radius.
  • Work by variable forces (area under F–x graphs) is introduced; general line integrals are beyond this course.

Impulse and momentum

Momentum p = mv is a vector. A force acting for a time changes it (impulse). In a collision with no external force, total momentum is conserved.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Definitions

Momentum p
p = mv, a vector in the direction of the velocity. Unit kg·m/s.
Impulse J
J = FavgΔt = Δp; equals the area under a force–time graph. Unit N·s (= kg·m/s).
Isolated system
A system on which the net external force is zero (or negligible during a brief collision).
Elastic collision
Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.
Inelastic collision
Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy is not. Perfectly inelastic: the objects stick together.
Centre of mass
xcm = m1x1 + m2x2 + … / m1 + m2 + …, the mass-weighted average position.
Symbols, meanings and units
SymbolMeaningUnit
pmomentumkg·m/s
JimpulseN·s
Favgaverage force during contactN
Δtcontact times
vi, vfvelocities before and after (signed)m/s

Formulas and their conditions

Impulse–momentum theorem

J = FavgΔt = Δp = mvf − mvi

Conditions and limits: Always; F_avg is the time-averaged net force. Use signed velocities.

Conservation of momentum

m1v1i + m2v2i = m1v1f + m2v2f

Conditions and limits: No net external force on the system during the interaction (or the collision is so brief that external impulses are negligible). In 2D, conserve each component separately.

Perfectly inelastic

vf = m1v1i + m2v2i / m1 + m2

Conditions and limits: Objects stick together after the collision.

Elastic, target at rest (1D)

v1f = m1 − m2 / m1 + m2v1i; v2f = 2m1 / m1 + m2v1i

Conditions and limits: Head-on elastic collision with m2 initially at rest.

Why momentum is conserved in a collision

Two carts collide. During contact, cart 1 pushes cart 2 with force F and cart 2 pushes cart 1 with −F (third law), for the same time Δt.

  1. Impulse on cart 2: Δp2 = FΔt.

    Impulse–momentum theorem.

  2. Impulse on cart 1: Δp1 = −FΔt.

    Equal and opposite force, same time.

  3. Δp1 + Δp2 = 0.

    Add them.

The total momentum does not change. External forces (friction with the track) would spoil this, but during a brief collision their impulse is usually negligible.

Worked example

Impulse and a change of direction

Problem. A 2 kg body changes velocity from +5 m/s to +2 m/s. Find the impulse. Then find the impulse if instead it rebounds at −2 m/s.

  1. J = m(vf − vi) = 2(2 − 5) = −6 N·s.

    Slowing down in the positive direction: negative impulse.

  2. Rebound: J = 2(−2 − 5) = −14 N·s.

    The velocity change is 7 m/s, not 3 m/s.

Result: −6 N·s; with a rebound, −14 N·s.

What it means: Bouncing requires a larger impulse than stopping: the wall must first stop the body and then push it back.

Scope

  • Collisions in one dimension are treated fully; two-dimensional collisions use the same idea with components.
  • Variable-mass systems (rockets) are beyond this topic.

Circular motion

An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating towards the centre, a = v²/r. Some real force (tension, friction, gravity, normal force) must supply the net inward force mv²/r.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Definitions

Uniform circular motion
Motion on a circle at constant speed.
Centripetal acceleration
ac = v2 / r = ω2r, directed towards the centre.
Period T and frequency f
T is the time for one revolution; f = 1/T revolutions per second (Hz).
Angular speed ω
ω = Δθ / Δt = 2π / T (rad/s); v = ωr.
Banked curve
A road tilted at angle θ so that the horizontal component of the normal force helps provide the inward force.
Symbols, meanings and units
SymbolMeaningUnit

vspeedm/s
accentripetal accelerationm/s²
Tperiods
ωangular speedrad/s
μscoefficient of static friction—

Formulas and their conditions

Centripetal acceleration

ac = v2 / r = ω2r

Conditions and limits: Uniform circular motion; directed towards the centre. For non-uniform circular motion this is the radial part of the acceleration.

Net inward force

ΣFin = mv2 / r

Conditions and limits: Sum of the components of the real forces along the radius, towards the centre positive.

Speed, period, angular speed

v = 2πr / T = ωr; ω = 2π / T = 2πf

Conditions and limits: Constant speed.

Maximum speed on a flat curve

vmax = √μsgr

Conditions and limits: Level road, friction alone provides the inward force; the tyres do not slip (static friction).

Ideal banking speed

tan θ = v2 / rg

Conditions and limits: Banked curve with no friction needed at this speed.

Where a = v²/r comes from

In a short time Δt the object moves through a small angle Δθ. Its velocity keeps size v but turns through the same angle Δθ.

  1. The change in velocity has magnitude |Δv| ≈ vΔθ.

    For a small angle, the arc swept by the tip of the velocity arrow is v × Δθ.

  2. The angle turned is Δθ = vΔt / r.

    Arc length travelled (vΔt) divided by radius.

  3. a = ∣Δv∣ / Δt = vΔθ / Δt = v·v / r = v2 / r.

    Divide by Δt.

  4. Δv points towards the centre (perpendicular to v).

    As Δt → 0 the change is at right angles to the velocity, i.e. inward.

Check units: (m/s)²/m = m/s².

Worked example

Centripetal acceleration

Problem. An object moves at 6 m/s around a circle of radius 3 m. Find its acceleration.

  1. ac = v2 / r = 36 / 3 = 12 m/s2.

    Directed towards the centre.

Result: 12 m/s2 towards the centre.

What it means: More than g (9.8 m/s²), even though the speed is modest, because the circle is tight.

Scope

  • Uniform circular motion and simple vertical circles. Rotational dynamics (torque, moment of inertia) and gravitation beyond the circular-orbit equation are covered in the collection notes and later courses.

Interference and standing waves

When you see

Waves overlap, reflect from a boundary, or resonate in a string or air column.

Choose this model

Superposition of waves with boundary conditions.

Principle

At a node, displacement is always zero; at an antinode, amplitude is largest. Allowed wavelengths are fixed by boundary conditions, not arbitrary harmonic labels.

First move

Sketch nodes and antinodes first. Count half-wavelengths along a string fixed at both ends before choosing n.

Worked reasoning

For a 1 m fixed string with wave speed 100 m/s, fundamental frequency is 50 Hz and second harmonic is 100 Hz.

Additional worked example

What is the third-harmonic frequency of the same string?

150 Hz because f3=3f1. The standing pattern contains three half-wavelengths.

Important distinction. An air column closed at one end has different allowed harmonics; do not import the fixed-string formula unchanged.

Temperature and ideal gas states

When you see

Pressure, volume, or temperature of a dilute gas changes.

Choose this model

Ideal gas in equilibrium; temperature must be absolute.

Principle

The gas law relates equilibrium states of a fixed amount of ideal gas. Pressure at fixed volume and mole number scales with kelvin temperature.

First move

Write two states. Use kelvins and keep n constant only if no gas enters or leaves.

Worked reasoning

Heating a sealed rigid vessel from 300 K to 450 K increases ideal-gas pressure by a factor 450/300=1.5.

Additional worked example

A rigid vessel at 200 kPa and 300 K reaches 600 K. Find final pressure if gas amount stays fixed.

400 kPa because P₂/P₁=T₂/T₁=2.

Important distinction. Doubling Celsius temperature does not generally double ideal-gas pressure.

31

Thermal energy and calorimetry

When you see

Bodies exchange heat and their temperatures change or a phase transition occurs.

Choose this model

Thermal system; energy transferred by temperature difference.

Principle

Specific heat governs temperature change within one phase. During an ideal phase change at fixed pressure, latent heat changes phase without changing temperature.

First move

Choose the system, set heat lost plus heat gained to zero if insulated, and treat each heating or phase segment separately.

Worked reasoning

Heating 0.50 kg of water by 10 K with c=4200 J/(kg·K) requires Q=0.50·4200·10=21 kJ.

Additional worked example

How much heat is needed to warm 0.20 kg of the same water by 5 K?

Q=0.20·4200·5=4.2 kJ, assuming no phase change or loss.

Important distinction. Use a temperature difference in K or °C, but use kelvins for absolute gas temperatures and entropy formulas.

32

First law and gas work

When you see

Heat enters or leaves a gas while its volume may change.

Choose this model

Thermodynamic system with a declared work sign convention.

Principle

Internal energy changes through heat added and work done by the system. Expansion usually gives positive work by the gas; compression gives negative work by the gas.

First move

State whether W means work by or on the gas. On a P-V graph, signed area under the path is work by the gas.

Worked reasoning

A gas receives 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work. Its internal energy rises by 300 J.

Additional worked example

An adiabatic gas is compressed by surroundings doing 150 J of work on it. Find ΔU.

Q=0 and Wby=-150 J, hence ΔU=0-(-150)=+150 J.

Important distinction. For a cyclic process, ΔU=0 over the whole cycle, though heat and work need not each be zero.

33

Microscopic gas energy

When you see

The question relates temperature to molecular motion or heat capacity.

Choose this model

Classical monatomic ideal gas, with translational degrees of freedom.

Principle

Absolute temperature measures average molecular translational kinetic energy. Internal energy of a monatomic ideal gas depends only on temperature, not separately on pressure or volume.

First move

Check the gas type and model. For a temperature change use ΔU=(3/2)nRΔT.

Worked reasoning

One mole of monatomic ideal gas warmed by 100 K gains about 1.5(8.314)(100)=1.25 kJ of internal energy.

Additional worked example

If two moles of this gas warm by 50 K, what is ΔU?

ΔU=(3/2)(2)(8.314)(50)≈1.25 kJ.

Important distinction. Do not apply the monatomic 3/2 factor to every gas without checking active degrees of freedom.

Heat engines and the Carnot limit

When you see

Heat flows between hot and cold reservoirs, sometimes producing work.

Choose this model

Cyclic engine with energy accounting and second-law limit.

Principle

A heat engine cannot turn all intake heat into work while operating cyclically between two reservoirs. Carnot efficiency is the upper limit for reservoir temperatures in kelvins.

First move

Draw heat-input, work-output, and rejected-heat arrows. First use Qh=W+Qc, then compare actual efficiency with the Carnot bound.

Worked reasoning

An engine takes 1000 J and rejects 600 J each cycle. It delivers 400 J and has 40% efficiency. At Th=500 K and Tc=300 K, 40% is the Carnot ceiling.

Additional worked example

If an engine takes 800 J and produces 200 J of work, how much heat does it reject and what is efficiency?

Qc=800-200=600 J and η=200/800=25%.

Important distinction. Carnot efficiency is a maximum under specified reservoir temperatures, not the expected efficiency of every actual engine.

35

Entropy and refrigeration

When you see

Heat flows between reservoirs or a device moves heat from cold to hot.

Choose this model

Thermal system plus surroundings; assess entropy of the combined isolated whole.

Principle

Entropy can decrease in one part while total entropy of an isolated system does not decrease. A refrigerator requires work to extract heat from a cold region and release still more heat to a warm region.

First move

Track positive heat magnitudes and the work input. For a reversible isothermal heat transfer, use ΔS=Qrev/T with kelvin temperature; for an irreversible process, evaluate entropy change along a reversible reference path.

Worked reasoning

A refrigerator removes 300 J from its cold compartment using 100 J of work. It rejects 400 J to the room and has cold-side COP=300/100=3.

Additional worked example

A refrigerator removes 500 J using 200 J of work. Find heat rejected and cold-side COP.

Heat rejected is 700 J by energy balance; COPc=500/200=2.5.

Important distinction. COP can exceed one because it counts heat moved, not work converted into heat; it is not an engine efficiency.
