## ENG-01 · Build a complete sentence

Recognise the main message of a sentence and connect a reason without leaving an unfinished thought.

### Explanation

A sentence tells the reader something complete. In an ordinary statement, find the subject and the verb that carries tense: in “The student checks the result”, student is the subject and checks is the present-tense verb. Words around this pair can add detail without changing the basic message. Instructions can omit an explicit subject: “Check the result” has an understood you.

A group beginning with because, although or if often depends on another clause. “Because the library closed” leaves the reader waiting for the consequence. “We studied at home because the library closed” gives both a complete statement and its reason. The connector expresses the relationship; it is not decoration.

Two complete statements need a suitable boundary. A full stop is often the clearest choice. A coordinating conjunction such as but can join them when the relationship matters. A comma by itself usually cannot join two independent clauses in formal academic writing. Long sentences are useful only when their parts remain clear.

### Language pattern

Complete statement + because + reason; Although + situation, + complete statement.

Attach the dependent information to the main message.

Limits: Some short utterances are natural in conversation. The examples here concern complete academic prose.

### Repair a fragment

Context: Draft: “Although the room was quiet.” The intended message is that the students still could not concentrate.

Although the room was quiet, the students could not concentrate.

Why it works:

- The students could not concentrate is a complete statement.

- Although introduces an unexpected contrast.

- The opening dependent clause is followed by a comma.

The sentence expresses a contrast; it does not claim that quietness caused the difficulty.

### Separate two findings

Context: Draft: “The first trial ended the team recorded the result.”

The first trial ended. The team recorded the result.

Why it works:

- Each statement has its own subject and verb.

- A full stop marks the boundary without inventing a causal relationship.

- “After the first trial ended, the team recorded the result” is also possible if the sequence is intended.

Choose the connection that matches the meaning, rather than making every sentence longer.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: Because the instructions were unclear.
  Revision: We requested clarification because the instructions were unclear.
  Reason: The reason is now attached to a complete message.

- Draft: The result was unexpected, we repeated the measurement.
  Revision: The result was unexpected, so we repeated the measurement.
  Reason: So connects two complete clauses and makes the consequence explicit.

### Keep in mind

- Find the main message before adding detail.

- Use a connector for a real relationship.

- A clear short sentence is preferable to an uncontrolled long one.

## ENG-02 · Choose tense and voice for your meaning

Distinguish a general process from completed work and choose active or passive voice deliberately.

### Explanation

Tense locates a statement in time or presents it as generally true. “The system records the temperature every minute” describes a routine. “The system recorded the temperature yesterday” describes completed work. A report can correctly contain several tenses because its sentences have different purposes. Consistency means a clear time relationship, not putting every verb in one tense.

Active voice names the doer before the action: “The team checked the sensor.” Passive voice makes the affected item the subject: “The sensor was checked.” Passive voice is useful when the item or procedure matters more than the doer. Active voice can be more direct when responsibility matters. Neither voice automatically makes a sentence more accurate or more academic.

Build an ordinary passive with a form of be and a past participle. Preserve time: is checked for a routine, was checked for a completed event. Some verbs do not normally take a passive because they have no object to promote: “The temperature rose”, not “The temperature was risen”. Follow any specific reporting conventions required by the course.

### Language pattern

Routine: the team checks / the sensor is checked. Completed work: the team checked / the sensor was checked.

Time and focus are separate choices.

Limits: Changing voice should preserve the underlying event. It can change emphasis or remove information about who acted.

### A general process

Context: A short guide explains how an ordinary water-filter system operates.

Water enters the filter. The filter removes suspended particles. The filtered water is collected in a tank.

Why it works:

- Present tense describes the general sequence.

- The second sentence uses active voice because the filter is a useful subject.

- The third uses passive voice to keep attention on the water.

Mixed voice is appropriate when each sentence remains clear.

### A completed investigation

Context: A team describes what it actually did on Monday.

We checked the balance before the trial. Each sample was weighed twice, and both readings were recorded.

Why it works:

- Checked, was weighed and were recorded all locate the actions in the completed investigation.

- We makes responsibility clear in the first sentence.

- Each sample is singular; both readings is plural.

The passive does not require present tense. It follows the time of the event.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: The students was asked to submit the report.
  Revision: The students were asked to submit the report.
  Reason: The auxiliary agrees with the plural subject.

- Draft: Passive voice is always more scientific.
  Revision: Choose the voice that makes the action and responsibility clear.
  Reason: Voice is a communication choice, not a quality score.

### Keep in mind

- State a routine in present tense when appropriate.

- Use past tense for completed procedures when appropriate.

- Preserve meaning when changing voice.

## ENG-03 · Keep subjects, articles and references clear

Make it clear what a sentence refers to and which noun controls its verb.

### Explanation

The main noun of a subject controls ordinary subject–verb agreement. In “The effect of the changes is small”, effect is the head noun; changes is inside an of-phrase. Do not choose a plural verb just because the nearest noun is plural. In “The changes affect the result”, changes is the subject, so affect has no third-person singular s.

A or an introduces one non-specific singular countable item. The often identifies an item that the reader can recognise from the context. “We borrowed a meter. The meter was damaged” introduces the item and then refers back to it. General plural and uncountable nouns can appear without an article: “Students need feedback”; “Information is useful”. Article use has exceptions, so context matters.

Pronouns and words such as this should point to something clear. If two people or two results could be the intended reference, repeat the noun. “This difference” can be clearer than this alone because it names the kind of idea being discussed. Clarity matters more than avoiding all repetition.

### Language pattern

A/an + one countable item on first introduction; the + identifiable item; main subject noun + agreeing verb.

Articles identify the reference, while agreement links the subject and verb.

Limits: A/an depends on the next sound: an hour, a university. These are introductory patterns, not every article rule.

### Find the real subject

Context: Draft: “The quality of the two reports are different.” The writer is comparing the quality of one report with the other.

The two reports differ in quality.

Why it works:

- The revision avoids an awkward singular subject and comparison.

- Reports is plural, so differ is the appropriate form.

- A simpler sentence can resolve both grammar and meaning problems.

Do not repair agreement mechanically while leaving the comparison unclear.

### Make a reference explicit

Context: Draft: “Maya sent Sara the revised chart after she corrected the labels.” Maya corrected them.

After correcting the labels, Maya sent Sara the revised chart.

Why it works:

- The opening phrase describes the subject Maya.

- The revised chart is identifiable in the shared context.

- The ambiguity about who corrected the labels is removed.

The revision preserves the intended sequence and identifies the actor.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: We received many useful informations.
  Revision: We received a lot of useful information.
  Reason: Information is normally uncountable in this meaning.

- Draft: A report was submitted. A report contained three charts.
  Revision: A report was submitted. The report contained three charts.
  Reason: The second sentence refers to the same report.

### Keep in mind

- Find the head noun, not merely the nearest noun.

- Use articles to help the reader identify the item.

- Repeat a noun when a pronoun would be ambiguous.

## ENG-04 · Choose precise vocabulary without exaggeration

Use appropriate word combinations and match the strength of a claim to the available evidence.

### Explanation

Useful vocabulary is more than a list of advanced synonyms. Learn a word with its meaning, grammatical role and common partners: conduct a study, collect data, reach a conclusion. A familiar precise phrase is often more effective than an unusual word chosen to sound academic. A synonym can change meaning, register or the pattern of words around it.

Word families help the same idea fit different sentence positions: analyse is a verb, analysis is a noun, and analytical is an adjective. “The team analysed the readings” and “The analysis revealed a difference” organise the message differently. These examples use British spelling; American analyze is also standard. Follow the chosen house style consistently.

Certainty words change a claim. May suggests possibility; tends to describes a recurring pattern with possible exceptions. Shows is stronger than suggests in many contexts, while proves is usually too strong for a small observational example. Qualifying a claim should make its limits precise, not make every sentence vague.

### Language pattern

The evidence suggests [limited claim] under [stated conditions].

State what the evidence supports and the boundary around it.

Limits: Do not weaken a directly observed fact without reason. “Twenty students attended” can be stated directly when the count is known.

### Replace an inflated claim

Context: An invented survey asks 18 volunteers whether a quiet room helped them study; 12 say yes.

In this small volunteer survey, 12 of 18 respondents said that the quiet room helped them study. The responses suggest that some students valued the space; they do not establish an improvement in grades.

Why it works:

- The first sentence reports the count and what was actually asked.

- The second gives a limited interpretation.

- Volunteer feedback is not a controlled measurement of attainment.

Precise language separates reported experience from a demonstrated effect.

### Choose a suitable word family

Context: Draft: “We did an analyse of the results and made a conclusion.”

We analysed the results and reached a conclusion.

Why it works:

- Analysed is the main verb for the first action.

- Reached a conclusion is a natural word combination.

- “We conducted an analysis” is also grammatical, but longer.

Choose the form that gives the sentence a clear structure.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: The tiny study proves that the method works for everyone.
  Revision: The study provides limited evidence about the participants observed.
  Reason: The revision removes an unsupported universal claim.

- Draft: The data do an improvement.
  Revision: The results show an improvement in the measured outcome.
  Reason: The verb and noun now form a meaningful combination; the outcome still needs to be identified.

### Keep in mind

- Learn vocabulary in meaningful combinations.

- Choose grammatical form before choosing a synonym.

- Match certainty to evidence.

## ENG-05 · Build a paragraph that develops one idea

Connect a clear point, supporting detail and an explanation without relying on a chain of linking words.

### Explanation

A paragraph gives the reader a manageable unit of meaning. A useful starting pattern is point, support and explanation. The point tells the reader what the paragraph is about. The support gives a reason, observation or example. The explanation shows why that detail matters. This is a flexible pattern, not a requirement that every paragraph contain exactly three sentences.

Cohesion comes from connections between ideas: consistent key terms, clear reference and an order the reader can follow. First, moreover and therefore cannot repair an illogical sequence. Therefore signals a conclusion or consequence; however signals contrast. Use them only when the relationship is present.

A paragraph can begin from information the reader already knows and then add something new. In “The library offers silent rooms. These rooms provide…”, the reference is easy to follow. Avoid changing the topic halfway through unless the new point develops the same purpose. Start a new paragraph when the focus genuinely changes.

### Language pattern

Point → relevant support → explanation of why it matters.

Develop the claim rather than merely repeat it.

Limits: An invented illustration must not be presented as real research evidence.

### Develop a practical claim

Context: Explain why a student group agreed on a shared file-naming system. This is an invented scenario.

A shared file-naming system made the group’s work easier to manage. Each draft began with the date and ended with the writer’s initials, so members could identify the latest contribution without opening every document. This reduced confusion about which version to discuss during meetings.

Why it works:

- The opening sentence states one point.

- The middle sentence supplies a concrete method.

- The final sentence explains the practical consequence within the scenario.

The example shows what the system did; it does not claim a measured time saving.

### Replace a misleading connector

Context: Draft: “The library is close to the bus stop. Therefore, it has comfortable chairs.”

The library is close to the bus stop. It also has comfortable chairs.

Why it works:

- The location does not explain the chairs.

- Also adds a second feature without claiming cause.

- If the paragraph concerns accessibility alone, the chair sentence may belong elsewhere.

Choose both the connector and the detail according to the paragraph’s purpose.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: The room is useful. It is very useful. It is helpful.
  Revision: The room is useful because students can discuss projects without disturbing silent study areas.
  Reason: A reason develops the point; synonyms merely repeat it.

- Draft: However, the deadline is Friday.
  Revision: The deadline is Friday.
  Reason: However needs a relevant contrast in the surrounding context.

### Keep in mind

- Develop one main focus per paragraph.

- Support a point with a specific relevant detail.

- A connector should name a real relationship.

## ENG-06 · Read an assignment before collecting material

Identify the requested action, evidence and boundaries of a university assignment.

### Explanation

An assignment usually contains an action, a subject and limits. Describe asks for an organised account of features or events. Compare asks for similarities and differences on a common basis. Evaluate asks for a judgement supported by criteria and evidence. These general meanings help, but the lecturer’s rubric and instructions control what is required in a particular course.

Separate the topic from the task. A task about study spaces may require a recommendation, not an essay containing everything known about libraries. Record the audience, required format, length and source requirements. If a crucial requirement is unclear, ask for clarification before assuming it.

Turn the instructions into a short plan showing how each part will be addressed. This is planning the work, not predicting a grade. A polished paragraph can still miss the assignment if it performs the wrong action. Keep the required decision visible while reading and drafting.

### Language pattern

Action + subject + criteria + evidence + deliverable + limits.

Translate the instruction into a concrete plan.

Limits: The same command word can be used differently across courses; check the actual rubric.

### Compare on the same basis

Context: Invented instruction: Compare two campus study spaces in terms of noise and opening hours. Use observations from one week.

The comparison will describe noise and opening hours for both spaces over the same week. One paragraph will compare noise; a second will compare opening hours. A short conclusion will state which differences matter for the specified use.

Why it works:

- Both places are compared against the same criteria.

- The observation period is held constant.

- The plan avoids unsupported claims about examination performance.

A common basis makes the comparison fair and relevant.

### Evaluate rather than merely describe

Context: Invented instruction: Evaluate whether a recorded introduction should supplement an in-person workshop, using access and opportunities for discussion as criteria.

A recording can improve access for students who miss the introduction, but it offers fewer immediate opportunities for discussion. A combined format is therefore worth considering, provided that students can ask follow-up questions during the workshop.

Why it works:

- The response judges against the two named criteria.

- It considers a benefit and a limitation.

- The recommendation is conditional, rather than a universal claim.

The judgement should later be supported by the evidence the assignment requires.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: The title mentions libraries, so any information about libraries is relevant.
  Revision: Select information that helps answer the requested comparison or judgement.
  Reason: Relevance depends on the task, not only the subject.

- Draft: A high word count guarantees a strong response.
  Revision: Cover the required parts clearly within the specified length.
  Reason: Length is a constraint, not evidence of quality.

### Keep in mind

- Find the action before researching.

- Use the same criteria for comparable options.

- Clarify important missing requirements.

## ENG-07 · Read for structure before reading every detail

Identify a passage’s main purpose and distinguish its central point from supporting detail.

### Explanation

Begin with the title, headings and visible structure. They suggest how a text is organised, but they do not replace reading it. A first overview helps you decide whether a paragraph introduces a problem, gives an example, presents evidence or explains a limitation. Then read the relevant passages closely.

The main idea is broader than a single example but narrower than the entire subject. A text about university libraries may actually argue for different kinds of study space. “Libraries” is only a topic. “A library needs both quiet and collaborative areas” is a claim that the details can develop.

Skimming is a quick search for overall structure and emphasis; scanning locates a specific detail such as a date or term. Neither method means ignoring the surrounding sentence when deciding what the detail means. A heading can be provisional, and a number can belong to a comparison rather than the author’s main conclusion.

### Language pattern

Topic → main claim → supporting detail → limitation.

Give each part of the passage a role.

Limits: Not every paragraph puts its main idea in its first sentence.

### See how a passage develops

Context: Original training passage; the campus and events are invented.

A campus library replaced one open study area with two smaller rooms. One room was reserved for silent work, while the other allowed group discussion. During the first month, staff received fewer complaints about conversations near individual study desks. However, some project groups found the discussion room too small. The experience suggests that separating activities can reduce conflict, but the size of each area still matters.

Why it works:

- The first two sentences describe the change.

- The next two supply a reported benefit and a limitation.

- The final sentence expresses a qualified conclusion.

- The main idea concerns matching spaces to activities, not simply adding more rooms.

An overview should preserve the limitation as well as the benefit.

### Locate a detail and check its role

Context: A reader wants to know the observation period in the passage above.

The passage reports staff feedback during the first month after the change.

Why it works:

- Scanning locates “first month”.

- Close reading identifies what happened during that month: staff received feedback.

- The passage does not provide a year-long evaluation.

The detail must keep its original time boundary.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: The passage proves that smaller rooms are always better.
  Revision: The passage describes one change with a benefit and a size limitation.
  Reason: The universal claim goes beyond the invented account.

- Draft: The heading alone gives the author’s full position.
  Revision: Use the heading as a guide, then read the claims and qualifications.
  Reason: A label cannot establish the complete argument.

### Keep in mind

- Survey the structure, then read closely.

- Distinguish topic, claim and example.

- Carry limitations into your summary.

## ENG-08 · Separate stated evidence from an assumption

Preserve quantities, conditions and uncertainty when interpreting a text.

### Explanation

A statement can be directly supported, contradicted, or left unresolved by a passage. Absence of evidence for a statement is different from evidence that it is false. Read the exact claim, including words such as some, all, only and may. A small change in one of these words can turn a supported claim into an unsupported one.

Inference connects information in a text, but it should remain within what the text reasonably supports. If a notice says a room closes at six, it does not tell us why. An attractive explanation based on personal experience is still an assumption unless the passage supplies evidence for it.

For IELTS-specific question formats, follow the wording of the actual instructions: information questions and writer-view questions are not interchangeable labels. This lesson demonstrates the underlying reading distinction through fully explained examples; it does not ask you to submit answers or predict an examination score.

### Language pattern

Exact claim → relevant evidence → supported, contradicted or unresolved interpretation.

Compare meanings, including their scope.

Limits: An interpretation may be plausible without being established by the text.

### Preserve some rather than all

Context: Original notice: “Some evening workshops will move online in November. The daytime timetable will remain unchanged.”

The notice supports a change to at least part of the evening programme. It contradicts a claim that the daytime timetable will change. It does not establish that all evening workshops will move online.

Why it works:

- Some limits the scope of the first statement.

- Unchanged directly addresses the daytime timetable.

- The notice gives no complete count of evening workshops.

A statement about part of a group cannot automatically be expanded to the whole group.

### Do not invent a reason

Context: Original notice: “The reading room will close at 18:00 on Thursday.”

Thursday’s closing time is stated. A claim that repairs caused the early closure is unresolved because the notice gives no reason.

Why it works:

- The time is directly available.

- “Because repairs are scheduled” would add information that is not present.

- The reader must not treat an unstated cause as either confirmed or disproved.

Accurate reading sometimes ends with “the text does not tell us”.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: No reason is given, so repairs definitely are not planned.
  Revision: The reason is not stated.
  Reason: Missing information does not prove its opposite.

- Draft: May improve means definitely improves.
  Revision: May improve expresses a possibility.
  Reason: The modal verb affects the strength of the claim.

### Keep in mind

- Track words that limit scope.

- Keep facts separate from plausible explanations.

- Do not turn an unknown into a definite claim.

## ENG-09 · Paraphrase and summarise without changing the claim

Restate a source accurately and distinguish a short summary from a close paraphrase.

### Explanation

A paraphrase restates a particular idea in a new structure while preserving its meaning. A summary selects the main points of a longer passage and leaves out less important detail. Replacing individual words with synonyms is unreliable: it can retain the original structure too closely or change the meaning of a technical term.

Read until the idea is clear, note its essential meaning, then write without following the original sentence word by word. Compare your version with the source afterwards. Check who did what, which group was involved, the time period, numerical values and qualifications. Retain a necessary technical term when a synonym would be inaccurate.

Paraphrasing does not remove the need to acknowledge someone else’s ideas. Use the citation style required for real assignments and keep enough source information to locate the original. The passage below is an invented teaching example, not a research publication. Its label identifies that status rather than inventing an author or journal.

### Language pattern

Source attribution + accurate restatement + original limits.

Change the expression while preserving the claim.

Limits: Do not fabricate bibliographic details; a real citation must point to a real source.

### A close paraphrase

Context: Training passage A: “In a voluntary survey of 40 students, 26 preferred receiving written instructions before a workshop. The survey did not measure their performance.”

Training passage A reports that 26 of 40 volunteers favoured getting instructions in writing before the workshop; it provides no measurement of how well they performed.

Why it works:

- The participant count and preference count are unchanged.

- Volunteers preserves the sampling condition.

- The final clause keeps the distinction between preference and performance.

A preference survey cannot be rewritten as evidence that a method improves grades.

### A shorter summary

Context: Summarise the same invented passage without every numerical detail.

Most respondents preferred advance written instructions, but the survey did not assess performance.

Why it works:

- Most is supported because 26 is more than half of 40.

- The details are compressed into the main finding.

- The limitation remains because it affects the interpretation.

A short summary can omit a number when the numerical detail is not essential to its purpose.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: The study proved that written instructions made everyone perform better.
  Revision: The survey reported a majority preference and did not measure performance.
  Reason: The revision removes invented causation and an invented universal result.

- Draft: Changing a few words means the idea needs no citation.
  Revision: Acknowledge borrowed ideas even when the wording is your own.
  Reason: Expression and intellectual origin are separate matters.

### Keep in mind

- Understand before rewriting.

- Preserve qualifiers and numbers.

- Cite genuine sources accurately.

## ENG-10 · Ask for clarification professionally

Write a short message that gives context and makes a specific request.

### Explanation

A useful academic message helps the recipient identify the situation quickly. Give a meaningful subject line, a suitable greeting, the relevant course or task, and one specific request. Explain what you have already checked when that prevents unnecessary back-and-forth. Politeness comes from respectful clarity, not from excessive apologising.

Distinguish clarification from an extension request. Clarification asks what an instruction means. An extension request asks to change an arrangement and may need a reason and a proposed date. Do not imply that permission has been granted merely because you asked. Course policies and the lecturer’s reply determine the outcome.

Keep confidential information out of a routine message unless it is necessary and the recipient is appropriate. The models below use invented names and circumstances. They are language examples, not messages that have been sent or promises that a request will be accepted.

### Language pattern

Context → what you checked → precise request → brief thanks.

Make it easy for the reader to answer the right question.

Limits: Adjust the greeting and level of formality to your institution’s conventions.

### Clarify a reference requirement

Context: A student is unsure whether the word count includes references.

Subject: Clarification about the report word count

Dear Dr Rahman,
I am preparing the report for Academic Skills. The instructions specify 800 words, but I could not find whether the reference list is included. Could you please confirm whether the 800-word limit applies to the main text only?
Thank you for your guidance.
Kind regards,
Lina

Why it works:

- The subject line identifies the issue.

- The body states the relevant instruction and the missing detail.

- The request has a clear, answerable focus.

A specific request is easier to resolve than “I do not understand the report”.

### Request rather than assume a change

Context: A student asks whether a meeting can move from Tuesday to Wednesday.

Dear Dr Rahman,
Would it be possible to move our Tuesday meeting to Wednesday afternoon? I have a timetable conflict on Tuesday. I understand if the original time must remain, and I will follow the arrangement you confirm.
Kind regards,
Lina

Why it works:

- Would it be possible introduces a request.

- The reason is brief and relevant.

- The final sentence recognises that the change is not yet agreed.

The message preserves the distinction between proposing and confirming.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: You must explain everything to me now.
  Revision: Could you clarify whether the report needs a separate conclusion?
  Reason: The revised request is specific and respectful.

- Draft: I have changed our meeting to Wednesday.
  Revision: Would Wednesday afternoon be possible?
  Reason: A request should not be presented as an agreed change.

### Keep in mind

- Provide enough context to identify the task.

- Ask one precise question.

- Do not assume approval before receiving it.

## ENG-11 · Describe a chart with an overview and accurate comparisons

Select the main pattern in a simple dataset and explain changes without inventing causes.

### Explanation

Begin by identifying what is measured, the units, the groups and the time period. An overview describes the most important pattern across the data. Supporting paragraphs then give selected values and comparisons. An overview is not a list of every number, and a data description is not an explanation of causes unless the supplied information supports them.

Percentage points and percentage change answer different questions. A rise from 40% to 55% is an increase of 15 percentage points. Relative to the starting share, the increase is 15 divided by 40, or 37.5%. State which measure you mean. Use approximate language only when appropriate to the precision shown.

For IELTS Academic Writing Task 1, current official guidance requires at least 150 words. The complete model below is an original example using invented data; it has not been scored by an IELTS examiner. The principle also applies to university reports, where the assignment may have different length and formatting requirements.

### Language pattern

Introduction to data → overview → selected comparisons with units.

Lead with the pattern, then show the evidence.

Limits: The dataset describes reported travel mode, not causes, journey frequency or environmental effects.

### A complete original data description

Context: Invented campus survey: main travel mode (%). 2015: bus 40, walking 20, car 40. 2020: bus 50, walking 25, car 25. 2025: bus 55, walking 30, car 15. Each annual sample contains 1,000 respondents and each row totals 100%.

The table compares the percentages of respondents who reported travelling mainly by bus, on foot or by car in three campus surveys conducted in 2015, 2020 and 2025. Each survey included 1,000 respondents.

Overall, bus use and walking became more common, while the proportion travelling mainly by car fell. Bus travel was the largest category in the final two surveys, and by 2025 it accounted for more than half of the responses.

In 2015, buses and cars each represented 40% of the sample, whereas walking accounted for the remaining 20%. Five years later, the bus share had risen to 50%. Walking and car travel were then equal at 25% each.

By 2025, the proportion reporting bus travel had reached 55%, and walking had increased to 30%. Car travel, in contrast, had declined to 15%, a fall of 25 percentage points from its initial level. Across the full period, the increase in walking was steady, at five percentage points between each pair of surveys, while the growth in bus use slowed in the second interval.

Why it works:

- The introduction identifies the measure and dates.

- The overview names the main directions without inventing reasons.

- The detail paragraphs select useful comparisons and retain percentage units.

- The ending compares changes across intervals; it does not add an unsupported opinion.

A complete description can be organised and selective while remaining faithful to every stated number.

### Distinguish two ways to describe a change

Context: The bus share rises from 40% to 55%.

The bus share increased by 15 percentage points, equivalent to a 37.5% increase relative to its original share.

Why it works:

- 55 minus 40 gives 15 percentage points.

- 15 divided by 40, multiplied by 100, gives the relative percentage change.

- Neither value is a claim that the number of bus journeys rose by that amount.

Name the denominator and the quantity being compared.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: The bus share increased by 15%.
  Revision: The bus share increased by 15 percentage points.
  Reason: The first wording confuses a difference between percentages with a relative change.

- Draft: The survey proves that cheaper tickets caused the increase.
  Revision: The survey shows an increase in the reported bus share.
  Reason: Ticket prices and causation are not given in the dataset.

### Keep in mind

- Identify the unit before describing change.

- Give an overview before selected details.

- Do not invent a cause to make a description sound complete.

## ENG-12 · Explain a process and report completed work

Describe a sequence accurately, with clear units and a deliberate choice of time and voice.

### Explanation

A process description explains how stages relate. Start with the input and destination, then follow a meaningful order. Use sequence expressions where they help, but avoid beginning every sentence with then. A reference such as the combined stream can connect stages naturally.

A general description often uses the present tense; a record of completed work commonly uses the past. Active and passive forms can both be appropriate. “The mixer combines the streams” and “The streams are combined” differ in focus. Neither is automatically more accurate. Preserve the actual method, measurements and conditions when writing a real report.

Numbers need units and a clear basis. In a mass-fraction example, 20% by mass means 20 units of solute mass in 100 units of total solution mass. ### Explain an idealised mixer

- The inputs are named before the output.

- While contrasts the two feed compositions without suggesting a time conflict.

## ENG-13 · Develop a position in a complete essay

State a qualified position, develop reasons and respond to a relevant limitation.

### Explanation

An opinion essay needs a position that answers the assigned issue. Develop a small number of relevant reasons with explanation and examples. A list of claims is not a developed argument. A useful example makes the reasoning visible; it does not need an invented statistic or a fictitious research citation.

A qualified position can be clear: support an action under stated conditions, or support part of a proposal while rejecting another part. A counterargument matters when it could change the judgement. Explain its significance and your response rather than adding “some people disagree” as a decorative sentence.

Current IELTS Academic Writing Task 2 guidance requires at least 250 words. The original model below exceeds that minimum, but word count does not establish quality or predict a band. Its paragraph structure is one workable choice, not a universal template to memorise. Real coursework must meet its own evidence and citation requirements.

### Language pattern

Position → developed reason → further reason or limitation → reasoned conclusion.

Make the judgement and its support easy to follow.

Limits: Do not invent research evidence or imply that the model has an examiner-awarded score.

### A complete original opinion essay

Context: Issue for the model: a university is considering making recordings of lecture introductions available alongside ordinary classes.

Universities should make recordings of lecture introductions available when they can do so responsibly. These recordings should support normal classes rather than replace opportunities to ask questions and discuss difficult ideas. Their value lies in improving access to the starting point of a lesson, while their limitations require careful attention.

One benefit is that students can return to unfamiliar terminology. A learner who understands an argument but misses one definition may struggle with everything that follows. A short recording allows that learner to revisit the definition before reading the course material. For example, a student encountering a new statistical term could listen again and then connect it to the worked example discussed in class. This is a practical use of repetition, not a reason to assume that watching alone produces understanding.

Recordings can also help students prepare for discussion. If an introduction explains the purpose and basic vocabulary of a topic, classroom time can be used to examine examples and address uncertainty. However, this arrangement works only when students can access the recording and the introduction is clear enough to use independently. Captions and a readable summary can help students who cannot easily use audio in their current setting.

There are also limits. A recording cannot respond to an unexpected misunderstanding, and a student may feel familiar with the language without being able to use the idea. Teachers therefore still need opportunities to explain, discuss and respond. The university should also make clear which material is available and how students are expected to use it.

In conclusion, recorded introductions can be a useful addition to university teaching when they are accessible and connected to the rest of the course. They should provide a reliable beginning for learning, while the wider teaching process continues to support explanation and discussion.

Why it works:

- The opening states support with conditions.

- The second paragraph develops one reason through a concrete example.

- The third discusses preparation and access.

- The fourth considers an important limitation.

- The conclusion preserves the original qualified position.

The argument is supported by explained reasoning and hypothetical examples, not fabricated research results.

### Make a position precise

Context: Draft position: “Recordings are good and bad and people have different opinions.”

Recorded introductions are useful as an accessible supplement, but they should not replace opportunities for discussion.

Why it works:

- Useful identifies the judgement.

- As an accessible supplement states the condition.

- The final clause makes the limit explicit.

A qualified position can be more precise than an absolute yes or no.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: A longer essay always earns a higher score.
  Revision: Develop a relevant, accurate response that meets the task requirements.
  Reason: Length beyond the minimum is not proof of quality.

- Draft: Research shows 95% of students benefit.
  Revision: A student may benefit by revisiting unfamiliar terminology.
  Reason: Do not invent a statistic or a source to strengthen the argument.

### Keep in mind

- State a position that answers the issue.

- Explain how each example supports the reason.

- Address meaningful limitations.

## ENG-14 · Use evidence without overstating it

Compare sources according to what each can establish and state the limits of a conclusion.

### Explanation

A claim needs evidence relevant to that claim. Preferences can support a statement about what respondents like; they cannot by themselves establish an effect on performance. A measured association can be useful, but alternative explanations may remain. Name the population, conditions and outcome before deciding how strong a conclusion should be.

When several sources discuss a topic, organise the writing around the issue rather than giving one unrelated paragraph per source. Explain where the sources agree, what each contributes and why their limits matter. Disagreement can arise because they measured different outcomes or used different participants.

The two records below are fictional teaching data. In a real assignment, use traceable sources, inspect the relevant sections and follow the required citation style. A plausible title, quotation or publication date must never be invented to make a paragraph appear researched.

### Language pattern

Claim + relevant evidence + explanation of the link + limitation.

Show why the evidence bears on the claim.

Limits: Combining weak evidence does not automatically produce a strong conclusion.

### Combine two kinds of information

Context: Fictional training record A: 12 of 18 volunteers preferred a quiet study room. Record B: observers counted 24 occupied seats in that room and 20 in a discussion area on one Tuesday at 14:00.

The training records suggest interest in the quiet room, but they describe different outcomes. Record A reports a preference among a small volunteer group. Record B records occupancy at one time. Together they can inform a decision to investigate demand further; they do not demonstrate that the room improves grades or that demand is always higher.

Why it works:

- The paragraph distinguishes preference from occupancy.

- The sample and time limits are preserved.

- The proposed next decision is proportionate to the evidence.

A useful synthesis can explain what remains unknown.

### Avoid a causal leap

Context: An invented observation finds that students who attend a study group also tend to submit more assignments.

Study-group attendance was associated with more frequent assignment submission in this observation. The result does not establish whether attendance caused the difference, because other factors may have influenced both.

Why it works:

- Associated reports a relationship without asserting cause.

- The outcome is assignment submission, not grades.

- The limitation gives a reason for cautious interpretation.

The wording preserves useful information while acknowledging uncertainty.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: Both sources mention the room, so they prove the same thing.
  Revision: Identify what each source actually measured.
  Reason: A shared topic does not imply identical evidence.

- Draft: A source is credible because its title sounds academic.
  Revision: Check the source, method and relevance to the specific claim.
  Reason: Presentation alone does not establish reliability.

### Keep in mind

- Match evidence to the exact claim.

- Keep methods and outcomes distinct.

- Use real, traceable references.

## ENG-15 · Revise meaning before polishing words

Improve a paragraph in a sequence that protects meaning, organisation and accuracy.

### Explanation

Revision and proofreading address different levels. Revision asks whether the text answers its purpose, develops the right ideas and uses valid evidence. Proofreading checks sentence-level details such as agreement, punctuation and spelling. Correcting spelling first can leave a polished paragraph that still makes an unsupported claim.

Review in a useful order: purpose and coverage; logic and paragraph order; facts and numerical values; sentence meaning; grammar and punctuation. Read the final version again to ensure that a local edit has not changed a number or removed an important condition. A checklist helps attention, but it does not certify that the writing is error-free.

Editing should preserve the author’s intended meaning when that meaning is supportable. When a claim is unsupported, revise the claim or find suitable evidence rather than merely replacing a strong verb with another impressive verb. Clear writing often results from deleting a repetition and naming an ambiguous reference.

### Language pattern

Purpose → structure → evidence → sentence clarity → final proofreading.

Fix the largest problem before the smallest one.

Limits: The sequence is a practical aid, not an automated quality or grade assessment.

### An annotated complete revision

Context: Invented draft: “The survey prove the room is perfect. 12 students like it. This means grades increased. It is very useful and useful.” Context: 18 volunteers answered a preference survey; 12 preferred the room; grades were not measured.

In a preference survey of 18 volunteers, 12 preferred the room. This result suggests that the space was useful to some respondents, but the survey did not measure grades. Further feedback would help explain which features they valued.

Why it works:

- The opening restores the missing denominator and volunteer context.

- Preferred reports the measured preference accurately.

- The unsupported claim about grades is removed.

- The repetition is replaced with a relevant next information need.

The revised paragraph is stronger because its claim is more defensible, not because its words are longer.

### Repair a dangling opening phrase

Context: Draft: “After checking the graph, the error became clear.” The intended checker was the student.

After checking the graph, the student identified the error.

Why it works:

- The opening action now belongs to the subject that follows.

- The student supplies the missing actor.

- The revision describes what became clear through an explicit action.

An introductory phrase should attach to the intended subject.

### Common mistakes

- Draft: The spelling is correct, so the argument must be correct.
  Revision: Check the reasoning and evidence separately from spelling.
  Reason: Different kinds of quality require different checks.

- Draft: Replace every repeated technical term with a synonym.
  Revision: Repeat a precise technical term when a synonym could change its meaning.
  Reason: Consistency can help the reader follow an argument.

### Keep in mind

- Revise the claim before polishing its surface.

- Recheck facts after rewriting.

- Preserve clear reference and consistent terminology.
