Scientific Inquiry and Reading Questions

Core Principles

  • A hypothesis is a testable explanation; a prediction is an expected observation if it is correct. A scientific theory is a well-supported explanatory framework, not an everyday guess.
  • A controlled experiment changes one independent variable and measures a dependent response while holding other factors as constant as possible. A control group provides a comparison.
  • Replication and an appropriate sample reduce the influence of chance. Correlation alone does not establish causation; design a test that separates alternative explanations.
  • Graphs usually place the independent variable on the horizontal axis and the dependent variable on the vertical axis. Check units, sample size, and error bars before concluding.
  • Limit conclusions to measured data. A result that conflicts with a prediction weakens the specific hypothesis; it does not invalidate all of science.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
Best experimental designUse comparable groups, change one factor, and specify the response measure, control, and replication.
A claim that X causes Y based only on correlationThe evidence is insufficient; a third factor or reverse causation may explain the association.
Graph lacks units or a controlState the limit on comparison and identify the missing information rather than inventing it.

Worked example. To test the effect of light on plant growth, keep species, water, soil, and trial duration the same. Vary light intensity alone, then measure mass gain or height in replicated groups.

Chemistry of Life and Biological Molecules

Core Principles

  • An element is a type of atom. Valence electrons influence bonding. Covalent bonds share electrons; ionic attraction follows electron transfer. Hydrogen bonds are weaker individually but important collectively.
  • Water is polar and forms hydrogen bonds. This explains cohesion, adhesion, resistance to temperature change, and why ice is less dense than liquid water.
  • Polar and ionic substances tend to dissolve in water; nonpolar substances tend to avoid it. In a membrane, hydrophilic phospholipid heads face outward and hydrophobic tails face inward.
  • A lower pH means a higher H⁺ concentration. A change of one pH unit means a tenfold change in H⁺ concentration. Buffers limit changes in pH.
  • Many macromolecules form when small units join with water released and break down when water is added. Do not treat all lipids as repeating polymers like proteins or nucleic acids.
  • Carbohydrates provide energy and structure: starch stores glucose in plants, glycogen stores it in animals, and cellulose provides structure. Triglycerides store energy; phospholipids form membranes.
  • A protein's amino acid sequence influences its folding and function. Changes in pH or temperature can alter shape and activity without breaking every covalent bond.
  • DNA stores information in its base sequence; RNA helps transfer and use that information. A nucleotide consists of a sugar, phosphate group, and nitrogenous base.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
A substance does not dissolve in water and enters the membrane coreThink of a nonpolar or lipid-like substance, not a free ion or typical sugar.
pH changes from 5 to 3Acidity increases; H⁺ concentration becomes 100 times higher.
A protein loses activity after heatingFirst consider altered folding and active-site shape, not loss of the amino acid sequence.
A molecule stores genetic informationLook for DNA and its nucleotide base sequence, not fatty acid order.
Ice floatsHydrogen-bonded structure makes ice less dense than liquid water.

Worked example. Solution A has pH 4 and solution B has pH 7. The difference is three units, so A has 10³, or 1,000, times the H⁺ concentration of B. Do not say that A has the higher pH.

Cells, Membranes, Transport, and Communication

Core Principles

  • Prokaryotic cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus; eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Both have a membrane, cytoplasm, DNA, and ribosomes.
  • Ribosomes make proteins. Rough ER starts secreted and membrane proteins; the Golgi modifies and sorts them; vesicles carry them; lysosomes digest materials.
  • Mitochondria are major sites of aerobic respiration; chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis in plants and algae. The cytoskeleton supports shape, movement, and internal transport.
  • The membrane is a fluid mosaic of a phospholipid bilayer and proteins. Selective permeability does not mean that every substance crosses in the same way.
  • Net diffusion moves down a concentration gradient. Facilitated diffusion uses a channel or carrier without directly consuming ATP; active transport moves against a gradient and requires energy.
  • Osmosis moves water across a selectively permeable membrane toward the side with more nonpenetrating solute. Animal cells may burst in a hypotonic solution or shrink in a hypertonic one; plant cells gain turgor in a hypotonic solution.

Read the diagram. Compare animal and plant cells in isotonic, hypotonic, and hypertonic solutions. Follow the water arrows, then predict whether the cell swells, stays firm, or shrinks.

  • Endocytosis and exocytosis move large cargo in vesicles. Do not confuse them with a small molecule passing through a channel.
  • Cell communication requires a signal, receptor, and response. A hormone often travels through body fluids, but only cells with a suitable receptor and working response pathway can react.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
A hypertonic solution surrounds a cellWater leaves. An animal cell shrinks; a plant cell loses turgor.
A substance moves against its gradientLook for a pump, energy source, or coupling mechanism; it is not facilitated diffusion.
A protein is secretedTrace rough ER ribosome → Golgi → vesicle → plasma membrane.
A cell has a wall, chloroplasts, and a large vacuoleIt is a plant cell; the wall does not replace its selective membrane.
All cells encounter a hormone but only some respondResponsive cells have the appropriate receptor and downstream pathway.

Worked example. Place cells in pure water. Water enters by osmosis. An animal cell may lyse; a plant cell becomes turgid because its wall resists expansion.

Cell Cycle, Mitosis, and Meiosis

Core Principles

  • Interphase includes G1 for growth, S for DNA replication, and G2 for preparation. DNA replication doubles DNA amount but does not turn a diploid cell into a tetraploid cell.
  • In mitosis, sister chromatids separate. The result is usually two genetically similar cells with the original chromosome number; mitosis supports growth, repair, and some asexual reproduction.
  • In meiosis I, homologous chromosomes separate. In meiosis II, sister chromatids separate. The usual result is four genetically varied haploid cells.
  • Crossing over between nonsister chromatids of homologous chromosomes occurs in prophase I. Independent orientation of homologous pairs at metaphase I provides another source of variation.

Read the diagram. Trace one parent cell down each branch. Mitosis preserves chromosome number in two cells; meiosis separates homologs first and sister chromatids second, producing four haploid cells.

  • Cell-cycle checkpoints stop progression when DNA is damaged or division requirements are unmet. Loss of control can contribute to tumor formation.
  • Nondisjunction in meiosis may produce gametes with abnormal chromosome numbers. Identify the stage of the error before predicting the products.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
Homologous chromosomes separateMeiosis I, not mitosis or meiosis II.
Sister chromatids separateMitosis or meiosis II; use ploidy and context to distinguish them.
DNA doubles but chromosome number does notS phase; each chromosome now has two sister chromatids.
Four varied haploid cells formMeiosis; crossing over and independent assortment create variation.
New somatic cells match the parent cellMitosis, unless a mutation is specified.

Worked example. A human cell with 2n = 46 produces two cells with 46 chromosomes each by mitosis. Meiosis produces gametes with n = 23; fertilization restores 2n = 46.

Enzymes, Energy, Respiration, and Photosynthesis

Core Principles

  • An enzyme lowers activation energy and speeds the approach to equilibrium. It does not change the equilibrium position or make an energetically unfavorable reaction favorable by itself. Active-site shape matters.
  • Temperature, pH, substrate concentration, and inhibitors affect enzyme rate. Once an enzyme is saturated, more substrate alone adds little speed; excessive heat may disrupt protein structure.
  • ATP is a short-term energy carrier. Removing its terminal phosphate can drive cellular work; ATP is regenerated from ADP and phosphate using energy from food or light.
  • Glycolysis in the cytosol converts one glucose to two pyruvate molecules. It invests 2 ATP and produces 4 ATP, for a net gain of 2 ATP plus NADH; oxygen is not directly required.
  • Aerobic respiration continues fuel oxidation through the citric acid cycle and electron transport chain. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor, and oxidative phosphorylation makes most ATP.

Read the diagram. Electrons move through the membrane complexes, which pump protons. Proton flow back through ATP synthase drives ATP formation; oxygen accepts electrons at the end.

  • Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue without oxygen. Yeast may produce ethanol and CO₂; animal muscle cells can produce lactate. Its ATP yield is far below full aerobic respiration.
  • Light reactions on thylakoid membranes use light and water to produce O₂, ATP, and NADPH. The released oxygen comes from water.
  • The Calvin cycle in the stroma fixes carbon from CO₂ using ATP and NADPH. The carbon in sugar does not come from light or water.
  • Plants both photosynthesize and respire. Photosynthesis in daylight does not switch off cellular respiration.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
An inhibitor or heat changes enzyme activityExamine the active site and reaction rate, not a change in energy conservation.
Where is most ATP from glucose made?During aerobic oxidative phosphorylation; glycolysis yields only 2 net ATP.
O₂ is absent but a little ATP is still madeGlycolysis plus fermentation can regenerate NAD⁺; the aerobic citric acid cycle is not running normally.
Trace the oxygen released by photosynthesisIt comes from water split during the light reactions.
Trace the carbon in newly made sugarIt comes from CO₂ fixed in the Calvin cycle.
A plant is kept in darknessLight-driven photosynthesis stops, but respiration can continue if fuel and O₂ are available.

Worked example. If the electron transport chain stops for lack of O₂, aerobic ATP production falls sharply. Some cells can maintain glycolysis by using fermentation to regenerate NAD⁺.

DNA, Gene Expression, and Biotechnology

Core Principles

  • DNA has two antiparallel strands: A pairs with T, and G with C. Base order carries information. Replication is semiconservative: each new molecule contains one old and one newly made strand.

Read the diagram. Track the original strands into the two daughter molecules. Each daughter double helix contains one parental strand and one newly synthesized strand.

  • Replication enzymes separate strands and synthesize new DNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction. One strand is continuous and the other forms fragments joined by ligase; high accuracy does not mean zero mutation.
  • Transcription makes RNA complementary to a DNA template. In eukaryotes, it begins in the nucleus and mRNA is processed before export. Ribosomes translate the mRNA in three-base codons.
  • AUG is the usual start codon and specifies methionine. Stop codons do not specify amino acids. Each tRNA carries an amino acid and pairs its anticodon with an mRNA codon.
  • A base substitution may be silent, change an amino acid, or create an early stop. An insertion or deletion not divisible by three can shift the reading frame. Effects depend on position and context.
  • Gene regulation controls when and how much RNA or protein a cell makes. Cells can share nearly the same DNA while expressing different genes. Bacteria and eukaryotes regulate expression in different ways.
  • A virus is not a cell and needs a host cell to copy its genome and make components. Its genetic material can be DNA or RNA. Antibiotics do not treat a viral infection directly.
  • Biotechnology uses tools to analyze DNA. PCR amplifies a chosen segment; gel electrophoresis separates fragments by migration; sequencing reveals base order. Interpret results using controls.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
A DNA template is given 3′ to 5′Write complementary mRNA 5′ to 3′; use U in RNA where the matching DNA sequence uses T.
One amino acid changesCheck for a base substitution and codon change; do not assume a frame shift.
All downstream codons change after a mutationSuspect an insertion or deletion not divisible by three.
Two cells share DNA but have different functionsLook for differences in gene expression and regulation.
An antibiotic fails against a pathogenCheck whether the pathogen is viral and whether the drug has a relevant target.
A DNA band travels farther from the gel wellIt is often a smaller fragment in standard separation; check migration direction and size ladder.

Worked example. A DNA template 3′-TAC GGA CTT ATT-5′ gives mRNA 5′-AUG CCU GAA UAA-3′. Translation yields methionine, proline, glutamate, then stop. Do not count the stop codon as an amino acid.

Mendelian Genetics and Probability

Core Principles

  • Genotype is the allele combination; phenotype is the observed trait. Dominance describes the heterozygote's appearance, not how common or biologically strong an allele is.
  • Segregation separates a gene's two alleles during gamete formation. Independent assortment applies to unlinked genes or genes far enough apart; do not assume it for closely linked genes.
  • With complete dominance, Aa × Aa gives a 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa genotypic ratio and a 3 dominant : 1 recessive phenotypic ratio. Aa × aa gives 1 : 1 in a testcross.
  • Incomplete dominance gives an intermediate heterozygous phenotype; codominance displays effects of both alleles. ABO blood groups involve multiple alleles and codominance of A and B.
  • Polygenic traits often show continuous variation. Pleiotropy means one gene affects multiple traits. The environment can change phenotype without changing genotype.
  • A male with a recessive X-linked allele often expresses it because he has one X chromosome. A father gives his X chromosome to daughters, not sons; trace pedigrees accordingly.
  • A trait in every generation may suggest dominance; an affected child of unaffected parents may suggest recessive inheritance if assumptions hold. A small pedigree alone rarely proves the mode.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
Aa × Aa with complete dominanceStart with 1 : 2 : 1 genotypes, then combine AA and Aa for the 3 : 1 phenotypes.
Two independent traits must occur togetherCalculate each probability and multiply; do not assume independence if genes are linked.
An affected father and a son in X-linked recessive inheritanceThe father gives his son Y, not X; examine the mother's X chromosome.
An affected child has unaffected parentsTest a recessive model with two carriers before invoking a new mutation.
A 1 : 2 : 1 phenotypic ratioConsider incomplete dominance or codominance rather than assuming 3 : 1.

Worked example. For Aa × Aa, P(recessive child) = 1/4. For two successive recessive children, multiply: 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16. For one recessive and one nonrecessive child in either order, 2 x (1/4) x (3/4) = 3/8.

Evolution and Population Change

Core Principles

  • Evolution at the population level is change in allele frequencies across generations. An individual does not automatically inherit a useful change acquired in response to need.
  • Natural selection needs heritable variation and differences in survival or reproduction. Evolutionary fitness means relative reproductive success, not muscular strength alone.
  • Mutation creates new alleles; it does not arise intentionally to meet an organism's need. Sexual reproduction reshuffles alleles, while selection changes frequencies when effects differ.
  • Genetic drift is random change that has a stronger effect in small populations. A founder effect starts a population from a small sample; a bottleneck sharply reduces an existing population.
  • Gene flow transfers alleles between populations through migrants or their gametes. It may reduce differences between populations and is distinct from mutation.
  • Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium is a reference model requiring a large population, random mating, and no mutation, migration, or selection. Departure suggests at least one assumption is unmet.
  • Speciation involves reduced gene exchange and accumulated differences leading to reproductive isolation. A visible difference alone does not prove that a new species has formed.
  • An evolutionary tree is a hypothesis about ancestry based on shared derived traits and other evidence. Tip order alone does not show closest relationship; compare recent common ancestors.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
Pesticide resistance becomes commonHeritable variation existed; resistant individuals survived and reproduced. The pesticide did not intentionally create the needed mutation.
A small population survives a disasterConsider a bottleneck and genetic drift.
Individuals move between populations and reproduceGene flow occurs when their alleles enter the new population.
The recessive aa frequency is 0.09 at equilibriumThat is q². Take its square root before finding p and 2pq.
Two evolutionary trees have different tip ordersCompare nodes and most recent common ancestors, not positions on the page.

Worked example. If 9% of a population is recessive aa under equilibrium, q² = 0.09, so q = 0.3 and p = 0.7. The heterozygote frequency is 2pq = 0.42, or 42%. Do not set q = 0.09.

Classification and Diversity of Life

Core Principles

  • The taxonomic hierarchy from broad to narrow is domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. A binomial name gives genus followed by species.
  • Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes without a membrane-bound nucleus or organelles. Their small size does not make them evolutionarily identical.
  • Protists are diverse eukaryotes that may photosynthesize, prey on others, or live as parasites. The name does not imply one feeding strategy.
  • Fungi are eukaryotes that absorb food after external digestion and usually have chitin cell walls. Plants usually photosynthesize and have cellulose cell walls.
  • Animals are multicellular and generally ingest food. Vertebrates are one animal branch; invertebrates are not a single uniform evolutionary group.
  • Land plants evolved traits that reduce water loss and permit reproduction on land. Vascular tissue, seeds, and flowers distinguish major branches.
  • Life's history is branching, not a straight ladder from lower to higher organisms. Living lineages share ancestors and then diverge.

Read the diagram. Read each fork as a shared ancestor. The platypus, kangaroo, and beaver form the mammal branch; the iguana is the outgroup. Branch position is not a ladder of progress.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
An organism lacks a membrane-bound nucleusStart with prokaryotes; microscopic size alone does not make it a protist.
Chitin wall and nutrient absorptionLikely a fungus; cellulose wall and chloroplasts suggest a plant.
Chloroplasts in a single eukaryotic cellIt may be a photosynthetic protist, not automatically a land plant.
Closest relatives in a phylogenetic treeFind the most recent shared node supported by traits, not superficial resemblance.

Worked example. A single aquatic cell with a nucleus that photosynthesizes may be a photosynthetic protist. Compare that possibility before choosing a bacterium or multicellular land plant.

Plant Structure, Transport, and Responses

Core Principles

  • Nonvascular plants rely more on water for gamete movement. The appearance of vascular tissue, then seeds, then flowers and fruits changed plant life on land. A seed is not a spore.
  • Gymnosperms have seeds not enclosed in an ovary that becomes fruit. Angiosperms have flowers and form fruits after fertilization.
  • Roots anchor plants and absorb water and minerals; stems support leaves and conduct materials; leaves are major sites of photosynthesis and gas exchange.
  • Xylem usually carries water and minerals upward from roots through processes linked to transpiration and cohesion. Phloem transports sugars from source to sink; direction depends on location.
  • Stomata admit CO₂ but also lose water. Guard cells control their opening. Drought may close stomata, reducing water loss and potentially reducing CO₂ uptake.
  • Apical meristems drive lengthwise growth; lateral meristems produce thickening in some plants. New growth is not merely expansion of old cells everywhere.
  • Auxin can promote stem-cell elongation and phototropism; greater action on the shaded side bends a stem toward light. Other plant hormones regulate germination, ripening, and stress responses.
  • Plant life cycles alternate a haploid gametophyte and diploid sporophyte. Which stage dominates differs among plant groups.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
Water and minerals rise from rootsUse xylem, not sugar-carrying phloem.
Sugar moves from leaf to root or fruitUse phloem from source to sink; transport is not always upward.
Wilting is accompanied by closed stomataBalance water conservation against reduced CO₂ uptake.
A stem bends toward lightGreater elongation on the shaded side follows unequal auxin action.
Seeds are enclosed in fruitAngiosperm; exposed seeds on a cone suggest gymnosperm.

Worked example. Label sugar in a leaf and find it later in a growing fruit: the leaf is a source, the fruit a sink, and phloem is the likely route. Label root-absorbed water: its main route is xylem.

Animal Systems and Homeostasis

Core Principles

  • Structural organization proceeds from cell to tissue, organ, and organ system. Homeostasis keeps variables within a range through feedback; negative feedback counteracts a deviation.
  • Efficient exchange needs a large, thin surface near transport fluid. Lung alveoli exchange O₂ and CO₂ by diffusion along partial-pressure gradients, not an ATP pump for each gas molecule.
  • An artery carries blood away from the heart; a vein returns it. Direction of flow, not oxygen content, defines the name. Capillaries exchange substances with tissues.
  • In human circulation: right heart → lungs → left heart → body → right heart. Red-cell hemoglobin carries most O₂. More breathing alone does not guarantee more O₂ delivery to every tissue.

Read the diagram. Follow both loops: the pulmonary circuit connects heart and lungs, while the systemic circuit connects heart and body. Arrow direction defines the route.

  • Digestion breaks large molecules into absorbable products. Most nutrient absorption occurs in the small intestine. The liver and pancreas add important secretions; food does not pass through them as through gut tubes.
  • Water and salt regulation protects cell volume and body-fluid concentration. Distinguish excretion from osmotic balance and trace water and solute movement before explaining a result.
  • Hormones travel through blood to cells with suitable receptors. Insulin helps lower high blood glucose; glucagon helps raise low blood glucose. Their effects are not in the same direction.
  • A neuron receives and transmits signals across synapses; communication between neurons is often chemical. Myelin increases conduction speed in certain axons.
  • Skeletal muscles move bones by contracting, often in opposing pairs. A nerve does not mechanically push a muscle; it triggers contraction.
  • Innate immunity is relatively rapid and broad. Adaptive immunity is more specific and builds memory. B cells make antibodies; T cells can help responses or kill infected cells.
  • Vaccination presents an antigen or its instructions in a way that lowers disease risk and supports immune memory. It does not directly kill every pathogen the instant it enters the body.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
A vessel leaves the heart with low-O₂ bloodIt may be a pulmonary artery; naming depends on flow away from the heart.
A variable rises and a response restores itNegative feedback; identify receptor, control center, and effector if asked.
Blood glucose rises after a mealExpect greater insulin action; when glucose falls, expect greater glucagon action.
A rapid general response precedes specific memoryThe first is innate, the later response adaptive.
Specific antibodies are producedLook for B or plasma cells; cytotoxic T cells often kill infected cells.
Gas moves between alveolus and bloodStart with diffusion gradient, area, and barrier thickness.

Worked example. At 70 beats/min and 70 mL/beat, cardiac output is 4,900 mL/min, or about 4.9 L/min. If heart rate alone rises, do not assume the new output without knowing stroke volume.

Reproduction and Development

Core Principles

  • Meiosis produces haploid gametes. Their union at fertilization restores a diploid zygote. Do not confuse meiosis with the subsequent mitotic growth of an embryo.
  • Sexual reproduction reshuffles alleles and increases offspring variation. Asexual reproduction usually yields more similar offspring, though mutations can still occur.
  • In humans, signals from the hypothalamus, pituitary, and ovaries or testes interact. Hormone changes regulate gamete production, ovulation timing, and the uterine lining.
  • After fertilization, early cell divisions are followed by germ-layer differentiation and organ formation. More cells do not mean that each cell gains more chromosomes or a different original genome.
  • The placenta exchanges materials, gases, and signals between mother and fetus. This does not usually mean that their blood mixes directly.
  • Assisted reproductive methods can help in particular cases, but claims about success or risk need comparison groups and controlled factors, not one case.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
n + n gametes uniteThe product is a 2n zygote; its later growth uses mitosis.
Genetically varied gametes formLook for meiosis, crossing over, and independent assortment.
Two tissues differ within one embryoDifferentiation and gene expression explain distinct functions despite a common origin.
O₂ or nutrients reach a fetusDescribe exchange across the placenta and two circulations, not literal mixing of maternal and fetal blood.

Worked example. If an embryo begins as a zygote with 2n = 46, its somatic cells normally retain 46 chromosomes after mitosis. Gametes it produces later have n = 23.

Ecology, Populations, and Ecosystems

Core Principles

  • Ecology studies interactions among organisms and with nonliving factors. Climate, soil, water, and light help determine biomes; location name alone is not enough.
  • A population is one species in one place and time. A community contains populations of several species. An ecosystem also includes nonliving factors, energy flow, and material cycles.
  • Population size changes through births, deaths, immigration, and emigration. J-shaped exponential growth temporarily assumes abundant resources; S-shaped logistic growth slows near carrying capacity K.
  • Density-dependent factors such as competition and disease often intensify as populations crowd. Weather disturbance can act regardless of density.
  • Predation, competition, mutualism, and parasitism affect partners differently. Identify each partner's effect as positive, negative, or neutral rather than relying only on the interaction name.
  • Energy moves from producers through consumers while much is lost as heat and life processes. Chemical elements cycle between living and nonliving components.

Read the diagram. Follow arrows from food to consumer. One organism can take part in several feeding paths, so a food web cannot always be reduced to one chain or one fixed trophic level.

  • A teaching model estimates about 10% energy transfer between trophic levels, but actual efficiency often varies roughly from 5% to 20%. Do not treat 10% as a universal constant.
  • Primary productivity is organic matter made by producers per area and time. Comparing systems requires units and total area; open ocean productivity is low per square meter but covers a vast area.
  • Climate change can alter a species' range, seasonal activity, and interactions. Temperature change alone does not prove a particular species has gone extinct without supporting data.

Question Clues

If the question mentionsStart with this decision
A J-shaped curve under temporarily rich conditionsExponential growth; numerical increase accelerates as N rises.
An S-shaped curve levels offCarrying capacity K and limiting factors; net growth approaches zero.
Disease spreads faster in a crowded populationA density-dependent factor.
Producers hold 1,000 energy unitsA teaching estimate is about 100 at the next level and 10 at the next, with variable efficiency.
Energy versus matterEnergy flows and dissipates as heat; chemical elements cycle.
Low production per square meter over a vast areaTotal contribution may still be large; multiply rate by area when comparing.

Worked example. A population starts at 200, has 30 births and 10 deaths, gains 5 immigrants and loses 15 emigrants: 200 + 30 - 10 + 5 - 15 = 210. If producers contain 1,000 energy units, the teaching estimate for primary consumers is about 100.

Quick Distinctions Before Choosing an Answer

When answer choices look similar, find the deciding evidence in the question or figure before applying the rule.

If you confuseLook for the deciding evidence
Correlation vs causationWas a variable isolated using a control and replication? Association alone does not establish cause.
Covalent vs hydrogen bondAre electrons shared between atoms, or is there attraction involving polar groups?
Diffusion vs active transportDoes the substance move down or against its gradient? Movement against it needs energy or coupling.
Mitosis vs meiosisDoes chromosome number fall, and do homologs separate before sister chromatids?
Glycolysis ATP vs total respiration ATPIs the question about glycolysis alone? Its net yield is 2 ATP per glucose.
Light reactions vs Calvin cycleIs the process releasing O₂ and making ATP and NADPH, or fixing CO₂ into carbohydrate?
DNA replication vs transcription vs translationWhat is the template and product: new DNA, RNA, or an amino acid chain?
Genotype vs phenotypeIs the question asking for allele combination or observed trait? Their ratios need not match.
Mutation vs natural selectionDid new variation arise, or did the frequency of existing variation change across generations?
Xylem vs phloemIs the cargo root-derived water and minerals, or sugar moving from a source to a sink?
Negative vs positive feedbackDoes the response weaken the initial change or amplify it?
Population size vs growth rateIs the question about N at one time or the change in N over time and its sources?
Energy flow vs matter cyclingDoes energy move and dissipate as heat, or is an element such as carbon recycled?
