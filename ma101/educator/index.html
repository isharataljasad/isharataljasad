Functions

01

Functions and their graphs

Meaning

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Its domain is the allowed input set; its range is the outputs actually attained. A graph passes the vertical line test precisely when it represents a function of x.

Method

Read a graph in this order: domain, range, intercepts, sign, increasing or decreasing intervals, then symmetry. Compute f(−x): equality with f(x) means even symmetry; equality with −f(x) means odd symmetry. Neither relation may hold. For a formula, exclude zero denominators and negative radicands of even roots.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=√(4−x²), require 4−x²≥0, so the domain is [−2,2]. Outputs run from 0 to 2. Since f(−x)=f(x), the upper semicircle is even.

Additional worked example

Find the domain and symmetry of g(x)=x/(x²−1).

Exclude x=−1 and x=1. Since g(−x)=−g(x), the function is odd on its symmetric domain.

Common error. A vertical line crossing twice disproves function status; a horizontal line crossing twice does not.

02

Composition of functions

Meaning

Composition feeds the output of one function into another. Order matters, and the domain must survive both stages.

Method

Identify the inner operation first, then the outer operation. For the domain of f∘g, require x in the domain of g and g(x) in the domain of f. To unpack a complicated expression, peel off the outermost action one layer at a time.

Worked reasoning

If f(u)=√u and g(x)=x−3, then (f∘g)(x)=√(x−3), defined for x≥3. But (g∘f)(x)=√x−3, defined for x≥0; the outputs and domains differ.

Additional worked example

Write h(x)=ln(1+x²) as a composition and state its domain.

Take g(x)=1+x² and f(u)=ln u. Then h=f∘g. Because 1+x²>0 for every real x, the domain is all real numbers.

Common error. Do not cancel the distinction between f(g(x)) and g(f(x)); test each domain from inside outward.

Limits

03

Average and instantaneous change

Meaning

Average change is the slope of a secant line across an interval. Instantaneous change is the limiting secant slope at one input, if that limit exists.

Method

Compute output change divided by input change and keep units: output units per input unit. To approach an instantaneous rate, replace the interval by [a,a+h], simplify while h≠0, and take h→0. The tangent slope can be positive, zero, or negative.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=x² at x=2, the secant slope over [2,2+h] is [(2+h)²−4]/h=4+h. Its limit is 4.

Additional worked example

Find the average rate of f(x)=1/x on [2,4].

[(1/4)−(1/2)]/(4−2)=−1/8. The negative sign records decreasing output as x increases.

Common error. Never treat an average rate over a finite interval as the rate at either endpoint.

04

Investigating a limit

Meaning

A limit describes what outputs approach near an input, not necessarily the output at that input. Both one-sided approaches must agree for a finite two-sided limit.

Method

Use a graph to see approach direction and a table of nearby inputs on both sides to estimate. Then seek an algebraic argument: numerical evidence alone can mislead near a sharp feature. Separate a hole, jump, vertical asymptote, and oscillation.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=(x²−4)/(x−2) with x≠2, nearby outputs approach 4 because f(x)=x+2 away from 2. The value f(2) remains undefined; the limit is 4.

Additional worked example

If f(x)=−1 for x<0 and f(x)=2 for x≥0, what is lim as x→0 of f(x)?

The left limit is −1 and the right limit is 2; the two-sided limit does not exist.

Common error. A table with values only on one side cannot establish a two-sided limit.

05

Algebraic limit methods

Meaning

Direct substitution works when the expression remains continuous. An apparent 0/0 signals algebra to perform, not a numerical answer.

Method

Factor and cancel a common nonzero factor near the point; rationalize radicals; or divide by the dominant power for behavior at infinity. For a trigonometric limit, rewrite it to expose sin(u)/u and account for any scale factor. Use one-sided signs to analyze a denominator tending to zero without cancellation.

Worked reasoning

lim as x→3 of (x²−9)/(x−3) equals lim(x+3)=6 after factoring. In contrast, lim as x→∞ of (3x²−1)/(2x²+5)=3/2 after division by x².

Additional worked example

Evaluate lim as x→0 of sin(5x)/x.

Rewrite as 5[sin(5x)/(5x)]; the inner ratio tends to 1, so the limit is 5.

Common error. Cancellation is legal for x near the target, not at the point where the original denominator is zero.

06

Formal definition of a limit

Meaning

The epsilon–delta definition makes “arbitrarily close” exact: every requested output tolerance ε>0 must admit an input tolerance δ>0.

Method

Start with |f(x)−L| and bound it by an expression containing |x−a|. Choose δ as a function of ε. When another factor varies with x, first keep x within a simple neighborhood such as |x−a|<1, then control the remaining factor.

Worked reasoning

To prove lim as x→3 of (2x+1)=7, observe |(2x+1)−7|=2|x−3|. Choosing δ=ε/2 makes 0<|x−3|<δ imply the required bound.

Additional worked example

Choose δ for lim as x→4 of (3x−2)=10.

Because |(3x−2)−10|=3|x−4|, δ=ε/3 works for every ε>0.

Common error. δ may depend on ε, but a proof cannot choose ε after choosing a particular x.

07

Continuity and the intermediate value theorem

Meaning

Continuity at a means f(a) exists, the limit exists, and they agree. For a continuous function on [a,b], every value between f(a) and f(b) is attained somewhere inside.

Method

Check domain and one-sided behavior before invoking continuity. A hole can be repaired by assigning the limiting value; a jump generally cannot. For an existence question, identify a continuous interval and show the target lies between endpoint outputs.

Worked reasoning

Let f(x)=x³−x−1. It is continuous. Since f(1)=−1 and f(2)=5, some c in (1,2) satisfies f(c)=0, even without an explicit root.

Additional worked example

Can the intermediate value theorem prove that x²−2 has a root between 1 and 2?

Yes. The polynomial is continuous, and its endpoint values are −1 and 2, so zero lies between them.

Common error. The theorem guarantees existence; it does not give a formula or prove uniqueness.

Derivatives

08

Derivative from its limit definition

Meaning

The derivative is the limit of difference quotients and represents local slope or instantaneous rate. Differentiability requires a finite common limit from both sides.

Method

Substitute f(a+h), subtract f(a), simplify the numerator, cancel h while h≠0, then take h→0. For radicals, multiply by the conjugate. At a corner or cusp, compare one-sided slopes.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=√x and a>0, [√(a+h)−√a]/h becomes 1/[√(a+h)+√a] after rationalization. Hence f′(a)=1/(2√a).

Additional worked example

Use the definition to find the derivative of f(x)=x²+1 at x=3.

The quotient is [(3+h)²+1−10]/h=6+h; its limit is 6.

Common error. Substituting h=0 before simplifying creates 0/0 and loses the limiting slope.

09

Power, constant, and sum rules

Meaning

Power rules replace repeated limit calculations for polynomial, reciprocal, and radical powers wherever the expression is differentiable.

Method

Rewrite roots and reciprocals as exponents, differentiate each term, and retain constants as coefficients. A standalone constant has derivative zero. Check the original domain, since rewriting a negative power does not create a value at x=0.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=3x⁴−2√x+5/x, rewrite as 3x⁴−2x^(1/2)+5x^(−1). Then f′(x)=12x³−x^(−1/2)−5x^(−2), for x>0.

Additional worked example

Differentiate 4/x³−7x²+9.

Write 4x^(−3)−7x²+9. The derivative is −12x^(−4)−14x.

Common error. The exponent falls by one after multiplying by the original exponent; it does not simply fall by one.

10

Product rule

Meaning

Two changing factors contribute separately to the change in their product. A derivative of a product is generally not the product of derivatives.

Method

Label the factors before differentiating. Differentiate one while holding the other, then reverse and add. Expand first only when that makes the algebra simpler; both routes must agree.

Worked reasoning

For y=(x²+1)eˣ, y′=2xeˣ+(x²+1)eˣ=eˣ(x²+2x+1).

Additional worked example

Differentiate (x³−2)sin x.

3x² sin x+(x³−2)cos x.

Common error. Both product-rule terms must appear, even if one factor looks simple.

11

Quotient rule

Meaning

When a numerator and denominator both vary, their effects oppose one another in the derivative.

Method

Name numerator f and denominator g, write “denominator times numerator derivative minus numerator times denominator derivative,” and divide by g². Preserve parentheses in the numerator. If the denominator is a simple power, rewriting as a negative exponent may be shorter.

Worked reasoning

For y=(x²+1)/x, the quotient rule gives [2x·x−(x²+1)]/x²=(x²−1)/x². Rewriting y=x+1/x gives the same 1−1/x².

Additional worked example

Differentiate sin x / x, for x≠0.

[x cos x−sin x]/x².

Common error. The denominator is squared as a whole; changing the order in the numerator changes the sign.

12

Rates of change in context

Meaning

A derivative inherits context and units. Position yields velocity; velocity yields acceleration. Speed is |velocity|, so direction and speed are distinct.

Method

Write the physical quantity as a function of time, differentiate, evaluate at the requested time, then report units. For landing or a turning point, solve the relevant equation first and reject times outside the physical domain.

Worked reasoning

If s(t)=80−16t² feet, then v(t)=−32t feet/second. The object reaches ground when 80−16t²=0, so t=√5 seconds; its impact velocity is −32√5 feet/second.

Additional worked example

For s(t)=t³−6t² meters, find velocity and acceleration at t=2 seconds.

v(t)=3t²−12t, so v(2)=−12 m/s. a(t)=6t−12, so a(2)=0 m/s².

Common error. A negative velocity means direction, not negative speed.

13

Derivatives of trigonometric functions

Meaning

The basic trigonometric derivatives support repeated differentiation of oscillating quantities. These rules require angles measured in radians.

Method

Use the six basic derivatives, then apply product, quotient, or chain rules when trig functions are combined or composed. Derive tan x from sin x/cos x if the sec²x rule is forgotten.

Worked reasoning

For y=3sin x−2tan x, y′=3cos x−2sec²x. For y=sin(5x), the chain rule adds the inner derivative, giving 5cos(5x).

Additional worked example

Differentiate cot x+sec x.

−csc²x+sec x tan x.

Common error. The derivative of cos x is negative sin x; degree measure changes the formula.

14

Chain rule

Meaning

A change in x first changes the inner expression, which then changes the outer function. Multiply those rates.

Method

Mark the outer layer and inner layer. Differentiate the outer form while leaving the inner expression in place, then multiply by the derivative of the inner. For multiple nested layers, repeat from outside inward.

Worked reasoning

For y=(1+3x²)⁵, y′=5(1+3x²)⁴·6x=30x(1+3x²)⁴. The factor 6x comes from the inside.

Additional worked example

Differentiate e^(x³) and sin²(2x).

The derivatives are 3x²e^(x³) and 4sin(2x)cos(2x), respectively.

Common error. If an outer derivative looks correct but lacks the inner derivative, the chain rule is incomplete.

15

Inverse trigonometric derivatives

Meaning

An inverse trigonometric function returns an angle from a ratio. Its derivative is not the reciprocal of the corresponding trigonometric derivative.

Method

Choose the correct inverse derivative and apply the chain rule to its argument. Respect real-domain restrictions: arcsin u and arccos u require |u|≤1, and their derivatives fail at boundary points where the denominator vanishes.

Worked reasoning

For y=arctan(x²), y′=2x/(1+x⁴). The denominator uses the square of the full input x².

Additional worked example

Differentiate arcsin(3x) where |x|<1/3.

3/√(1−9x²).

Common error. The notation sin⁻¹x means arcsin x here, not 1/sin x.

16

Equation of a tangent line

Meaning

A tangent line passes through (a,f(a)) with slope f′(a). Its local agreement with the curve is first order.

Method

Identify the requested x-coordinate a, evaluate f(a), compute f′(a), and insert both into point–slope form. Verify that the result passes through the point and has a plausible slope.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=x² at a=3, the point is (3,9) and slope is 6. Thus y−9=6(x−3), or y=6x−9.

Additional worked example

Find the tangent line to y=eˣ at x=0.

The point is (0,1) and slope is 1, giving y=x+1.

Common error. The derivative value is the slope, not the y-coordinate of the tangency point.

17

Implicit differentiation

Meaning

A curve can define y without a simple explicit formula. Differentiate both sides with respect to x, treating y as a changing function of x.

Method

Whenever a differentiated term contains y, apply the chain rule and include y′. Collect every y′ term, factor, and solve. A resulting slope may depend on both x and y; evaluate it at a point only after finding the general relation.

Worked reasoning

For x²+y²=25, differentiation gives 2x+2yy′=0, so y′=−x/y when y≠0. At (3,4), the tangent slope is −3/4.

Additional worked example

For xy=6, find y′.

Product rule gives y+xy′=0, hence y′=−y/x where x≠0.

Common error. Differentiating y² as 2y rather than 2yy′ silently treats y as a constant.

18

Higher derivatives

Meaning

Repeated differentiation measures the change of a rate. For position, the second derivative is acceleration; in graphs, its sign indicates concavity.

Method

Differentiate one stage at a time and label each result. For periodic functions, find the derivative cycle before jumping to a high order. For e^(kx), each differentiation adds a factor k.

Worked reasoning

The derivative cycle of sin x is cos x, −sin x, −cos x, sin x. Hence the 101st derivative is cos x because 101 leaves remainder 1 upon division by 4.

Additional worked example

Find the fourth derivative of e^(2x).

Each step contributes a factor 2, so the result is 16e^(2x).

Common error. A second derivative is not the square of the first derivative.

19

Logarithmic and exponential derivatives

Meaning

Exponential differentiation reproduces the exponential while logarithmic differentiation converts products and powers into sums and coefficients.

Method

Use chain rule for an exponential or logarithm of an expression. For y=xˣ with x>0, take natural logs: ln y=x ln x; differentiate implicitly and multiply back by y. For a positive base a≠1, aˣ=e^(x ln a).

Worked reasoning

For y=xˣ, y′/y=ln x+1, so y′=xˣ(ln x+1). For ln(x²+1), the derivative is 2x/(x²+1).

Additional worked example

Differentiate 3^(2x).

The derivative is 2 ln(3)·3^(2x).

Common error. The derivative of ln|u| is u′/u only where u≠0; do not discard a chain factor.

20

Hyperbolic and inverse hyperbolic derivatives

Meaning

Hyperbolic functions are built from exponentials. Their derivatives resemble trig derivatives but some signs differ: (cosh x)′=sinh x, without a minus sign.

Method

Apply the basic derivative and then the chain rule. For inverse hyperbolic tangent, use (artanh u)′=u′/(1−u²), valid in the real interval |u|<1. Check the argument domain before evaluating.

Worked reasoning

For y=[artanh(x²)]³ on |x|<1, y′=3[artanh(x²)]²·2x/(1−x⁴).

Additional worked example

Differentiate sinh(3x)+cosh x.

3cosh(3x)+sinh x.

Common error. Do not import the minus sign from the derivative of ordinary cosine into hyperbolic cosine.

21

Related rates

Meaning

Related rates connect several quantities changing with time through one geometric or physical equation.

Method

Draw and label a diagram; write a relation between variables; differentiate with respect to time before inserting the instant’s measurements; solve for the requested rate and state units and direction. If the geometry changes width, a constant input volume rate need not produce a constant height rate.

Worked reasoning

For a sphere at radius 5 ft expanding at 0.1 ft/min, dV/dt=4π(5)²(0.1)=10π ft³/min.

Additional worked example

A circle radius grows at 2 cm/s. How quickly does area grow when radius is 3 cm?

A=πr², so dA/dt=2πr(dr/dt)=12π cm²/s.

Common error. Substituting r before differentiating can incorrectly turn a changing quantity into a constant.

22

Linear approximation and differentials

Meaning

Near a known point, the tangent line gives a simple estimate for a smooth curve. The approximation improves as x approaches a.

Method

Choose a nearby a where f(a) and f′(a) are easy. Set Δx=x−a and estimate Δf≈f′(a)Δx. Check whether the estimate’s direction is sensible and remember that the neglected curvature creates error.

Worked reasoning

For √1.08, use f(x)=√x at a=1. Then f(1)=1 and f′(1)=1/2, so √1.08≈1+(1/2)(0.08)=1.04.

Additional worked example

Estimate ∛8.12 using a=8.

For f(x)=x^(1/3), f(8)=2 and f′(8)=1/12. With Δx=0.12, the estimate is 2+0.12/12=2.01.

Common error. A tangent-line estimate is local; using a faraway a can make it poor.

Applications of derivatives

23

Absolute extrema on an interval

Meaning

An absolute maximum or minimum is the largest or smallest attained function value over a domain. A critical point is an interior point where f′=0 or f′ does not exist.

Method

For a continuous function on a closed interval, compute f at both endpoints and at every interior critical point; compare the outputs. On open or unbounded intervals, check limiting behavior and whether a bound is actually attained.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=x²−4x on [0,5], f′=2x−4 vanishes at x=2. Values are f(0)=0, f(2)=−4, f(5)=5. Thus the absolute minimum is −4 and maximum is 5.

Additional worked example

Find the absolute extrema of f(x)=x(4−x) on [0,4].

f′=4−2x=0 at x=2. The values are 0, 4, 0, so the maximum is 4 at x=2 and minimum is 0 at both endpoints.

Common error. A zero derivative yields a candidate, not proof of an absolute extreme.

24

Mean value theorem and Rolle’s theorem

Meaning

If f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), some tangent slope equals the interval’s secant slope. Rolle’s theorem is the special case f(a)=f(b), giving f′(c)=0.

Method

Verify continuity, differentiability, and endpoint values first. Compute the secant slope, solve f′(c)=that slope, and keep only solutions strictly inside (a,b). A corner or gap can invalidate the theorem even if the endpoint values match.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=x² on [1,3], the secant slope is (9−1)/2=4. Since f′(x)=2x, c=2 satisfies the theorem.

Additional worked example

Apply Rolle’s theorem to f(x)=x²−4 on [−2,2]. Find c.

The endpoint values are both 0 and the polynomial is smooth. f′(x)=2x, so c=0.

Common error. The theorem guarantees a c inside the interval only after its hypotheses are checked.

25

First and second derivative tests

Meaning

The sign of f′ tells whether a curve rises or falls. The sign of f″ tells concavity and can classify a stationary point when f″ is nonzero.

Method

Find critical points, split the domain into intervals, and test the sign of f′ on each. At a point with f′=0, f″>0 implies a local minimum and f″<0 a local maximum. If f″=0, return to the first derivative test or another argument.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=x³−3x, f′=3(x−1)(x+1). It changes + to − at −1 (local maximum) and − to + at 1 (local minimum).

Additional worked example

Classify the stationary point of f(x)=x⁴ at x=0.

f″(0)=0 is inconclusive, but f′=4x³ changes from negative to positive, so x=0 is a local minimum.

Common error. f″=0 does not mean “no extremum”; it means the second derivative test did not decide.

26

L’Hôpital’s rule

Meaning

For a quotient with a verified 0/0 or ∞/∞ form, differentiating numerator and denominator separately can reveal its limit, under the rule’s conditions.

Method

Determine the form before applying the rule. Differentiate top and bottom independently, then re-evaluate; repeat only if the new quotient remains indeterminate. Transform products, differences, and variable powers into a quotient or take logarithms before using the rule.

Worked reasoning

lim as x→0 of (eˣ−1)/x is 0/0, then lim eˣ/1=1. This is not a quotient-rule differentiation.

Additional worked example

Evaluate lim as x→∞ of x/eˣ.

It is ∞/∞, so one application gives lim 1/eˣ=0.

Common error. The rule does not apply to a quotient merely because a denominator approaches zero.

27

Curve sketching from evidence

Meaning

A reliable sketch combines domain, intercepts, asymptotes, symmetry, increasing or decreasing intervals, extrema, and concavity.

Method

Start with the domain and boundary behavior. Calculate intercepts and possible symmetry. Use sign charts for f′ and f″; mark critical and inflection candidates only after checking actual sign changes. Plot a few exact points and join them without crossing excluded inputs.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=x³−3x, the odd graph crosses at −√3, 0, √3. f′=3x²−3 gives a local maximum at −1 and minimum at 1. f″=6x changes sign at 0, giving an inflection point there.

Additional worked example

For f(x)=1/x, state domain, symmetry, and asymptotes.

Domain x≠0; f(−x)=−f(x), so odd symmetry. x=0 is a vertical asymptote and y=0 a horizontal asymptote.

Common error. f″=0 is an inflection candidate; verify a concavity change before labeling it.

28

Applied optimization

Meaning

Optimization turns a real constraint into a one-variable objective, then uses derivative evidence to choose a feasible best value.

Method

Sketch and label variables, write the objective, use constraints to eliminate variables, determine the physical domain, find critical points, compare with boundaries, then translate the winning x back into the requested dimensions or quantity.

Worked reasoning

A 12-by-12 sheet forms an open box by cutting x from each corner. V(x)=x(12−2x)² for 0<x<6. V′=12(x−2)(x−6); the interior maximum is at x=2, giving V=128 cubic units.

Additional worked example

A rectangle has perimeter 20. What dimensions maximize its area?

Let one side be x; the other is 10−x, with 0<x<10. A=x(10−x), and A′=10−2x vanishes at x=5. The maximum is a 5-by-5 square.

Common error. An algebraic critical point outside the feasible domain cannot solve the original problem.

29

Newton’s method

Meaning

Newton’s method uses a tangent-line x-intercept to refine a root estimate for f(x)=0.

Method

Choose an initial estimate near the desired root. Evaluate f and f′ at that estimate and iterate. Stop when the change and residual are suitably small. If f′ is zero or very small, or the starting point is poor, the iteration may fail or move to a different root.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=x²−2 and x₀=1.5, x₁=1.5−(2.25−2)/3=1.41667. One further step gives approximately 1.41422, close to √2.

Additional worked example

Use one Newton step for f(x)=x²−3 from x₀=2.

x₁=2−(4−3)/4=1.75.

Common error. An iteration value is an approximation; check the residual f(xₙ) rather than assuming convergence.

Integrals

30

Approximate area and distance

Meaning

A rectangle sum approximates accumulated area. With velocity as the height, the sum approximates displacement; absolute velocity approximates distance traveled.

Method

Partition [a,b] into equal widths. Select left endpoints, right endpoints, or midpoints as sample positions; multiply each height by width and add. For an increasing positive function, left sums underestimate and right sums overestimate. A midpoint estimate is often better but is not universally exact.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=x on [0,2] with two equal rectangles, Δx=1. Left sum is f(0)+f(1)=1; right sum is f(1)+f(2)=3; midpoint sum is f(0.5)+f(1.5)=2.

Additional worked example

Compute L₂ and R₂ for f(x)=x² on [0,2].

With Δx=1, L₂=f(0)+f(1)=1 and R₂=f(1)+f(2)=5.

Common error. Do not omit Δx; summing heights alone has the wrong units.

31

Riemann sums and the fundamental theorem

Meaning

A definite integral is the limit of signed rectangle sums. The fundamental theorem links this accumulation to antiderivatives.

Method

Interpret ∫ as signed area or accumulated change. To evaluate, find an antiderivative F and subtract F(a) from F(b). Reversing bounds changes the sign; adjacent intervals add. If an accumulation function is A(x)=∫ from a to x of f(t)dt, then A′(x)=f(x) where f is continuous.

Worked reasoning

∫ from 0 to 2 of (3x²−1)dx = [x³−x] from 0 to 2 = 6. The integrand may be negative on part of the interval, but the integral is net signed accumulation.

Additional worked example

Evaluate ∫ from 1 to 3 of 2x dx.

An antiderivative is x²; 3²−1²=8.

Common error. A definite integral can be negative; “area” without qualification is not always the same as signed integral.

32

Substitution in integration

Meaning

Substitution reverses the chain rule by replacing a repeated inner expression with a new variable.

Method

Set u equal to the inner expression, compute du, and rewrite the entire integrand. For a definite integral, convert both bounds to u-values or return to x before evaluating, never mix systems. Differentiate an indefinite result to check.

Worked reasoning

∫2x cos(x²)dx: let u=x², du=2x dx. Then ∫cos u du=sin u+C=sin(x²)+C.

Additional worked example

Evaluate ∫ from 0 to 1 of 2x/(1+x²) dx.

Set u=1+x², giving bounds 1 to 2. The integral is ∫ from 1 to 2 of du/u = ln 2.

Common error. Replacing g(x) by u while leaving an unmatched x or dx behind is not a completed substitution.

Applications of integrals

33

Area between curves

Meaning

Thin strips add vertical or horizontal gaps between boundaries. Geometric area is nonnegative, so a crossing may require splitting an interval.

Method

Find intersections to establish bounds. Decide whether vertical or horizontal strips describe the region more simply. For vertical strips integrate top minus bottom with respect to x; for horizontal strips integrate right minus left with respect to y. Split where the ordering changes.

Worked reasoning

Between y=x and y=x² on [0,1], x≥x², so A=∫₀¹(x−x²)dx=[x²/2−x³/3]₀¹=1/6.

Additional worked example

Find the area enclosed by y=4 and y=x².

Intersections are x=−2 and 2. A=∫₋₂²(4−x²)dx=32/3.

Common error. Integrating one curve alone gives area to the axis, not necessarily area between two curves.

34

Volumes by disks and washers

Meaning

Slices perpendicular to the rotation axis are circles or rings. Each cross-sectional area is π times outer radius squared minus inner radius squared.

Method

Sketch the region and axis. Pick a slice perpendicular to the axis, measure outer and inner radii as distances from the axis, then integrate cross-sectional area across the full solid. A disk is a washer with inner radius zero.

Worked reasoning

Rotate the region under y=x, 0≤x≤2, about the x-axis. Each disk has radius x, so V=π∫₀²x²dx=8π/3.

Additional worked example

Rotate the region between y=2 and y=1, 0≤x≤3, about the x-axis. Find the volume.

Outer radius 2 and inner radius 1 give V=π∫₀³(4−1)dx=9π.

Common error. A radius is distance to the rotation axis, not automatically the displayed function value.

35

Volumes by cylindrical shells

Meaning

Slices parallel to the rotation axis sweep out thin cylindrical shells. Circumference times height times thickness gives each shell’s volume.

Method

Draw a representative strip parallel to the axis. Radius is its distance to the axis; height is upper minus lower or right minus left; thickness fixes whether the integration variable is x or y. Compare with washers and choose the setup with simpler boundaries.

Worked reasoning

Rotate the region under y=x on [0,2] about the y-axis. Vertical strips have radius x and height x, so V=2π∫₀²x²dx=16π/3.

Additional worked example

Rotate the region under y=1 on [0,2] about the y-axis. Use shells.

Radius x, height 1; V=2π∫₀²x dx=4π.

Common error. The shell radius and height are different geometric measurements; do not square the radius as in a disk.

36

Average value of a function

Meaning

Average value is total signed accumulation divided by interval length. For a continuous function, some point in the interval attains this average.

Method

Compute the definite integral, then divide by b−a. Keep it distinct from the endpoint average [f(a)+f(b)]/2, which is generally different. If the function represents a rate, the average is a rate with the original output units.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=x³ on [0,2], the integral is [x⁴/4]₀²=4. Dividing by interval length 2 gives average value 2.

Additional worked example

Find the average value of sin x on [0,π].

∫₀^π sin x dx=2, so the average is 2/π.

Common error. The integral itself is accumulated total; the average requires division by interval length.

Graph and differential equations

37

Reading graphs of f, f′, and f″

Meaning

The graph of f′ records slopes of f. The graph of f″ records slopes of f′ and concavity of f.

Method

Where f rises, plot f′ above the axis; where f falls, plot it below. Mark f′=0 at smooth horizontal tangents. Then apply the same slope reading to f′ to obtain f″. At a corner, f′ may be undefined, so do not draw a false zero.

Worked reasoning

For f(x)=x², slopes are negative left of zero, zero at zero, positive right of zero. Thus f′(x)=2x crosses the axis at zero; f″(x)=2 is positive everywhere.

Additional worked example

If f′ is positive but decreasing on an interval, what can be said about f and f″?

f is increasing, while f″ is negative (where defined), so its graph is concave down.

Common error. Positive f″ does not mean positive f; it means the slope f′ is increasing.

38

Slope fields

Meaning

A differential equation supplies a slope at each point where its right-hand side is defined. A slope field shows short line segments with those slopes.

Method

Evaluate F at a grid of points and draw a short segment at each, without connecting points automatically. A solution curve follows the segments from its initial condition. Look for zero-slope lines and undefined regions before sketching.

Worked reasoning

For y′=x, all points with the same x share the same slope. Integrating gives y=x²/2+C; through (0,2), C=2.

Additional worked example

For y′=y−x, what is the slope along y=x?

It is zero because y−x=0 along that line; field marks there are horizontal.

Common error. A field segment is local slope information, not a straight solution line through the whole plane.

39

Separable equations and exponential change

Meaning

A separable equation factors into a y-part and an x-part. When growth rate is proportional to the current amount, solutions are exponential.

Method

For y′=ky, write dy/y=k dx on a region where y≠0, integrate to ln|y|=kx+C, and recover y=Ce^(kx). Include the zero solution, which division by y temporarily omits. Apply the initial condition; a positive k grows and negative k decays.

Worked reasoning

For y′=−0.03y with y(0)=500, y=500e^(−0.03x). Its half-life solves 1/2=e^(−0.03x), so x=ln2/0.03≈23.1 time units.

Additional worked example

Solve y′=0.05y, y(0)=100, and find doubling time.

y=100e^(0.05x). Doubling requires 2=e^(0.05x), so x=ln2/0.05≈13.9 time units.

Common error. Dividing by y without restoring y=0 can discard a valid solution.
