Atomic structure

Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons. Z (protons) fixes the element; A counts protons plus neutrons; the charge counts missing or extra electrons.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Every chemical property begins with atomic structure. The number of protons decides which element you have; the electrons decide how it bonds and reacts; the neutrons affect mass and nuclear stability. Isotopic masses give the atomic masses used in every mole calculation in chemistry and chemical engineering.

The idea

An atom has a tiny, dense nucleus containing positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons, surrounded by negatively charged electrons. Almost all the mass is in the nucleus; almost all the volume is the space occupied by electrons. A nucleus is about 10−15 m across, an atom about 10−10 m — a factor of 100 000.

The atomic number Z is the number of protons. It defines the element: every atom with 6 protons is carbon. The mass number A is the number of protons plus neutrons. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A), for example carbon-12 and carbon-14. Isotopes have almost identical chemistry because chemistry is governed by electrons.

An ion has gained or lost electrons; the number of protons never changes in a chemical reaction. A cation (positive) has lost electrons: Mg2+ has 12 protons and 10 electrons. An anion (negative) has gained electrons: Cl− has 17 protons and 18 electrons.

The atomic mass printed on the periodic table is a weighted average of the masses of the naturally occurring isotopes, weighted by their abundances. That is why chlorine’s atomic mass is 35.45 u even though no chlorine atom has that mass.

Background you need

Charges and signs

A proton has charge +1 (in units of e = 1.602 × 10−19 C), an electron −1. Net charge = (number of protons) − (number of electrons).

Weighted average

A weighted average multiplies each value by its fraction (abundance/100) and adds: Σ(fraction × value). The fractions must add to 1.

An example, explained

Neutrons in an isotope

Problem. An atom has mass number 23 and atomic number 11. How many neutrons does it have, and what element is it?

  1. n = A − Z = 23 − 11 = 12.

    Mass number counts protons and neutrons.

  2. Z = 11 is sodium.

    The proton number identifies the element.

Result: 12 neutrons; the atom is sodium-23.

What it means: Any atom with 11 protons is sodium, whatever its neutron number.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: Using the periodic-table atomic mass as A to find neutrons.

    Correct idea: A is a whole number for a specific isotope. Chlorine’s 35.45 is an average, not a mass number.

  • Misunderstanding: Changing the number of protons when an ion forms.

    Correct idea: Only electrons are gained or lost in chemical changes.

  • Misunderstanding: Adding electrons for a positive charge.

    Correct idea: Positive means electrons were lost: electrons = Z − (positive charge).

  • Misunderstanding: “Isotopes are different elements.”

    Correct idea: Isotopes have the same Z, so they are the same element with different masses.

Build atoms and ions from three counts

Idea. Atomic number Z counts protons and identifies the element. Mass number A counts protons plus neutrons in one isotope. Electrons set charge: losing electrons makes a cation; gaining them makes an anion. Isotopes of an element differ in neutron count.

Decision. Read the element symbol to obtain Z, subtract Z from A for neutrons, then adjust the electron count for the signed ionic charge. Use the sign of charge as a check.

Worked case. For an ion with Z=17, A=37, and charge −1, there are 17 protons, 20 neutrons, and 18 electrons.

Additional worked example

How many protons, neutrons, and electrons are in an ion with Z=12, A=25, charge +2?

There are 12 protons, 25−12=13 neutrons, and 12−2=10 electrons.

Clarification. Mass number belongs to one isotope; it is not the decimal average atomic mass printed in a periodic table.

Use isotopic abundance as a weighted average

Idea. The atomic mass of a natural element is the abundance-weighted mean of its isotope masses. Abundance percentages must be converted to fractions before multiplying.

Decision. Set up one mass times one fractional abundance for each isotope. Check that fractions sum to one and that the answer lies between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses.

Worked case. An element with 75.0% isotope mass 10.0 u and 25.0% isotope mass 11.0 u has mean mass 0.750(10.0)+0.250(11.0)=10.25 u.

Additional worked example

An element has 20% isotope mass 50 u and 80% isotope mass 52 u. Find its average atomic mass.

0.20(50)+0.80(52)=51.6 u. The result is closer to 52 u because that isotope is more abundant.

Clarification. A weighted average is not the simple midpoint unless the abundances are equal.

Quantum theory

Light comes in photons with energy E = hν. Electrons in atoms can only have certain energies, so atoms absorb and emit light only at particular wavelengths. Electrons are described by orbitals labelled by quantum numbers.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Quantum theory explains why elements have their chemical properties, why they give characteristic colours in flames and spectra, and how spectroscopy — the main analytical tool of chemistry — identifies substances and measures concentrations. It also underlies lasers, LEDs, solar cells and the periodic table itself.

The idea

Light is an electromagnetic wave with wavelength λ and frequency ν related by c = λν. But in 1900–1905 Planck and Einstein showed that light energy comes in packets called photons, each with energy E = hν = hc / λ. Shorter wavelength means higher energy: ultraviolet photons carry more energy than red ones.

The photoelectric effect proved this. Light ejects electrons from a metal only if each photon has enough energy (frequency above a threshold). Brighter light of too low a frequency ejects none, because brightness means more photons, not more energetic ones.

Heated hydrogen gas emits light only at certain wavelengths — a line spectrum, not a continuous rainbow. Bohr explained this: the electron can only occupy levels with energies En = −2.18 × 10−18 J / n2, n = 1, 2, 3, … When it drops from a higher level to a lower one, it emits a photon whose energy equals the difference, ΔE = hν. The energies are negative because the electron is bound; zero means the electron has been removed (ionised).

Electrons also behave as waves (de Broglie, λ = h/mv), and the full quantum-mechanical model replaces Bohr’s orbits by orbitals: regions where the electron is likely to be found. Each orbital is labelled by three quantum numbers, and each electron by a fourth: n (shell, size and energy), l (subshell shape: s, p, d, f), ml (orientation) and ms (spin, +½ or −½).

The Heisenberg uncertainty principle says an electron’s position and momentum cannot both be known exactly, which is why we speak of probability, not paths.

Background you need

Scientific notation

Quantum quantities are tiny: h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s. Multiply coefficients and add exponents: (6.626 × 10−34)(2.998 × 108) = 1.986 × 10−25.

Unit conversions

1 nm = 10−9 m. Energy per mole = energy per photon × NA (6.022 × 1023 mol−1).

An example, explained

Energy of a photon

Problem. Find the energy of one photon of green light (λ = 500 nm) and of one mole of such photons.

  1. E = hc / λ = (6.626 × 10−34)(2.998 × 108) / 500 × 10−9 = 3.97 × 10−19 J.

    Convert nm to m first.

  2. Per mole: 3.97 × 10−19 × 6.022 × 1023 = 2.39 × 105 J/mol = 239 kJ/mol.

    Multiply by Avogadro’s number.

Result: 3.97 × 10−19 J per photon; 239 kJ/mol.

What it means: This is comparable to chemical bond energies, which is why visible and UV light can drive chemical reactions (photosynthesis, fading of dyes).

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: Forgetting to convert nm to m.

    Correct idea: Multiply nanometres by 10⁻⁹ before using c or hc.

  • Misunderstanding: “Brighter light has more energetic photons.”

    Correct idea: Brightness is the number of photons; each photon’s energy depends only on its frequency.

  • Misunderstanding: Applying E_n = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸/n² J to atoms other than hydrogen.

    Correct idea: The Bohr formula works for one-electron species only.

  • Misunderstanding: Thinking of orbitals as fixed circular paths.

    Correct idea: Orbitals are probability regions; the electron has no definite path.

  • Misunderstanding: Allowing l = n.

    Correct idea: l ranges from 0 to n − 1.

Connect light to quantized energy

Idea. Light has wave frequency ν and wavelength λ related by its speed in vacuum. A photon carries energy proportional to frequency. Atomic line spectra reflect discrete energy differences between allowed states.

Decision. Convert wavelength to meters, then use c/λ for frequency or hc/λ for one photon’s energy. The shorter the wavelength, the larger the frequency and photon energy.

Worked case. A 500 nm photon has energy (6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s)(3.00×10⁸ m/s)/(500×10⁻⁹ m)≈3.98×10⁻¹⁹ J.

Additional worked example

At equal light speed, which photon is more energetic: 400 nm or 800 nm? By what factor?

The 400 nm photon has twice the frequency and twice the energy of the 800 nm photon.

Clarification. Increasing wavelength decreases photon energy; intensity changes photon count, not the energy of each photon of fixed frequency.

Read orbitals as probability regions

Idea. An orbital is a mathematical state describing electron probability, not a classical path. A shell has principal quantum number n; subshells s, p, d, f correspond to l=0,1,2,3. Each orbital holds at most two electrons of opposite spin.

Decision. Check allowed quantum numbers before filling. An s subshell has one orbital, p has three, and d has five. Capacities are therefore 2, 6, and 10 electrons.

Worked case. The n=2 shell permits 2s and 2p, but no 2d because l must be smaller than n. The whole shell has one s plus three p orbitals and can hold eight electrons.

Additional worked example

How many orbitals and electrons can the 3p subshell hold?

A p subshell has three orbitals. At two electrons per orbital, 3p can hold six electrons.

Clarification. The label 3p does not mean three electrons; it names a subshell in the third shell.

Build electron configurations and ions

Idea. Electrons fill lower-energy orbitals first, subject to the Pauli exclusion principle and Hund’s rule. Valence electrons govern much chemistry. For main-group cations, electrons are removed from the outermost principal shell; transition-metal cations lose ns electrons before (n−1)d.

Decision. Count total electrons from Z and ion charge. Fill subshells in their usual order and check the final count. For an ion, remove electrons from the neutral configuration according to the outermost-shell rule.

Worked case. Neutral Na has 11 electrons: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹. Na⁺ has ten: 1s²2s²2p⁶. Neutral Fe is [Ar]4s²3d⁶; Fe²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁶.

Additional worked example

Give the electron configuration of Cl⁻ (Z=17).

Cl⁻ has 18 electrons, so its configuration is 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶, the same electron count as Ar.

Clarification. Do not remove a 3d electron before a 4s electron when forming a typical first-row transition-metal cation.

Periodic table

Electron configurations follow from filling orbitals in order of energy. The periodic table groups elements with similar outer electrons, and effective nuclear charge explains the trends in size, ionisation energy and electronegativity.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

The periodic table is chemistry’s map. From an element’s position you can predict its outer electrons, the ions it forms, how reactive it is, whether it is a metal, and what kind of bonds it makes. These predictions guide material selection, corrosion behaviour and reaction design in chemical engineering.

The idea

Electrons fill orbitals from the lowest energy upward (the Aufbau principle): 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, … Each orbital holds at most two electrons with opposite spins (Pauli exclusion principle). Within a subshell of equal-energy orbitals, electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before pairing (Hund’s rule).

The valence electrons (outermost shell) govern chemistry. Elements in the same group have the same valence configuration — for example ns1 for the alkali metals — and therefore similar properties. The table’s blocks show which subshell is being filled: s, p, d or f.

The key to the trends is effective nuclear charge Zeff: the positive charge an outer electron actually feels, less than Z because inner electrons shield it. Across a period, Z increases while the added electrons are in the same shell and shield each other poorly, so Zeff increases: the atoms become smaller and hold their electrons more tightly. Down a group, a new, larger shell is added each period, so atoms get bigger and outer electrons are easier to remove.

So atomic radius decreases across a period and increases down a group; ionisation energy (energy to remove an electron) and electronegativity (pull on bonding electrons) increase across and decrease down. Small exceptions have clear reasons: ionisation energy drops from Be to B (the 2p electron is higher in energy than 2s) and from N to O (pairing repulsion in O’s 2p).

Ions: cations are smaller than their atoms (fewer electrons, sometimes a whole shell removed); anions are larger. When transition metals form ions they lose their 4s electrons before 3d: Fe is [Ar]3d64s2, Fe2+ is [Ar]3d6.

An example, explained

Configuration and unpaired electrons of oxygen

Problem. Write the ground-state configuration of oxygen (Z = 8) and find the number of unpaired electrons.

  1. 1s2 2s2 2p4.

    Fill 1s, 2s, then 2p with the remaining four electrons.

  2. 2p has three orbitals: put one electron in each, then pair the fourth: ↑↓ ↑ ↑.

    Hund’s rule.

Result: 1s²2s²2p⁴, with 2 unpaired electrons (oxygen atoms are paramagnetic).

What it means: A 2p³ configuration (nitrogen) would have 3 unpaired electrons.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: Filling 3d before 4s for neutral atoms, or removing 3d before 4s for ions.

    Correct idea: Fill 4s before 3d; ionise 4s before 3d.

  • Misunderstanding: Pairing electrons before filling each orbital of a subshell singly.

    Correct idea: Hund’s rule: singly first, parallel spins.

  • Misunderstanding: “Atoms get bigger across a period because they have more electrons.”

    Correct idea: They get smaller: the added electrons are in the same shell while Z_eff increases.

  • Misunderstanding: Treating trends as rules with no exceptions.

    Correct idea: Be/B and N/O ionisation dips, and Cr/Cu configurations, are the standard exceptions.

Predict periodic trends with effective attraction

Idea. Across a period, increasing nuclear charge tends to pull valence electrons closer when shielding changes modestly. Down a group, additional occupied shells tend to increase atomic radius. Ionization energy is energy needed to remove an electron from a gas-phase atom; electron affinity describes electron addition.

Decision. Compare positions first, then check electron configurations for exceptions. Cations are smaller than their parent atoms; anions are larger. For an isoelectronic series, the ion with more protons attracts the same electron count more strongly.

Worked case. O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, and Mg²⁺ all have ten electrons. Their radius decreases as Z rises from 8 to 12; Mg²⁺ is smallest.

Additional worked example

Which is generally larger: Na or Na⁺? Which is larger in an isoelectronic pair: F⁻ or Na⁺?

Na is larger than Na⁺ because it retains its outer 3s electron. F⁻ is larger than Na⁺ because F⁻ has fewer protons attracting the same ten electrons.

Clarification. A periodic trend is a model with exceptions; do not claim every adjacent ionization energy follows a perfectly monotonic pattern.

Chemical bonding

Atoms bond by transferring electrons (ionic) or sharing them (covalent). Lewis structures count the electrons; VSEPR turns electron domains into shapes; shape and electronegativity decide polarity.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Bonding explains why salt is a hard, high-melting solid while methane is a gas; why water dissolves ions and oil does not; why some molecules absorb infrared radiation. Molecular shape and polarity decide boiling points, solubility and reactivity — the properties that matter when choosing solvents, designing separations or handling chemicals safely.

The idea

Atoms bond because the bonded arrangement has lower energy. Main-group atoms often end up with eight valence electrons around them (the octet rule; hydrogen needs two).

In an ionic bond, electrons are transferred from a metal to a non-metal, forming cations and anions held together by electrostatic attraction. Ionic compounds form extended lattices, not separate molecules: NaCl describes the 1:1 ratio of ions in the crystal. In a covalent bond, non-metal atoms share pairs of electrons. Sharing is unequal when the atoms have different electronegativities: the bond is polar, with partial charges δ+ and δ−.

A Lewis structure shows every valence electron: bonding pairs as lines, lone pairs as dots. Count the total valence electrons, connect the atoms with single bonds, complete octets on outer atoms, then on the central atom, forming double or triple bonds if necessary. Formal charges (valence electrons − lone-pair electrons − bonds) help choose the best structure: formal charges close to zero, with any negative charge on the more electronegative atom. When two or more equally good structures differ only in where the double bond is, the real molecule is a resonance hybrid of them.

VSEPR (valence-shell electron-pair repulsion): the electron domains around a central atom (each bond — single, double or triple — or lone pair counts as one domain) spread out as far apart as possible. Two domains: linear (180°); three: trigonal planar (120°); four: tetrahedral (109.5°). The molecular shape names only the atom positions: with four domains, 4 bonds give tetrahedral (CH₄), 3 bonds + 1 lone pair give trigonal pyramidal (NH₃, 107°), 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs give bent (H₂O, 104.5°). Lone pairs repel more strongly and squeeze the bond angles.

A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles do not cancel. CO₂ has polar bonds, but it is linear and the two dipoles cancel, so the molecule is non-polar. Water is bent, so its dipoles add to a net dipole: water is polar. Shape and bond polarity must both be considered.

Background you need

Valence electrons from the periodic table

For main-group elements, the number of valence electrons equals the group’s last digit: H 1, C 4, N 5, O 6, F and Cl 7. Add one electron for each negative charge of an ion; subtract one for each positive charge.

Electronegativity

Increases across a period and up a group (F highest). Pauling values: H 2.20, C 2.55, N 3.04, O 3.44, F 3.98, Na 0.93, Cl 3.16.

An example, explained

Counting valence electrons

Problem. How many valence electrons are in CO₂, H₂O and the nitrate ion NO₃⁻?

  1. CO₂: 4 + 2 × 6 = 16.

    C has 4, each O 6.

  2. H₂O: 2 × 1 + 6 = 8.

    Each H has 1.

  3. NO₃⁻: 5 + 3 × 6 + 1 = 24.

    Add one electron for the 1− charge.

Result: 16, 8 and 24.

What it means: Every structure you draw must use exactly this many electrons.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: Calling NaCl a molecule.

    Correct idea: It is an ionic lattice; NaCl is its formula unit (ratio).

  • Misunderstanding: Counting a double bond as two domains in VSEPR.

    Correct idea: Any bond counts as one domain.

  • Misunderstanding: Naming the shape from the electron geometry.

    Correct idea: H₂O has tetrahedral electron geometry but a bent molecular shape.

  • Misunderstanding: “A molecule with polar bonds is polar.”

    Correct idea: Symmetric shapes (CO₂, CCl₄, BF₃) cancel bond dipoles.

  • Misunderstanding: Forgetting the ion charge when counting electrons.

    Correct idea: Anions add electrons; cations subtract.

Distinguish ionic and covalent bonding

Idea. An ionic solid is stabilized by an extended network of electrostatic attractions. A covalent bond shares electron density between nuclei. Bond polarity reflects unequal sharing, while molecular polarity depends on both bond dipoles and geometry.

Decision. Identify the particles and structure first. For ionic compounds, balance formula-unit charge and consider lattice behavior; for molecules, draw connectivity and electron pairs. Do not treat a formula unit as an isolated ionic molecule.

Worked case. NaCl is represented by a 1:1 formula unit in an ionic lattice. HCl is a molecular covalent compound with a polar bond. Water has polar O–H bonds and a bent geometry, so its dipoles do not cancel.

Additional worked example

Why does CO₂ have polar C=O bonds but no permanent molecular dipole?

CO₂ is linear. The two equal bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel as vectors.

Clarification. Electronegativity difference alone cannot decide the total polarity of a molecule; geometry matters.

Draw Lewis structures and formal charges

Idea. A Lewis structure allocates valence electrons as bonding pairs and lone pairs. Formal charge is a bookkeeping charge in a chosen electron assignment, not a measured atomic charge.

Decision. Sum valence electrons and adjust for overall charge. Sketch a reasonable skeleton, join atoms with single bonds, complete terminal octets where applicable, place remaining electrons on the center, and create multiple bonds if needed. Compare formal charges and plausible electronegativity placement.

Worked case. For CO₂, 16 valence electrons give O=C=O with two lone pairs on each oxygen. Each atom has formal charge zero.

Additional worked example

How many valence electrons does NH₄⁺ contain, and what is the formal charge on N when it has four N–H bonds and no lone pair?

5+4(1)−1=8 electrons. Nitrogen has FC=5−0−4=+1; each H has zero.

Clarification. Do not force hydrogen to have an octet. Some central atoms have incomplete or expanded octets, so check the element and electron count.

Use resonance without moving nuclei

Idea. Resonance structures are alternative Lewis electron placements for the same nuclear arrangement. The real electron distribution is not rapidly flipping between drawings; a resonance hybrid can have delocalized bonding. Equivalent contributors often share the same energy.

Decision. Keep atoms and sigma-bond connectivity fixed while moving permissible electron pairs and pi bonds. Preserve the total electron count and overall charge in every contributor.

Worked case. In NO₃⁻, three equivalent drawings put the N=O bond at different oxygen atoms. The measured N–O bonds are equivalent in the resonance hybrid, with average bond order between one and two.

Additional worked example

When drawing another resonance contributor for a carbonate ion, may the carbon and oxygen nuclei exchange positions?

No. Only the electron placement changes; the atom connectivity and total ion charge remain the same.

Clarification. Resonance is not the same as chemical equilibrium between different molecules.

Predict shape and polarity with VSEPR

Idea. Electron domains around a central atom repel and arrange to reduce crowding. A multiple bond counts as one domain for the basic VSEPR arrangement. Molecular shape describes atom positions; electron geometry also counts lone pairs.

Decision. Draw a Lewis structure, count bonding regions and lone pairs on the central atom, then name electron geometry and molecular shape separately. Add bond dipole vectors to test net polarity.

Worked case. H₂O has four electron domains around O: two bonds and two lone pairs. Its electron geometry is tetrahedral, but its molecular shape is bent; the O–H dipoles do not cancel.

Additional worked example

Predict the electron geometry, molecular shape, and polarity of NH₃.

N has three bonds and one lone pair: tetrahedral electron geometry, trigonal-pyramidal molecular shape, and a net molecular dipole.

Clarification. The word “tetrahedral” for electron geometry does not mean four atoms surround the center.

Reactions in solution

In water, many compounds exist as ions. Reactions in solution — precipitation, acid–base and redox — are written as net ionic equations, and their amounts are calculated through moles using balanced equations.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Most chemistry in industry, the environment and living things happens in water: water treatment removes ions by precipitation, titrations measure concentrations in quality control, neutralisation treats acidic waste, and redox reactions drive corrosion and batteries. Knowing which species actually react, and in what amounts, is the basis of process design and analysis.

The idea

A balanced equation conserves atoms and charge. Its coefficients give mole ratios, never mass ratios: in 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, 2 mol of H₂ react with 1 mol of O₂. To calculate amounts, always go through moles (see the figure).

When ionic compounds or strong acids dissolve, they separate into ions: they are strong electrolytes. NaCl(aq) is really Na⁺(aq) and Cl⁻(aq). Weak acids (such as acetic acid) and weak bases ionise only partly (weak electrolytes); sugar does not ionise at all (non-electrolyte).

Writing reactions as ions shows what really happens. Mixing AgNO₃(aq) with NaCl(aq) forms solid AgCl. In the complete ionic equation, Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ appear unchanged on both sides — they are spectator ions. Removing them gives the net ionic equation: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s). Solubility rules tell you which products are insoluble (precipitates).

The three main reaction types are: precipitation (an insoluble solid forms), acid–base neutralisation (H⁺ from an acid combines with OH⁻ from a base: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O for strong acid–strong base), and oxidation–reduction (redox), in which electrons are transferred. Oxidation is loss of electrons (oxidation number increases); reduction is gain (oxidation number decreases). They always happen together.

When reactants are not in the exact mole ratio, the one that runs out first is the limiting reactant; it determines the maximum (theoretical) yield. The actual yield divided by the theoretical yield gives the percent yield. For solutions, moles = molarity × volume, which makes titration possible: measure the volume of a solution of known concentration needed to react exactly with an unknown.

An example, explained

Moles from a balanced equation

Problem. For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, how much water forms from 3 mol H₂ with excess O₂? How much O₂ is used?

  1. n(H₂O) = 3 × 2 / 2 = 3 mol.

    Ratio H₂O : H₂ = 2 : 2.

Balance a reaction by conserving atoms

Idea. A balanced chemical equation states a whole-number ratio of reacting entities. The formulas identify substances; coefficients count them. Chemical reactions conserve each element and total charge.

Decision. Confirm the reactant and product formulas first. Change coefficients only, not subscripts. Balance elements appearing in fewer species first, leave H and O for later when practical, then check every count and reduce coefficients.

Worked case. For propane combustion, C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O. Both sides contain 3 C, 8 H, and 10 O atoms.

Idea. Oxidation numbers are a bookkeeping tool for electron transfer. An increase in oxidation number is oxidation; a decrease is reduction. The oxidizing agent is reduced, and the reducing agent is oxidized.

Worked case. In Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, Zn changes 0→+2 and loses two electrons; Cu changes +2→0 and gains two. Cu²⁺ is the oxidizing agent.

Additional worked example

In 2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO, name what is oxidized and what is reduced.

Mg goes 0→+2 and is oxidized. O goes 0→−2 and is reduced. The O₂ is the oxidizing agent.

Clarification. An oxidizing agent gains electrons; its name describes what it does to the other reactant.

Use coefficients as mole ratios

Idea. A balanced equation connects amounts of substances in moles, not directly in grams. The reaction coefficient ratio is the bridge between different species.

Decision. Convert given mass or volume to moles of the known species, apply the coefficient ratio, then convert to the requested unit. Include the substance name in each factor so accidental inverse ratios are visible.

Worked case. For N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃, 0.50 mol N₂ can produce 1.0 mol NH₃ if enough H₂ is available.

Additional worked example

For 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O, how many moles of water can 1.5 mol O₂ produce with excess H₂?

Clarification. A coefficient is not a mass multiplier. Convert grams to moles before using the balanced equation.

Find the limiting reagent and yield

Idea. The limiting reagent sets the maximum product, because it is consumed first in the ideal reaction. Theoretical yield is calculated from it; actual yield is measured. Excess reagent remains after the reaction.

Decision. Calculate the possible product from each reactant separately, in moles. The smaller product amount determines the limit. Then compute theoretical mass and compare with measured mass.

Worked case. For N₂+3 H₂→2 NH₃, 2.0 mol N₂ could make 4.0 mol NH₃, while 3.0 mol H₂ could make 2.0 mol NH₃. H₂ limits production. If 1.5 mol NH₃ is collected, percent yield is 75%.

Additional worked example

For 2 H₂+O₂→2 H₂O, 3.0 mol H₂ reacts with 1.0 mol O₂. Which limits, and how much H₂O forms ideally?

O₂ can make 2.0 mol H₂O; H₂ could make 3.0 mol. O₂ limits, so theoretical yield is 2.0 mol H₂O.

Clarification. The reactant with fewer moles is not necessarily limiting; compare its required coefficient ratio.

Properties of solutions

A solution’s composition is described by concentration units (molarity, molality, mass percent, mole fraction). Dilution conserves solute. Dissolved particles lower vapour pressure and freezing point and raise boiling point and osmotic pressure.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Chemical engineers prepare, mix and separate solutions constantly: making reagents to a specified concentration, diluting stock solutions, predicting how much gas dissolves in a liquid under pressure, protecting engines with antifreeze, and understanding osmosis in membranes and water purification. Choosing the right concentration unit for the job avoids serious errors.

The idea

A solution is a homogeneous mixture: a solute dissolved in a solvent. Whether something dissolves depends on the balance between the attractions broken (solute–solute, solvent–solvent) and those formed (solute–solvent): “like dissolves like” — polar and ionic substances dissolve in polar solvents such as water; non-polar substances in non-polar solvents.

A saturated solution holds the maximum amount of solute at that temperature and is in equilibrium with undissolved solid. Stirring speeds up dissolving but does not change how much can dissolve. Most solids become more soluble in hot water (not all); gases become less soluble as temperature rises, but more soluble at higher partial pressure (Henry’s law) — the reason carbonated drinks fizz when opened.

Concentration can be expressed in several ways. Molarity (mol per litre of solution) is convenient for measuring volumes in the lab, but changes slightly with temperature because volume does. Molality (mol per kilogram of solvent) does not change with temperature and is used for freezing- and boiling-point calculations. Mass percent and mole fraction are also common.

When a solution is diluted, solvent is added but the moles of solute do not change: c1V1 = c2V2.

Colligative properties depend on the number of dissolved particles, not their identity. A non-volatile solute lowers the solvent’s vapour pressure, so the solution boils at a higher temperature and freezes at a lower one; it also creates osmotic pressure across a semipermeable membrane. Ionic solutes give more particles: NaCl gives about 2 per formula unit (the van ’t Hoff factor i ≈ 2), CaCl₂ about 3, glucose 1. Real values of i are slightly lower than ideal because ions interact.

Properties of gases

An ideal gas obeys PV = nRT with T in kelvin. The simple gas laws, gas densities, mixtures (Dalton) and gas stoichiometry all follow, and kinetic-molecular theory explains why.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Gases are everywhere in chemical engineering: reactor feeds and products, combustion, compressors, storage cylinders, airbags, breathing gases. The ideal gas law lets you calculate how much gas a vessel holds, what pressure it will reach when heated, or what volume of gas a reaction produces — and knowing its assumptions tells you when a real gas will deviate.

The idea

A gas is described by four variables: pressure P, volume V, temperature T and amount n. For an ideal gas they are linked by PV = nRT. Temperature must be absolute (kelvin): T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15. Using °C gives nonsense, such as zero volume at 0 °C.

Holding two variables fixed gives the classic laws. Boyle: at constant n and T, P ∝ 1/V (halve the volume, double the pressure). Charles: at constant n and P, V ∝ T. Gay-Lussac: at constant n and V, P ∝ T. Avogadro: at constant P and T, V ∝ n — equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules. For one sample changing conditions, use P1V1 / T1 = P2V2 / T2.

Kinetic-molecular theory explains these laws. Gas molecules are in constant random motion; their own volume is negligible compared with the container; they do not attract or repel except during elastic collisions; and their average kinetic energy is proportional to the absolute temperature. Pressure is the result of molecules colliding with the walls. Heating makes them move faster and hit harder and more often.

In a mixture, each gas behaves as if it were alone: its partial pressure is Pi = niRT/V, and the total pressure is the sum (Dalton’s law), so Pi = χiPtotal.

Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour at high pressure (molecules’ own volume matters) and low temperature (attractions matter). At ordinary conditions the ideal gas law is usually accurate to a few percent.

Thermochemistry

Reactions release or absorb heat. Measure heat with q = mcΔT (calorimetry); express reaction heats as ΔH; combine them with Hess’s law, enthalpies of formation or bond enthalpies.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Energy balances are at the heart of chemical engineering: sizing heat exchangers, controlling reactor temperature so that an exothermic reaction does not run away, comparing fuels, and estimating energy costs. Thermochemistry gives the numbers and the sign conventions those balances need.

The idea

Separate the universe into the system (the reaction or substance you study) and the surroundings (everything else). Energy flows between them as heat q or work w. The first law of thermodynamics — conservation of energy — says ΔU = q + w, where U is the internal energy. Chemistry sign convention: q > 0 when heat flows into the system, w > 0 when work is done on the system.

Most reactions happen at constant pressure (open to the atmosphere). Then the heat exchanged equals the change in enthalpy, qp = ΔH. An exothermic reaction releases heat, ΔH < 0 (the surroundings get warmer); an endothermic reaction absorbs heat, ΔH > 0 (the surroundings get colder).

Temperature is not the same as heat. Heat is energy transferred; temperature measures how hot something is. The heat needed to change a temperature is q = mcΔT, where c is the specific heat capacity (water: 4.184 J/(g·°C), unusually high). A calorimeter measures reaction heats: the heat released by the reaction is absorbed by the water (and the calorimeter), so qreaction = −qwater.

Enthalpy is a state function: ΔH depends only on the starting and final states, not the route. That gives Hess’s law: if a reaction is the sum of steps, its ΔH is the sum of their ΔH values. Reversing a reaction changes the sign of ΔH; multiplying its coefficients multiplies ΔH. A thermochemical equation’s ΔH refers to the amounts written.

Tabulated standard enthalpies of formation ΔHf° (forming 1 mol of compound from its elements in their standard states; zero for an element in its standard state) let you compute any reaction: ΔH°rxn = ΣnΔHf°(products) − ΣnΔHf°(reactants). Average bond enthalpies give quick estimates: breaking bonds costs energy, forming bonds releases it.

Electrochemistry

Redox reactions can be split so that electrons flow through a wire. Galvanic cells convert chemical energy to electrical energy (E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode); electrolysis uses electricity to drive non-spontaneous reactions (Faraday’s laws).

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Why it matters

Batteries, fuel cells, corrosion, electroplating and the industrial production of aluminium, chlorine and sodium hydroxide are all electrochemistry. Engineers use electrode potentials to predict which metals corrode, to design corrosion protection (sacrificial anodes), and Faraday’s laws to size electrolysis plants.

The idea

In a redox reaction electrons move from the species being oxidised to the species being reduced. If the two half-reactions are placed in separate compartments connected by a wire, the electrons must travel through the wire: an electric current. This is a galvanic (voltaic) cell.

Oxidation happens at the anode, reduction at the cathode (“an ox, red cat”). In a galvanic cell the anode is the negative terminal and electrons flow through the external wire from anode to cathode. A salt bridge (or porous barrier) lets ions move between the solutions so that each stays electrically neutral; without it the current stops immediately.

Each half-reaction has a standard reduction potential E° (in volts), measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (defined as 0 V) at 1 M, 1 bar and usually 25 °C. The more positive E°, the stronger the tendency to be reduced. The cell voltage is E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, using both values as reduction potentials without changing their signs. Multiplying a half-reaction to balance electrons does not change its E° (potential is an intensive property).

A positive E°cell means the reaction is spontaneous as written: ΔG° = −nFE°cell < 0. Away from standard concentrations, the Nernst equation adjusts the voltage; as a battery discharges, the reactant concentrations fall and the voltage drops.

In electrolysis an external power supply forces a non-spontaneous reaction, for example splitting molten NaCl into Na and Cl₂. The amount of product is set by the charge passed: Q = It, and n(e⁻) = Q/F, where F = 96 485 C/mol is the charge of one mole of electrons (Faraday’s law).

Clarification. Anode means oxidation in both galvanic and electrolytic cells; the electrode sign differs between those devices.

Calculate electrolysis from charge

Idea. An external power supply can drive an otherwise unfavorable redox change. The charge passed determines moles of electrons, which connect to the amount of product through its balanced half reaction. Faraday constant F≈96485 C/mol electrons.

Decision. Find Q from current and time in seconds, divide by F, and use electrons per product ion. Check which half reaction is actually possible in the specified electrolyte, especially in water.

Worked case. Depositing Cu from Cu²⁺ + 2e−→Cu with 1930 C ideally uses 1930/96485≈0.0200 mol electrons and gives 0.0100 mol Cu, about 0.635 g.

Additional worked example

How many moles of Ag can form ideally from Ag⁺ + e−→Ag when 9648.5 C passes?

n(e−)=9648.5/96485=0.100 mol, so 0.100 mol Ag forms if current efficiency is 100%.

Clarification. Do not divide charge by F and call the result moles of metal before using the half-reaction electron ratio.

Background: matter, measurement, formulas and moles

The quantitative foundation that reactions in solution, solutions and gases rely on: classification, measurement, unit conversion, formulas and the mole.

Classify matter before naming a change

Idea. An element contains one kind of atom; a compound has different elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio. A mixture combines substances without fixing their chemical identities. A physical change alters state or distribution; a chemical change makes different substances.

Decision. Ask whether the sample has variable composition. If yes, it is a mixture. If a pure substance can be decomposed chemically, it is a compound. To classify a change, compare chemical identity before and after rather than appearance alone.

Worked case. Salt dissolved in water is a homogeneous mixture: the amount of salt can vary and the salt can be recovered by evaporating water. Burning magnesium makes magnesium oxide, a new compound.

Additional worked example

A clear sample contains water and ethanol in a variable ratio. Is it an element, compound, or mixture? Is distillation a chemical reaction?

It is a homogeneous mixture. Distillation separates the components by differing volatility without changing their chemical identities.

Clarification. “Clear” does not mean pure. Bubbles or a color change alone are clues, not proof of a particular chemical reaction.

Measure with units and honest precision

Idea. A measurement is a value with a unit and an uncertainty. Density is mass per volume; it can help identify a substance only under comparable conditions. Significant figures describe reported precision, not the number of digits a calculator displays.

Decision. Convert units before combining values. In multiplication or division, retain the fewest significant figures among measured inputs; in addition or subtraction, retain the least precise decimal place. Keep guard digits until the final line.

Worked case. A 12.4 g sample occupies 5.0 mL. Its density is 2.48 g/mL before rounding, or 2.5 g/mL at two significant figures.

Additional worked example

A sample has mass 18.62 g and volume 7.5 mL. Give its density with appropriate significant figures.

18.62/7.5 = 2.4826… g/mL; the volume has two significant figures, so report 2.5 g/mL.

Clarification. An exact counted number or a defined unit conversion does not usually limit significant figures. Never round every intermediate step.

Move between particles moles and mass

Idea. One mole contains Avogadro’s number of specified entities. Molar mass M connects a measured mass with amount n. The entity must be named: atoms, molecules, formula units, ions, or electrons.

Decision. Write a conversion chain with units. For a compound, sum each atomic mass multiplied by its subscript to find M; then use the formula subscripts again if the question asks for constituent atoms.

Worked case. Water has M≈18.0 g/mol. A 9.0 g sample is 0.50 mol H₂O, containing about 3.01×10²³ molecules and 1.00 mol hydrogen atoms.

Additional worked example

How many moles of oxygen atoms are present in 0.25 mol CO₂?

Each CO₂ molecule has two oxygen atoms, so 0.25 mol CO₂ contains 0.50 mol O atoms.

Clarification. A mole of molecules is not a mole of each atom in that molecule; apply subscripts after the molecule count.

Find empirical and molecular formulas

Idea. An empirical formula is the smallest whole-number atom ratio. A molecular formula is an integer multiple of it when an actual molecular molar mass is known.

Decision. Assume a 100 g sample when percentages are given. Convert each element mass to moles, divide all amounts by the smallest, and multiply every ratio by the same small integer if needed. Finally find k from the molar masses.

Worked case. A sample with 40.0% C, 6.7% H, and 53.3% O gives approximately 3.33:6.7:3.33 mol, or CH₂O. If its molecular molar mass is about 180 g/mol, k=6 and the molecular formula is C₆H₁₂O₆.

Additional worked example

A compound has empirical formula CH₂O and molecular molar mass 60 g/mol. Give its molecular formula.

Empirical-formula mass is about 30 g/mol. The multiplier is 60/30=2, so the formula is C₂H₄O₂.

Clarification. Never round a ratio such as 1.50 to 2. Multiply all ratios by 2 to preserve the composition.

Write formulas from charges and names

Idea. A chemical formula records composition. Ionic formulas use the smallest whole-number ion ratio that balances charge. Molecular names often use prefixes, while common ionic names identify the cation and anion; variable-charge metals require the stated charge.

Decision. Write ion symbols and charges first. Choose subscripts that make total charge zero, reduce to the smallest ratio, and use parentheses when more than one polyatomic ion is required.

Worked case. Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻ combine as Al₂(SO₄)₃: two aluminum ions contribute +6, and three sulfate ions contribute −6.

Additional worked example

Write the formula formed by Ca²⁺ and NO₃⁻.

Ca(NO₃)₂. One calcium ion needs two nitrate ions to balance +2.

Clarification. Changing an ion’s subscript inside a polyatomic ion changes its identity. Balance with whole ions, not rewritten atoms.

Relate orbital overlap to bond order

Idea. A sigma bond has electron density along the internuclear axis; a pi bond has sideways overlap. In simple localized models, single, double, and triple bonds contain one sigma bond plus zero, one, or two pi bonds. Molecular-orbital bond order compares bonding and antibonding electrons.

Decision. Use Lewis and VSEPR for shape, localized orbital overlap for sigma/pi counting, and molecular-orbital reasoning when delocalization or magnetism matters. Distinguish the model from a literal picture of electrons in fixed tracks.

Worked case. Ethene C₂H₄ has a C=C double bond: one C–C sigma and one C–C pi bond. Its carbon centers are approximately trigonal planar in a localized hybridization description.

Additional worked example

How many sigma and pi bonds form the C≡C bond in ethyne?

One sigma bond and two pi bonds make the triple bond.

Clarification. A double bond does not consist of two independent sigma bonds.

Compare intermolecular forces

Idea. London dispersion acts between all atoms and molecules. Permanent dipole interactions require polar molecules. Hydrogen bonding is a particularly strong directional attraction when H is bonded to N, O, or F and interacts with a suitable lone pair. These are interactions between molecules, not the covalent bonds within them.

Decision. For comparable size, inspect polarity and hydrogen-bond capability. For nonpolar species, greater polarizability and surface contact often strengthen dispersion. Stronger attractions usually raise boiling point, but structure and size must also be considered.

Worked case. H₂O boils much higher than H₂S despite lower molar mass because water forms a strong hydrogen-bond network; H₂S does not form the same network.

Additional worked example

Among CH₄ and C₄H₁₀, which generally has the higher normal boiling point and why?

C₄H₁₀ has stronger dispersion interactions because its electron cloud is larger and more polarizable, so its boiling point is higher.

Clarification. Hydrogen bonding does not mean any molecule containing hydrogen can hydrogen-bond strongly.

Read phase changes as competing energies

Idea. A phase change at fixed pressure can absorb or release energy while temperature remains approximately constant. Vapor pressure rises with temperature; boiling occurs when vapor pressure equals external pressure. A phase diagram marks stable phases and coexistence curves.

Decision. Use heat capacity for a temperature-changing segment and latent enthalpy for a phase-change plateau. On a phase diagram, identify the state by the plotted pressure and temperature; crossing a boundary changes phase.

Worked case. Heating 2.0 mol liquid water to its boiling point is a temperature step. Vaporizing it at that point needs roughly 2.0(40.7)=81.4 kJ if ΔHvap≈40.7 kJ/mol, without a further temperature rise during ideal boiling.

Additional worked example

What happens to the normal boiling temperature of a liquid if external pressure is lowered?

It generally decreases: the vapor pressure equals the lower external pressure at a lower temperature.

Clarification. A temperature plateau during a phase change does not mean no energy is entering; energy changes particle arrangement.

Connect solid structure to properties

Idea. A crystalline solid repeats a unit cell. Its macroscopic properties depend on particle type and bonding: ionic, molecular, metallic, or covalent-network. The density of a cubic crystal follows from the number Z of formula units or atoms per cell, molar mass M, and edge length a.

Decision. Classify bonding before predicting conductivity and melting behavior. For unit-cell density, count shared corner, edge, face, and interior particles correctly, convert a to centimeters or meters consistently, and use Z for the chosen conventional cell.

Worked case. A simple cubic cell has eight corner particles each shared among eight cells, so Z=1. A body-centered cubic cell adds one full interior particle to those corners, so Z=2.

Additional worked example

How many atoms are in a face-centered cubic conventional unit cell?

Eight corners contribute 8(1/8)=1 and six faces contribute 6(1/2)=3; total Z=4.

Clarification. A drawing of a cell displays parts shared with neighbors; counting every visible corner atom as one overcounts.

Predict direction with entropy and free energy

Idea. Entropy counts how energy and matter can be distributed. At fixed temperature and pressure, negative reaction Gibbs free-energy change favors forward change toward equilibrium. It says nothing about reaction speed. For an isolated whole, total entropy does not decrease spontaneously.

Decision. Convert T to kelvins and use consistent energy units for ΔH and TΔS. Check the signs: ΔH<0 and ΔS>0 favors forward change at all positive temperatures; ΔH>0 and ΔS>0 favors it only above a threshold in this constant-value approximation.

Worked case. If ΔH=+20 kJ/mol and ΔS=+100 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, at 300 K the entropy term is 30 kJ/mol and ΔG=−10 kJ/mol.

Additional worked example

For the same approximate ΔH and ΔS at 100 K, what is ΔG and is the forward reaction favored?

TΔS=10 kJ/mol, so ΔG=+10 kJ/mol. Forward change is not favored under these conditions.

Clarification. “Thermodynamically favored” does not mean fast; an activation barrier can make a favorable reaction extremely slow.

Determine a rate law from data

Idea. A reaction rate measures concentration change per time. Reaction orders in an observed rate law come from experiment and need not equal coefficients in the overall balanced equation.

Decision. Compare two experiments that hold all but one reactant concentration fixed. Determine how the rate factor changes, infer its exponent, repeat for other reactants, and then solve for k with consistent units.

Worked case. If doubling [A] at fixed [B] doubles rate, m=1. If tripling [B] at fixed [A] multiplies rate by nine, n=2. The law is rate=k[A][B]².

Additional worked example

A reaction rate quadruples when [A] doubles with other concentrations fixed. What is the order in A?

Since 2^m=4, m=2; it is second order in A.

Clarification. An overall stoichiometric coefficient cannot be copied into a rate law unless the reaction step is established as elementary.

Separate mechanism and activation energy

Idea. A mechanism proposes elementary steps whose sum is the overall reaction. An intermediate is formed and later consumed; a catalyst is consumed and regenerated. The slowest relevant step can constrain rate, but a proposed mechanism must reproduce the observed rate law.

Decision. Check that intermediates cancel and the overall equation matches. For temperature effects, the Arrhenius equation shows why a higher temperature usually increases k. A catalyst supplies a lower-barrier route; it does not change the reaction’s equilibrium constant at a fixed temperature.

Worked case. For a fixed activation energy E_a>0, raising T makes −E_a/(RT) less negative, so k rises. A catalyst can speed both forward and reverse approaches to equilibrium.

Additional worked example

Does adding a catalyst change K for a reversible reaction at the same temperature?

No. It changes the pathway and speeds approach to equilibrium; it does not change the equilibrium composition set by K at that temperature.

Clarification. A favorable ΔG does not remove activation energy. Rate and equilibrium answer different questions.

Use reaction quotient before predicting direction

Idea. For aA+bB ⇌ cC+dD, the equilibrium expression uses activities, often approximated by concentrations for dilute solutions. Pure solids and liquids have activity near one and are omitted. At a fixed temperature, Q compares the current state with K.

Decision. Write a balanced reaction first, then form Q using only appropriate species and exponents. If Q<K, the net reaction proceeds forward until equilibrium; if Q>K, it proceeds in reverse.

Worked case. For H₂+I₂ ⇌ 2HI, K=4. If concentrations are [HI]=1.0 M and [H₂]=[I₂]=1.0 M, Q=1<4, so net formation of HI is favored.

Additional worked example

For the same reaction and K=4, what direction is favored if [HI]=3.0 M and [H₂]=[I₂]=1.0 M?

Q=3²/(1·1)=9>4, so the mixture shifts toward H₂ and I₂.

Clarification. A large K describes equilibrium preference, not instantaneous speed. Use Q to decide direction from a non-equilibrium starting mixture.

Respond to a disturbance without guessing

Idea. An equilibrium system responds to a changed condition until a new equilibrium is reached. Adding a species or changing volume changes Q initially; changing temperature can change K because the thermal energy of reactants and products differs.

Decision. First calculate or reason how Q changes while K is fixed, then choose the direction that restores Q=K. For temperature, treat heat as a reaction term qualitatively or use thermodynamic evidence. A catalyst changes neither K nor the equilibrium position.

Worked case. For N₂+3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, reducing volume raises all partial pressures. Because the product side has fewer gas moles, the new equilibrium is shifted toward NH₃ under the ideal-gas model.

Additional worked example

For an exothermic forward reaction at equilibrium, what happens to K when temperature rises?

K decreases for an exothermic forward reaction; higher temperature favors the endothermic reverse direction.

Clarification. Do not say “pressure shift” when an inert gas is added at constant volume: the reacting gases’ partial pressures stay the same in the ideal model.

Distinguish acid strength from concentration

Idea. An acid donates a proton and a base accepts one in the Brønsted–Lowry model. Strong acids dissociate essentially completely in dilute water; weak acids establish an equilibrium. Concentration says how much acid is present, while strength says how extensively it ionizes.

Decision. Identify the acid–base pair and whether the species is strong or weak. At 25 °C in dilute water, pH+pOH≈14 because Kw≈1.0×10⁻¹⁴. For concentrated or nonideal solutions, activities and temperature matter.

Worked case. A dilute strong monoprotic acid at 1.0×10⁻³ M gives [H⁺]≈1.0×10⁻³ M and pH≈3.00, if activity corrections and water autoionization are negligible.

Additional worked example

At 25 °C, what are pH and pOH for an ideal dilute solution with [OH⁻]=1.0×10⁻⁴ M?

pOH=4.00 and pH≈10.00.

Clarification. A 0.001 M strong acid can be less acidic than a sufficiently concentrated weak acid; “strong” is not a synonym for “concentrated”.

Solve a weak-acid equilibrium

Idea. A weak acid HA only partly ionizes: HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻. Its acid-dissociation constant relates equilibrium concentrations in the dilute approximation. An ICE table connects an initial concentration to the final ionization extent.

Decision. Write initial, change, and equilibrium amounts. If the small-x assumption x≪C is used, verify it afterward. Otherwise solve the quadratic. For a conjugate pair at 25 °C, KaKb=Kw.

Worked case. For HA initially 0.10 M with Ka=1.0×10⁻⁵, x≈√(KaC)=1.0×10⁻³ M; x/C≈1%, so the approximation is reasonable and pH≈3.00.

Additional worked example

For an acid with Ka=1.0×10⁻⁵ at initial concentration 0.40 M, estimate [H⁺] using the small-x approximation.

[H⁺]≈√(4.0×10⁻⁶)=2.0×10⁻³ M. The fractional ionization is 0.5%, so the approximation is consistent.

Clarification. Never apply [H⁺]≈C to a weak acid without checking ionization; never use √(KaC) without checking the approximation.

Use buffers as a ratio of conjugates

Idea. A buffer contains appreciable weak acid and conjugate base, allowing small additions of acid or base to be consumed. The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation expresses a concentration-ratio approximation for a suitable buffer.

Decision. React added strong acid or base stoichiometrically with the buffer component first. Then use the remaining acid/base ratio only if both components remain and activity effects are modest.

Worked case. At equal HA and A⁻ amounts, the ratio is 1, its log is zero, and pH≈pKa. Adding strong acid converts some A⁻ into HA, decreasing the ratio and pH.

Additional worked example

A buffer has pKa=4.8 and [A⁻]/[HA]=10. Estimate its pH.

pH≈4.8+log10(10)=5.8.

Clarification. The ratio equation cannot rescue a buffer after one component is exhausted by too much added strong acid or base.

Read titration stoichiometry before the curve

Idea. At equivalence, stoichiometric amounts have reacted; the pH there depends on acid and base strength, not merely on the fact that equivalents match. The half-equivalence point of a weak-acid/strong-base titration has pH≈pKa in a suitable dilute model.

Decision. Calculate moles before mixing and identify the region: before equivalence, at equivalence, or after. Use a buffer relation only before equivalence when both weak acid and conjugate base are present.

Worked case. Titrating 25.0 mL of 0.100 M monoprotic acid requires 25.0 mL of 0.100 M NaOH for 1:1 equivalence. A weak acid’s equivalence solution is generally basic because its conjugate base hydrolyzes.

Additional worked example

How many mL of 0.200 M NaOH neutralize 20.0 mL of 0.100 M HCl?

HCl amount is 0.0200 L×0.100 M=0.00200 mol. Required NaOH volume is 0.00200/0.200=0.0100 L=10.0 mL.

Clarification. Equivalence and pH 7 coincide for an ideal strong-acid/strong-base titration at 25 °C, not for every acid–base pair.

Compare ion product with solubility product

Idea. A sparingly soluble solid establishes a dissolution equilibrium. Ksp is written from dissolved ions, each raised to its coefficient; the pure solid is omitted. The current ion product Qsp predicts whether precipitation is thermodynamically favored.

Decision. Write the dissolution equation first. Compare Qsp with Ksp: Qsp>Ksp favors precipitation, Qsp<Ksp favors dissolution. Account for dilution when two solutions are mixed, and for common ions already present.

Worked case. For MX₂(s) ⇌ M²⁺ + 2X⁻, a molar solubility s in pure water gives [M²⁺]=s and [X⁻]=2s, so Ksp=4s³.

Additional worked example

If Ksp=4.0×10⁻¹² for this idealized MX₂ salt in pure water, estimate s.

4s³=4.0×10⁻¹², so s³=1.0×10⁻¹² and s=1.0×10⁻⁴ M.

Clarification. Ksp cannot be compared directly with another salt’s Ksp to rank solubility when their ion stoichiometries differ.

Name coordination species with oxidation states

Idea. Transition-metal ions often bind ligands to form coordination complexes. Coordination number counts donor atoms attached to the metal, not merely the number of ligand molecules. Variable oxidation states and ligand-field effects help explain color and magnetism.

Decision. Identify ligand charges and the overall complex charge, then solve the metal oxidation state. Count donor atoms for coordination number; a bidentate ligand contributes two donor atoms.

Worked case. In [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺, NH₃ is neutral, so Cu has oxidation state +2 and four monodentate donors give coordination number four.

Additional worked example

Find the metal oxidation state and coordination number in [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻, treating each CN⁻ as a monodentate ligand.

Six CN⁻ contribute −6; x−6=−4 gives Fe(+2). Six donors give coordination number six.

Clarification. The subscript on a ligand does not by itself give the metal oxidation state; include every ligand charge.

Balance nuclei and use half life

Idea. Nuclear transformations change nuclei rather than ordinary chemical bonds. A balanced nuclear equation conserves mass number and atomic number across its sides. Radioactive decay is probabilistic for one nucleus but follows a predictable exponential law for a large sample.

Decision. Identify the emitted particle and balance A and Z separately. For a half-life problem, count elapsed half-lives or use the exponential form. The half-life is characteristic of the radionuclide and is not usually changed by ordinary chemical conditions.

Worked case. An 80 mg sample with a 5 day half-life has 40 mg after 5 days and 20 mg after 10 days.

Additional worked example

What fraction remains after three half-lives?

(1/2)³=1/8, or 12.5% of the original radioactive nuclei.

Clarification. A half-life does not mean the entire sample disappears at that time.

Recognize organic connectivity and functional groups

Idea. Organic molecules can have the same molecular formula but different connectivity or spatial arrangement. Functional groups indicate characteristic patterns of bonding and reactivity, though the whole molecule and conditions still matter.

Decision. Count C, H, heteroatoms, bonds, and rings. Distinguish structural isomers from conformations. Locate common groups such as alkene C=C, alcohol O–H attached to carbon, carbonyl C=O, carboxylic acid –COOH, and amine nitrogen.

Worked case. Ethanol CH₃CH₂OH and dimethyl ether CH₃OCH₃ share C₂H₆O but differ in connectivity and functional group; they are constitutional isomers.

Additional worked example

Are CH₃CH₂CH₂OH and CH₃CHOHCH₃ identical structures or constitutional isomers?

They are constitutional isomers: both are C₃H₈O alcohols, but the OH group attaches to different carbon atoms.

Clarification. A molecular formula alone cannot uniquely determine structure or properties.

Make chemical claims with balances and exposure

Idea. Environmental chemistry applies matter balance, reaction pathways, phase behavior, and dose or exposure. A substance’s hazard and actual risk are different: risk also depends on amount, route, duration, and context. Claims about pollutants need identified species and conditions.

Decision. For a claim about air or water, write the relevant species, source and sink, transport medium, and measurable concentration unit. Check whether a reaction creates or removes the species under the stated conditions before asserting a benefit.

Worked case. Carbon monoxide from incomplete combustion is not interchangeable with carbon dioxide from complete combustion. Measuring CO in air requires a concentration such as ppm and an exposure period; “some CO” alone cannot quantify risk.

Additional worked example

Why is “the water contains 5 mg of pollutant” inadequate for comparing two water samples?

It gives total mass without sample volume. A concentration such as mg/L, the pollutant identity, and relevant exposure context are needed.

Clarification. A dramatic chemical name or a single mass value is not a quantitative exposure assessment.
