Atomic structure

Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons. Z (protons) fixes the element; A counts protons plus neutrons; the charge counts missing or extra electrons.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Method

How to count particles

  1. Find Z from the element symbol (periodic table): protons = Z.
  2. Neutrons = A − Z, using the mass number of the stated isotope.
  3. Electrons = Z − charge (subtract a positive charge, add the size of a negative charge).
  4. For average atomic mass, convert percentages to fractions and form Σ(fraction × mass).
  5. Check: a cation has fewer electrons than protons; an anion has more.

Key formulas

  • n = A − ZNeutrons — A is the mass number of a specific isotope, not the average atomic mass.
  • electrons = Z − qElectrons in an ion — q is the charge with its sign: Mg2+ (q = +2) has 12 − 2 = 10; Cl− (q = −1) has 17 + 1 = 18.
  • Mavg = Σ (fractional abundance × isotopic mass)Average atomic mass — Abundances as fractions that add to 1 (divide percentages by 100).

Worked examples

Neutrons in an isotope

Problem. An atom has mass number 23 and atomic number 11. How many neutrons does it have, and what element is it?

  1. n = A − Z = 23 − 11 = 12.

    Mass number counts protons and neutrons.

  2. Z = 11 is sodium.

    The proton number identifies the element.

Result: 12 neutrons; the atom is sodium-23.

What it means: Any atom with 11 protons is sodium, whatever its neutron number.

Electrons in an ion

Problem. How many electrons are in Mg2+ (Z = 12) and in S2− (Z = 16)?

  1. Mg2+: 12 − 2 = 10 electrons.

    A 2+ charge means two electrons fewer than protons.

  2. S2−: 16 + 2 = 18 electrons.

    A 2− charge means two extra electrons.

Result: Mg2+: 10 electrons; S2−: 18 electrons.

What it means: Ionisation never changes the proton number or the element.

Average atomic mass

Problem. Boron is 19.9% ¹⁰B (10.013 u) and 80.1% ¹¹B (11.009 u). Find its average atomic mass.

  1. 0.199 × 10.013 + 0.801 × 11.009.

    Weighted average with fractional abundances.

  2. = 1.993 + 8.818 = 10.81 u.

    Add the contributions.

Result: 10.81 u (as on the periodic table).

What it means: The average is closer to 11 because boron-11 is about four times more abundant.

Abundance from the average mass

Problem. Copper has isotopes ⁶³Cu (62.930 u) and ⁶⁵Cu (64.928 u); its average atomic mass is 63.546 u. Find the abundance of ⁶³Cu.

  1. Let x be the fraction of ⁶³Cu; then 1 − x is ⁶⁵Cu.

    Two isotopes whose fractions add to 1.

  2. 62.930x + 64.928(1 − x) = 63.546.

    Weighted-average equation.

  3. 64.928 − 1.998x = 63.546 ⇒ x = 1.382 / 1.998 = 0.692.

    Solve for x.

Result: About 69.2% ⁶³Cu (and 30.8% ⁶⁵Cu).

What it means: The accepted natural abundance of ⁶³Cu is 69.15%, which agrees.

Where students go wrong

  • Misunderstanding: Using the periodic-table atomic mass as A to find neutrons.

    Correct idea: A is a whole number for a specific isotope. Chlorine’s 35.45 is an average, not a mass number.

  • Misunderstanding: Changing the number of protons when an ion forms.

    Correct idea: Only electrons are gained or lost in chemical changes.

  • Misunderstanding: Adding electrons for a positive charge.

    Correct idea: Positive means electrons were lost: electrons = Z − (positive charge).

  • Misunderstanding: “Isotopes are different elements.”

    Correct idea: Isotopes have the same Z, so they are the same element with different masses.

Read atoms, isotopes, and ions

Situation. A symbol has a mass number or charge, or a question asks for particles.

Meaning. Atomic number Z counts protons and defines the element. Mass number A counts protons plus neutrons. An ion differs from the neutral atom only in electron count; isotopes differ in neutron count.

First move. Read Z and A first. Set protons=Z and neutrons=A−Z. Let zion be the signed ionic charge number, such as +3 or −1; then electrons=Z−zion.

Worked case. For ⁵⁶Fe³⁺, Z=26: it has 26 protons, 30 neutrons, and 23 electrons.

Additional worked example

How many protons, neutrons, and electrons are in ³⁵Cl⁻?

Chlorine has Z=17. The ion has 17 protons, 35−17=18 neutrons, and 18 electrons because its charge is −1.

Explanation. A positive ion lost electrons; its nucleus did not lose protons.

Quantum theory

Light comes in photons with energy E = hν. Electrons in atoms can only have certain energies, so atoms absorb and emit light only at particular wavelengths. Electrons are described by orbitals labelled by quantum numbers.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Method

Photon and spectrum calculations

  1. Convert wavelengths to metres (nm × 10⁻⁹).
  2. Use c = λν to switch between wavelength and frequency.
  3. Use E = hν = hc/λ for one photon; multiply by NA and divide by 1000 for kJ/mol.
  4. For hydrogen transitions, compute ΔE from the level formula, then λ = hc/|ΔE|.
  5. Check the region: 400–700 nm is visible; shorter is UV, longer is IR.

Key formulas

  • c = λνWave relation — All electromagnetic radiation in vacuum.
  • E = hν = hc / λPhoton energy — Energy of one photon. Multiply by NA for a mole of photons.
  • En = −2.18 × 10−18 J / n2Hydrogen energy levels (Bohr) — Hydrogen atom (one electron) only. Multi-electron atoms need the full quantum model.
  • ΔE = 2.18 × 10−18 J (1 / nf2 − 1 / ni2) ; |ΔE| = hc / λTransition energy — Hydrogen. ΔE < 0 for emission (n_i > n_f).
  • λ = h / mvde Broglie wavelength — Any moving particle; significant only for very small masses such as electrons.
  • n2 orbitals and 2n2 electrons per shell; 2l + 1 orbitals per subshellOrbital counts — s: 1 orbital, p: 3, d: 5, f: 7.

Worked examples

Energy of a photon

Problem. Find the energy of one photon of green light (λ = 500 nm) and of one mole of such photons.

  1. E = hc / λ = (6.626 × 10−34)(2.998 × 108) / 500 × 10−9 = 3.97 × 10−19 J.

    Convert nm to m first.

  2. Per mole: 3.97 × 10−19 × 6.022 × 1023 = 2.39 × 105 J/mol = 239 kJ/mol.

    Multiply by Avogadro’s number.

Result: 3.97 × 10−19 J per photon; 239 kJ/mol.

What it means: This is comparable to chemical bond energies, which is why visible and UV light can drive chemical reactions (photosynthesis, fading of dyes).

Scaling with wavelength

Problem. A photon’s wavelength is halved. By what factor does its energy change? If its frequency is tripled instead?

  1. E = hc/λ: halving λ doubles E.

    Energy is inversely proportional to wavelength.

  2. E = hν: tripling ν triples E.

    Energy is proportional to frequency.

Result: ×2 and ×3.

What it means: Blue photons (≈ 450 nm) carry about 1.5 times the energy of red ones (≈ 680 nm).

Frequency from wavelength

Problem. A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz. Find the wavelength.

  1. λ = c / ν = 2.998 × 108 / 1.00 × 108 = 3.00 m.

    c = λν.

Result: λ ≈ 3.0 m.

What it means: Radio photons carry very little energy (6.6 × 10⁻²⁶ J): far too little to break bonds.

Ionisation energy of hydrogen

Problem. How much energy is needed to remove the electron from a ground-state hydrogen atom? Express it per mole.

  1. From n = 1 to n = ∞: ΔE = 0 − (−2.18 × 10−18) = 2.18 × 10−18 J.

    E_∞ = 0.

  2. × 6.022 × 1023 = 1.31 × 106 J/mol = 1312 kJ/mol.

    Per mole.

Result: 2.18 × 10−18 J per atom = 1312 kJ/mol.

What it means: This agrees with the measured first ionisation energy of hydrogen.

Allowed quantum numbers

Problem. Which set is not allowed: (a) n = 2, l = 1, ml = −1; (b) n = 3, l = 3, ml = 0; (c) n = 4, l = 2, ml = +2?

  1. (a) l = 1 < 2 and |ml| ≤ 1: allowed (a 2p orbital).

    Check l < n and −l ≤ m_l ≤ l.

  2. (b) l = 3 is not less than n = 3: not allowed.

    l can be at most n − 1 = 2.

  3. (c) l = 2 < 4 and ml = +2 ≤ 2: allowed (a 4d orbital).

    Both rules satisfied.

Result: Set (b) is not allowed.

What it means: This is why there are no “3f” orbitals.

Where students go wrong

  • Misunderstanding: Forgetting to convert nm to m.

    Correct idea: Multiply nanometres by 10⁻⁹ before using c or hc.

  • Misunderstanding: “Brighter light has more energetic photons.”

    Correct idea: Brightness is the number of photons; each photon’s energy depends only on its frequency.

  • Misunderstanding: Applying E_n = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸/n² J to atoms other than hydrogen.

    Correct idea: The Bohr formula works for one-electron species only.

  • Misunderstanding: Thinking of orbitals as fixed circular paths.

    Correct idea: Orbitals are probability regions; the electron has no definite path.

  • Misunderstanding: Allowing l = n.

    Correct idea: l ranges from 0 to n − 1.

Connect wavelength to photon energy

Situation. Light has a wavelength, frequency, or photon energy.

Meaning. Light frequency and wavelength are inversely related at a fixed propagation speed; each photon carries energy proportional to frequency. Atomic spectra reflect discrete energy differences.

First move. Convert wavelength to meters. Use ν=c/λ and E=hν for one photon. Shorter wavelength means higher photon energy.

Worked case. A 500 nm photon has energy (6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s)(3.00×10⁸ m/s)/(500×10⁻⁹ m)≈3.98×10⁻¹⁹ J.

Additional worked example

Which has greater photon energy, 400 nm or 800 nm light, and by what factor?

The 400 nm photon has twice the frequency and twice the energy.

Explanation. Light intensity changes photon count; it does not change the energy of a photon at fixed frequency.

Fill orbitals before predicting behavior

Situation. An electron configuration or unpaired-electron count is needed.

Meaning. Subshell capacities follow the number of orbitals: s holds 2, p 6, d 10, f 14 electrons. Hund filling maximizes unpaired electrons before pairing within equal-energy orbitals.

First move. Count electrons from atomic number, adjust for ion charge, fill orbitals in energy order, and remove electrons from the highest principal shell first for common transition-metal cations.

Worked case. Neutral Fe is [Ar]4s²3d⁶; Fe²⁺ loses the two 4s electrons and is [Ar]3d⁶.

Additional worked example

Write the condensed configuration of Mg²⁺.

Neutral Mg is [Ne]3s²; losing two 3s electrons gives [Ne].

Explanation. Writing a transition-metal cation by removing 3d electrons before 4s often gives the wrong configuration.

Periodic table

Electron configurations follow from filling orbitals in order of energy. The periodic table groups elements with similar outer electrons, and effective nuclear charge explains the trends in size, ionisation energy and electronegativity.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Method

Writing configurations and predicting trends

  1. Count the electrons (Z for a neutral atom; adjust for charge).
  2. Fill subshells in the Aufbau order, respecting capacities; use noble-gas shorthand.
  3. For orbital diagrams, apply Hund’s rule; count unpaired electrons.
  4. For cations of transition metals, remove the ns electrons first.
  5. For trends, compare positions: same period → Z_eff argument; same group → number of shells argument. Mention known exceptions.

Key formulas

  • 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p 6s 4f 5d 6p …Filling order — Ground states of most atoms. Exceptions include Cr ([Ar]3d⁵4s¹) and Cu ([Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹).
  • s: 2, p: 6, d: 10, f: 14 electronsCapacity — Each orbital holds 2 electrons of opposite spin (Pauli).
  • Zeff ≈ Z − (core electrons)Valence effective charge (estimate) — A rough guide for comparing main-group atoms; refined models (Slater’s rules) are beyond this topic.
  • remove ns before (n − 1)dIon formation, transition metals — Fe → Fe2+: remove the two 4s electrons.

Worked examples

Configuration and unpaired electrons of oxygen

Problem. Write the ground-state configuration of oxygen (Z = 8) and find the number of unpaired electrons.

  1. 1s2 2s2 2p4.

    Fill 1s, 2s, then 2p with the remaining four electrons.

  2. 2p has three orbitals: put one electron in each, then pair the fourth: ↑↓ ↑ ↑.

    Hund’s rule.

Result: 1s²2s²2p⁴, with 2 unpaired electrons (oxygen atoms are paramagnetic).

What it means: A 2p³ configuration (nitrogen) would have 3 unpaired electrons.

A transition-metal ion

Problem. Write configurations for Fe (Z = 26) and Fe3+.

  1. Fe: [Ar]3d64s2.

    18 core electrons + 8.

  2. Fe3+: remove the two 4s electrons, then one 3d: [Ar]3d5.

    Remove ns before (n − 1)d.

Result: Fe = [Ar]3d⁶4s²; Fe³⁺ = [Ar]3d⁵.

What it means: Writing Fe³⁺ as [Ar]3d³4s² is a common error; the 4s electrons go first.

Electrons needed for a noble-gas configuration

Problem. An atom has shell arrangement 2, 8, 7. How many electrons must it gain to complete an octet? Which element and ion is it?

  1. Outer shell has 7; an octet needs 8: gain 1.

    Main-group atoms tend to reach 8 valence electrons.

  2. Total electrons 17 ⇒ chlorine; ion Cl−.

    Z = 17.

Result: Gains 1 electron to form Cl− (configuration of argon).

What it means: Halogens (group 17) form 1− ions; alkali metals (2, 8, 1 etc.) form 1+ ions.

Compare sizes and ionisation energies

Problem. Arrange Na, Mg and K in order of increasing atomic radius, and of increasing first ionisation energy.

  1. Na and Mg are in period 3; Mg has higher Zeff, so Mg is smaller than Na.

    Across a period.

  2. K is below Na (an extra shell), so K is larger than Na.

    Down a group.

  3. Ionisation energy follows the reverse order.

    Smaller atoms hold electrons more tightly.

Result: Radius: Mg < Na < K. First ionisation energy: K < Na < Mg (419 < 496 < 738 kJ/mol).

What it means: This is why potassium reacts with water more violently than sodium.

Isoelectronic ions

Problem. Order O2−, F−, Na+ and Mg2+ by size.

  1. All have 10 electrons (the neon configuration).

    Same electron count.

  2. Their proton numbers are 8, 9, 11, 12: more protons pull the same electrons closer.

    Higher nuclear charge, smaller ion.

Result: Mg²⁺ < Na⁺ < F⁻ < O²⁻ (smallest to largest).

What it means: For isoelectronic species, size decreases as Z increases.

Where students go wrong

  • Misunderstanding: Filling 3d before 4s for neutral atoms, or removing 3d before 4s for ions.

    Correct idea: Fill 4s before 3d; ionise 4s before 3d.

  • Misunderstanding: Pairing electrons before filling each orbital of a subshell singly.

    Correct idea: Hund’s rule: singly first, parallel spins.

  • Misunderstanding: “Atoms get bigger across a period because they have more electrons.”

    Correct idea: They get smaller: the added electrons are in the same shell while Z_eff increases.

  • Misunderstanding: Treating trends as rules with no exceptions.

    Correct idea: Be/B and N/O ionisation dips, and Cr/Cu configurations, are the standard exceptions.

Explain a periodic trend with attraction

Situation. The question ranks atom size or ionization energy.

Meaning. Across a period, increasing effective nuclear charge often pulls valence electrons inward. Down a group, an added shell usually increases atomic radius. Ionization energy generally moves opposite atomic radius.

First move. Identify period and group first. Use shell number for a vertical comparison and nuclear attraction or shielding for a horizontal comparison, then look for known exceptions when precision matters.

Worked case. Across period 3, Na is larger than Cl. The stronger effective attraction in Cl helps hold its valence electrons more tightly.

Additional worked example

Which neutral atom is larger, K or Na? Which usually has the lower first ionization energy?

K is larger because it has an additional occupied shell and generally has the lower first ionization energy.

Explanation. Do not apply a simple across-period trend to compare an atom with its ion without accounting for electron gain or loss.

Chemical bonding

Atoms bond by transferring electrons (ionic) or sharing them (covalent). Lewis structures count the electrons; VSEPR turns electron domains into shapes; shape and electronegativity decide polarity.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Method

From formula to polarity

  1. Count the total valence electrons (adjust for ion charge).
  2. Choose the central atom (usually the least electronegative, never H) and draw single bonds to the outer atoms.
  3. Complete octets of outer atoms, then place remaining electrons on the central atom.
  4. If the central atom lacks an octet, form double or triple bonds. Check formal charges; consider resonance.
  5. Count electron domains on the central atom → electron geometry → molecular shape (atoms only).
  6. Decide polarity: are there polar bonds, and do their dipoles cancel by symmetry?

Key formulas

  • Σ(valence electrons of atoms) − (ion charge)Total valence electrons — For anions the charge is negative, so electrons are added: CO₃²⁻ has 4 + 3(6) + 2 = 24.
  • FC = V − L − BFormal charge — V = valence electrons of the free atom; L = electrons in lone pairs on the atom; B = number of bonds (shared pairs). The formal charges add up to the overall charge.
  • total positive charge = total negative chargeIonic formula — Ionic compounds are neutral: Al³⁺ and O²⁻ give Al₂O₃ (2 × 3 = 3 × 2).
  • 2 domains: linear 180°; 3: trigonal planar 120°; 4: tetrahedral 109.5°; 5: trigonal bipyramidal; 6: octahedralVSEPR geometry — Domains around the central atom; lone pairs compress angles slightly.

Worked examples

Counting valence electrons

Problem. How many valence electrons are in CO₂, H₂O and the nitrate ion NO₃⁻?

  1. CO₂: 4 + 2 × 6 = 16.

    C has 4, each O 6.

  2. H₂O: 2 × 1 + 6 = 8.

    Each H has 1.

  3. NO₃⁻: 5 + 3 × 6 + 1 = 24.

    Add one electron for the 1− charge.

Result: 16, 8 and 24.

What it means: Every structure you draw must use exactly this many electrons.

Lewis structure and shape of ammonia

Problem. Draw the Lewis structure of NH₃ and predict its shape and polarity.

  1. Valence electrons: 5 + 3(1) = 8 (4 pairs).

    Count.

  2. Three N–H bonds use 3 pairs; the fourth pair is a lone pair on N.

    N has an octet; each H has 2.

  3. Four domains → tetrahedral electron geometry; one lone pair → trigonal pyramidal shape, ≈107°.

    VSEPR.

  4. N–H bonds are polar (3.04 vs 2.20) and the pyramid is not symmetric, so the dipoles do not cancel.

    Polarity.

Result: Trigonal pyramidal and polar.

What it means: This polarity, with hydrogen bonding, makes ammonia very soluble in water.

Formal charge and resonance: nitrate

Problem. Draw NO₃⁻ and find the formal charges.

  1. 24 electrons; N central with three O atoms.

    Count and skeleton.

  2. After completing octets, N needs one double bond: one N=O and two N–O.

    Complete the central octet.

  3. FC: N = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1; double-bonded O = 6 − 4 − 2 = 0; each single-bonded O = 6 − 6 − 1 = −1. Sum: +1 + 0 − 1 − 1 = −1 ✓.

    Formal charges add to the ion’s charge.

  4. The double bond can be to any of the three O atoms: three equivalent resonance structures.

    Resonance.

Result: A resonance hybrid: all three N–O bonds are identical, between single and double in length; trigonal planar, 120°.

What it means: Resonance structures are not flipping back and forth; the real ion is one average structure.

Formula of an ionic compound

Problem. Write the formulas of the compounds formed by (a) Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻, (b) Al³⁺ and O²⁻.

  1. (a) One Mg²⁺ balances two Cl⁻: MgCl₂.

    +2 − 2 = 0.

  2. (b) Lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6: two Al³⁺ (+6) and three O²⁻ (−6): Al₂O₃.

    Balance the charges.

Result: MgCl₂ and Al₂O₃.

What it means: Subscripts come from balancing charges; they are not the charges themselves.

Polar bonds, non-polar molecule

Problem. CCl₄ has four polar C–Cl bonds. Is the molecule polar?

  1. Carbon has four bonding domains and no lone pairs: tetrahedral.

    VSEPR.

  2. Four identical bond dipoles arranged symmetrically in a tetrahedron cancel.

    Vector sum is zero.

Result: CCl₄ is non-polar.

What it means: CH₂Cl₂, with two different kinds of bond, is polar.

Where students go wrong

  • Misunderstanding: Calling NaCl a molecule.

    Correct idea: It is an ionic lattice; NaCl is its formula unit (ratio).

  • Misunderstanding: Counting a double bond as two domains in VSEPR.

    Correct idea: Any bond counts as one domain.

  • Misunderstanding: Naming the shape from the electron geometry.

    Correct idea: H₂O has tetrahedral electron geometry but a bent molecular shape.

  • Misunderstanding: “A molecule with polar bonds is polar.”

    Correct idea: Symmetric shapes (CO₂, CCl₄, BF₃) cancel bond dipoles.

  • Misunderstanding: Forgetting the ion charge when counting electrons.

    Correct idea: Anions add electrons; cations subtract.

Build a Lewis structure that accounts for charge

Situation. Connectivity, lone pairs, or formal charge are requested.

Meaning. A Lewis structure tracks valence electrons in bonds and lone pairs. Formal charge is a bookkeeping tool for comparing plausible structures, not a measured ionic charge at every atom.

First move. Count total valence electrons including ion charge. Connect atoms, fill outer octets when applicable, place remaining electrons centrally, and use multiple bonds if needed. Calculate formal charges and check total.

Worked case. For CO₂, O=C=O uses 16 valence electrons, gives octets, and has zero formal charge on all atoms.

Additional worked example

How many valence electrons does NO₃⁻ have before drawing bonds?

N contributes 5, three O contribute 18, and the −1 charge adds 1: total 24 electrons.

Explanation. An octet alone does not prove the best structure; check formal charge, atom placement, and valid exceptions.

Turn electron domains into molecular shape

Situation. A Lewis structure must predict a three-dimensional shape.

Meaning. VSEPR uses the number of electron domains around the central atom. Single, double, and triple bonds each count as one domain; lone pairs affect the molecular shape and angle.

First move. Draw Lewis first, count bonding and lone-pair domains, name the electron geometry, then describe the positions of atoms alone. Use symmetry to test whether bond dipoles cancel.

Worked case. H₂O has four electron domains around O, two bonding and two lone pairs. Its electron geometry is tetrahedral, molecular shape is bent, and it is polar.

Additional worked example

What is the molecular shape of NH₃ and why is it polar?

Four domains with one lone pair give a trigonal-pyramidal shape. Its N–H bond dipoles do not cancel.

Explanation. Electron geometry and molecular geometry differ whenever a central lone pair replaces an atom.

Reactions in solution

In water, many compounds exist as ions. Reactions in solution — precipitation, acid–base and redox — are written as net ionic equations, and their amounts are calculated through moles using balanced equations.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Method

Solving a reaction-in-solution problem

  1. Write and balance the molecular equation; identify the reaction type.
  2. If asked, write the complete ionic and net ionic equations (split only soluble strong electrolytes).
  3. Convert every given quantity to moles (n = m/M or n = cV).
  4. Find the limiting reactant: divide each reactant’s moles by its coefficient; the smallest result is limiting.
  5. Use the mole ratio from the limiting reactant to find moles of product, then convert to the requested unit.
  6. Check charge and atom balance, units and significant figures.

Key formulas

  • n = m / M; n = cVMoles — m in grams, M in g/mol; c in mol/L, V in litres.
  • nB = nA × coefficient of B / coefficient of AMole ratio — Only from a balanced equation, and only for the limiting reactant.
  • % yield = actual yield / theoretical yield × 100%Percent yield — Theoretical yield computed from the limiting reactant.
  • cAVA × b / a = cBVB for aA + bB → productsTitration at equivalence — At the equivalence point. For a 1:1 reaction (HCl + NaOH), cAVA = cBVB.
  • soluble: Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻ compounds (all); most Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻ (except Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺); most SO₄²⁻ (except Ba²⁺, Pb²⁺, Ca²⁺ slightly). Insoluble: most CO₃²⁻, PO₄³⁻, OH⁻, S²⁻ (except with Group 1 and NH₄⁺)Solubility guidelines (summary) — Rules of thumb at room temperature; “insoluble” means very slightly soluble.

Worked examples

Moles from a balanced equation

Problem. For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, how much water forms from 3 mol H₂ with excess O₂? How much O₂ is used?

  1. n(H₂O) = 3 × 2 / 2 = 3 mol.

    Ratio H₂O : H₂ = 2 : 2.

  2. n(O₂) = 3 × 1 / 2 = 1.5 mol.

    Ratio O₂ : H₂ = 1 : 2.

Result: 3 mol H₂O; 1.5 mol O₂ consumed.

What it means: In grams: 3 mol × 18.02 g/mol = 54.1 g of water.

Limiting reactant in a precipitation

Problem. 0.20 mol Ag⁺ is mixed with 0.12 mol Cl⁻ (Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl). Find the maximum amount of AgCl and what is left over.

  1. The ratio is 1 : 1, and there is less Cl⁻.

    Divide each by its coefficient (1): 0.20 vs 0.12.

  2. Cl⁻ is limiting: n(AgCl) = 0.12 mol.

    The limiting reactant fixes the product.

  3. Ag⁺ left: 0.20 − 0.12 = 0.08 mol.

    Excess reactant remains in solution.

Result: 0.12 mol AgCl (≈ 17.2 g, M = 143.32 g/mol); 0.08 mol Ag⁺ remains.

What it means: The reactant present in the larger amount is not automatically in excess; compare moles divided by coefficients.

Mass of precipitate from solution volumes

Problem. 50.0 mL of 0.100 M BaCl₂ is mixed with excess Na₂SO₄. What mass of BaSO₄ precipitates? (M(BaSO₄) = 233.4 g/mol)

  1. Net ionic: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s).

    BaSO₄ is insoluble; Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are spectators.

  2. n(Ba²⁺) = 0.100 × 0.0500 = 5.00 × 10−3 mol.

    n = cV with V in litres.

  3. n(BaSO₄) = 5.00 × 10−3 mol (1 : 1); m = 5.00 × 10−3 × 233.4 = 1.17 g.

    Mole ratio, then mass.

Acid–base titration

  1. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (1 : 1). Net ionic: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

    Strong acid, strong base.

  2. n(NaOH) = 0.150 × 0.0186 = 2.79 × 10−3 mol = n(HCl).

    At equivalence the moles match.

  3. c(HCl) = 2.79 × 10−3 / 0.0250 = 0.112 M.

    c = n/V.

Result: [HCl] = 0.112 M.

What it means: For H₂SO₄, which gives two H⁺ per formula, the NaOH needed would double for the same concentration.

Identify oxidation and reduction

Problem. In Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s), which species is oxidised, which reduced, and what are the oxidising and reducing agents?

  1. Zn: 0 → +2 (loses 2 e⁻): oxidised.

    Oxidation number increases.

  2. Cu: +2 → 0 (gains 2 e⁻): reduced.

    Oxidation number decreases.

  3. Oxidising agent: Cu²⁺ (it takes electrons). Reducing agent: Zn (it gives electrons).

    Each agent is the reactant that causes the other change.

Result: Zn is oxidised (reducing agent); Cu²⁺ is reduced (oxidising agent).

What it means: Half-reactions: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ and Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. This reaction powers the Daniell cell (Electrochemistry).

Percent yield

Problem. A reaction should give 12.0 g of product (theoretical yield) but 10.2 g is collected. Find the percent yield.

  1. 10.2 / 12.0 × 100% = 85.0%.

    Actual over theoretical.

Result: 85.0%.

What it means: A percent yield above 100% signals an error, such as a wet (not fully dried) product.

Properties of solutions

A solution’s composition is described by concentration units (molarity, molality, mass percent, mole fraction). Dilution conserves solute. Dissolved particles lower vapour pressure and freezing point and raise boiling point and osmotic pressure.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Method

Solution calculations

  1. Identify which concentration unit the problem needs (molarity for volumes and reactions; molality for ΔTb, ΔTf; mole fraction for Raoult).
  2. Convert masses to moles and volumes to litres (or solvent masses to kg).
  3. For dilution use c₁V₁ = c₂V₂; for colligative properties decide i from the formula of the solute.
  4. Substitute, keeping units, and check whether the answer is a change (ΔT) or a final value (T).

Key formulas

  • c = n / V; c1V1 = c2V2Molarity and dilution — V is the volume of solution. Dilution: only solvent added, no reaction.
  • m = nsolute / mass of solvent (kg)Molality — Divide by the solvent’s mass, not the solution’s.
  • cgas = kHPgasHenry’s law — Dilute gas solubility at constant temperature; P is the partial pressure of that gas. Some books define the constant the other way round (P = k c); check units.
  • Psolvent = χsolventP°solventRaoult’s law — Ideal solution; non-volatile solute.
  • ΔTb = iKbm; ΔTf = iKfmBoiling and freezing points — Dilute solutions; m is molality; ΔT is the size of the change (boiling point rises, freezing point falls).
  • Π = iMRTOsmotic pressure — Dilute solutions; M molarity, T in kelvin, R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K).

Worked examples

Molarity

Problem. Find the molarity of 0.30 mol of solute in 0.50 L of solution, and of 5.85 g NaCl in 250 mL of solution.

  1. c = 0.30 / 0.50 = 0.60 M.

    c = n/V.

  2. n(NaCl) = 5.85 / 58.44 = 0.100 mol; c = 0.100 / 0.250 = 0.400 M.

    Convert grams to moles and mL to L.

Result: 0.60 M and 0.400 M.

What it means: To prepare the NaCl solution, dissolve 5.85 g and make up to 250 mL in a volumetric flask (not add 250 mL of water).

Dilution

Problem. A 0.20 L sample of 1.5 M solution is diluted to 0.60 L. Find the final concentration.

  1. c2 = c1V1 / V2 = 1.5 × 0.20 / 0.60 = 0.50 M.

    Moles of solute are conserved.

Result: 0.50 M.

What it means: Tripling the volume divides the concentration by three.

Preparing a dilute solution from a stock

Problem. What volume of 12.0 M HCl is needed to make 500 mL of 0.600 M HCl?

  1. V1 = c2V2 / c1 = 0.600 × 500 mL / 12.0 = 25.0 mL.

    Same unit (mL) on both sides.

  2. Add the 25.0 mL of acid to water, then make up to 500 mL.

    Safety: always add acid to water.

Result: 25.0 mL of the stock solution.

What it means: Diluting concentrated acid releases heat; adding it slowly to water avoids splattering.

Antifreeze: freezing-point depression

Problem. Dissolve 0.10 mol glucose in 0.50 kg of water (Kf = 1.86 °C·kg/mol). Find the freezing point.

  1. m = 0.10 mol / 0.50 kg = 0.20 mol/kg.

    Molality: divide by the solvent mass.

  2. ΔTf = iKfm = 1 × 1.86 × 0.20 = 0.372 °C.

    Glucose does not ionise: i = 1.

  3. Tf = 0 − 0.372 = −0.372 °C.

    The freezing point is lowered from 0 °C.

Result: Freezing point ≈ −0.37 °C.

What it means: Using 0.10 mol NaCl instead would give about −0.74 °C (i ≈ 2).

Henry’s law

Problem. At 25 °C, kH for CO₂ in water is 3.4 × 10−2 mol/(L·atm). Find the CO₂ solubility at a CO₂ partial pressure of 2.5 atm (a sealed soft drink), and at 4.0 × 10−4 atm (open air).

  1. Sealed: c = 3.4 × 10−2 × 2.5 = 0.085 M.

    c = k_H P.

  2. Open air: c = 3.4 × 10−2 × 4.0 × 10−4 = 1.4 × 10−5 M.

    Much lower partial pressure.

Result: 0.085 M sealed; 1.4 × 10−5 M in open air.

What it means: Opening the bottle lowers the CO₂ pressure, so gas comes out of solution as bubbles.

Osmotic pressure

Problem. Find the osmotic pressure of 0.10 M glucose at 25 °C.

  1. Π = iMRT = 1 × 0.10 × 0.08206 × 298 = 2.45 atm.

    T in kelvin: 25 + 273 = 298 K.

Result: Π ≈ 2.4 atm.

What it means: Even dilute solutions produce large osmotic pressures; reverse osmosis must exceed them to push water out.

Properties of gases

An ideal gas obeys PV = nRT with T in kelvin. The simple gas laws, gas densities, mixtures (Dalton) and gas stoichiometry all follow, and kinetic-molecular theory explains why.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Method

Gas-law problems

  1. Convert temperature to kelvin and choose pressure/volume units that match your value of R.
  2. If one sample changes conditions, use the combined gas law, cancelling constant variables.
  3. If you need n (or mass), use PV = nRT; for mass, n = m/M.
  4. For reactions producing gases: moles from stoichiometry, then V = nRT/P.
  5. For mixtures, find each partial pressure; for gas collected over water subtract the water vapour pressure.
  6. Check: does heating raise P (or V)? Does compressing raise P?

Key formulas

  • PV = nRTIdeal gas law — Ideal behaviour (low to moderate pressure, not too cold); T in kelvin; units consistent with R.
  • P1V1 / T1 = P2V2 / T2Combined gas law — Fixed amount of gas (n constant). Drop any variable that is also constant.
  • d = PM / RT; M = dRT / PDensity and molar mass — From PV = nRT with n = m/M. Units: d in g/L with R in L·atm/(mol·K) and P in atm.
  • Ptotal = P1 + P2 + …; Pi = χiPtotalDalton’s law — Ideal gases that do not react with each other.
  • urms = √3RT / M; rate1 / rate2 = √M2 / M1Molecular speed and effusion — u_rms with R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and M in kg/mol. Graham’s law at the same T and P.

Worked examples

Boyle’s law

Problem. An ideal gas at 2.0 bar occupies 3.0 L. At constant temperature it is compressed to 1.5 L. Find the new pressure.

  1. P2 = P1V1 / V2 = 2.0 × 3.0 / 1.5 = 4.0 bar.

    n and T constant.

Result: 4.0 bar.

What it means: Halving the volume doubled the pressure.

Heating a sealed container

Problem. A rigid container holds gas at 100 kPa and 300 K. It is heated to 450 K. Find the pressure.

  1. Constant V and n: P1 / T1 = P2 / T2.

    Gay-Lussac’s law.

  2. P2 = 100 × 450 / 300 = 150 kPa.

    Temperatures in kelvin.

Result: 150 kPa.

What it means: Using °C (e.g. 27 °C → 177 °C) directly would wrongly predict a 6.6-fold increase. This is why aerosol cans must not be heated.

Moles in a cylinder

Problem. A 50.0 L cylinder contains nitrogen at 15.0 atm and 25 °C. How many moles and what mass of N₂ does it hold?

  1. n = PV / RT = 15.0 × 50.0 / 0.08206 × 298.15 = 30.7 mol.

    T = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15 K.

  2. m = 30.7 × 28.02 = 859 g.

    M(N₂) = 28.02 g/mol.

Result: ≈ 30.7 mol, about 0.86 kg of N₂.

What it means: At 15 atm the ideal-gas estimate is good to a few percent for nitrogen.

Problem. An unknown gas has density 1.25 g/L at 0 °C and 1.00 atm. Find its molar mass.

  1. M = dRT / P = 1.25 × 0.08206 × 273.15 / 1.00 = 28.0 g/mol.

    Rearranged ideal gas law.

Result: M ≈ 28.0 g/mol (consistent with N₂ or CO).

What it means: Gas density measurements are a classic way to identify gases.

Gas volume from a reaction

Problem. What volume of CO₂ at 25 °C and 1.00 atm is produced when 10.0 g of CaCO₃ decomposes? CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g).

  1. n(CaCO₃) = 10.0 / 100.09 = 0.0999 mol.

    M(CaCO₃) = 100.09 g/mol.

  2. n(CO₂) = 0.0999 mol (1 : 1).

    Mole ratio.

  3. V = nRT / P = 0.0999 × 0.08206 × 298.15 / 1.00 = 2.44 L.

    Ideal gas law.

Result: ≈ 2.44 L of CO₂.

What it means: Coefficients give mole ratios; the gas law converts moles to volume.

Partial pressures

Problem. A mixture contains 0.60 mol N₂ and 0.20 mol O₂ at a total pressure of 2.0 atm. Find each partial pressure.

  1. χ(N₂) = 0.60 / 0.80 = 0.75; χ(O₂) = 0.25.

    Mole fractions.

  2. P(N₂) = 0.75 × 2.0 = 1.5 atm; P(O₂) = 0.25 × 2.0 = 0.50 atm.

    P_i = χ_i P_total.

Result: N₂ 1.5 atm, O₂ 0.50 atm (sum 2.0 atm).

What it means: Oxygen’s partial pressure, not the total pressure, controls how much oxygen dissolves in blood or water.

Graham’s law

Problem. How many times faster does H₂ (M = 2.0 g/mol) effuse than O₂ (M = 32.0 g/mol)?

  1. rate(H2) / rate(O2) = √32.0 / 2.0 = √16 = 4.0.

    Lighter molecules move faster at the same temperature.

Result: H₂ effuses 4.0 times as fast.

What it means: Rates depend on the square root of the mass ratio, not the ratio itself.

Thermochemistry

Reactions release or absorb heat. Measure heat with q = mcΔT (calorimetry); express reaction heats as ΔH; combine them with Hess’s law, enthalpies of formation or bond enthalpies.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Method

Thermochemistry calculations

  1. Decide what is the system and what is the surroundings; assign the sign of q from the direction of heat flow.
  2. Calorimetry: q = mcΔT for the water (plus C_calΔT); q_reaction = −q_surroundings; divide by moles reacted for ΔH per mole.
  3. Hess’s law: arrange the given equations (reverse, multiply) so that they add to the target equation; do the same to their ΔH values.
  4. Formation enthalpies: products minus reactants, each multiplied by its coefficient; elements in standard states count as zero.
  5. Scale ΔH to the actual amount reacting; report sign and unit (kJ or kJ/mol).

Key formulas

  • ΔU = q + w; w = −PextΔVFirst law — Chemistry convention (positive into the system). w = −PΔV for expansion against constant external pressure.
  • q = mcΔTHeat and temperature — No phase change within the temperature range; c approximately constant.
  • qreaction = −(mwatercwaterΔT + CcalΔT)Calorimetry — Insulated calorimeter, no heat lost. Omit C_cal if the calorimeter’s own heat capacity is negligible.
  • ΔHoverall = ΣΔHsteps; reverse: −ΔH; × k: kΔHHess’s law — Always (enthalpy is a state function). States (s, l, g, aq) must match.
  • ΔH°rxn = ΣnΔHf°(products) − ΣnΔHf°(reactants)From formation enthalpies — Standard conditions; n = coefficients; use the correct physical states (H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) differ by 44 kJ/mol).
  • ΔH ≈ Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed)From bond enthalpies (estimate) — Gas-phase estimate using average values; accurate to perhaps 10–20 kJ/mol per bond.

Worked examples

Heat to warm a sample

Problem. Find q for 50 g of a substance with c = 2.0 J/(g·°C) warmed by 3.0 °C.

  1. q = mcΔT = 50 × 2.0 × 3.0 = 300 J.

    Direct substitution.

Result: q = +300 J (heat absorbed by the sample).

What it means: Water (c = 4.184) would need about twice as much heat for the same change.

Coffee-cup calorimetry

Problem. 50.0 mL of 1.0 M HCl and 50.0 mL of 1.0 M NaOH, both at 22.0 °C, are mixed; the temperature rises to 28.7 °C. Assume the solution has mass 100.0 g and c = 4.184 J/(g·°C), and neglect the cup. Find ΔH per mole of water formed.

  1. qsolution = 100.0 × 4.184 × 6.7 = 2.80 × 103 J.

    ΔT = 28.7 − 22.0 = 6.7 °C.

  2. qreaction = −2.80 kJ.

    Heat released by the reaction is absorbed by the solution.

  3. n(H₂O) = 0.0500 L × 1.0 M = 0.050 mol.

    H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, 1 : 1.

  4. ΔH = −2.80 kJ / 0.050 mol = −56 kJ/mol.

    Per mole of reaction.

Result: ΔH ≈ −56 kJ/mol.

What it means: The accepted value for strong acid–strong base neutralisation is about −56 to −57 kJ/mol.

Reversing a reaction

Problem. A reaction has ΔH = −120 kJ as written. Find ΔH for the reverse reaction, and for twice the reverse reaction.

  1. Reverse: +120 kJ.

    Reversing changes the sign.

  2. Twice the reverse: 2 × (+120) = +240 kJ.

    Multiplying coefficients multiplies ΔH.

Result: +120 kJ and +240 kJ.

What it means: If forming a bond releases energy, breaking it costs the same amount.

Enthalpy of combustion from formation enthalpies

Problem. Find ΔH° for CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l).

  1. Products: −393.5 + 2(−285.8) = −965.1 kJ.

    Coefficients multiply ΔH_f°.

  2. Reactants: −74.6 + 2(0) = −74.6 kJ.

    O₂ is an element in its standard state.

  3. ΔH° = −965.1 − (−74.6) = −890.5 kJ.

    Products minus reactants.

Result: ΔH° ≈ −890 kJ per mole of CH₄.

What it means: Burning 1.0 kg of methane (62.3 mol) releases about 5.5 × 10⁴ kJ. With H₂O(g) instead of H₂O(l) the answer would be −802 kJ.

Hess’s law

Problem. A → B: ΔH = +20 kJ; B → C: ΔH = −50 kJ. Find ΔH for A → C and for C → A.

  1. Add the steps: A → C, ΔH = 20 + (−50) = −30 kJ.

    B cancels.

  2. C → A is the reverse: +30 kJ.

    Change the sign.

Result: A → C: −30 kJ; C → A: +30 kJ.

What it means: The path through B does not matter; only the start and end states do.

First law with expansion work

Problem. A system absorbs 100 J of heat and does 30 J of work on its surroundings. Find ΔU.

  1. q = +100 J; w = −30 J (work done by the system).

    Signs from the system’s point of view.

  2. ΔU = 100 + (−30) = 70 J.

    First law.

Result: ΔU = +70 J.

What it means: Adding 130 J would be wrong: energy used to do work leaves the system.

Where students go wrong

  • Misunderstanding: Confusing heat and temperature.

    Correct idea: Heat is energy transferred (J); temperature is a measure of hotness (°C, K).

  • Misunderstanding: Wrong sign in calorimetry.

    Correct idea: If the water warms, the reaction released heat: q_reaction is negative.

  • Misunderstanding: Forgetting to multiply ΔH_f° by the coefficients.

    Correct idea: Use n × ΔH_f° for each species.

  • Misunderstanding: Giving elements a non-zero ΔH_f°.

    Correct idea: Elements in their standard states have ΔH_f° = 0 (but O₃ or C(diamond) do not).

  • Misunderstanding: Mixing up H₂O(l) and H₂O(g).

    Correct idea: Their ΔH_f° values differ by 44 kJ/mol (the enthalpy of vaporisation).

Electrochemistry

Redox reactions can be split so that electrons flow through a wire. Galvanic cells convert chemical energy to electrical energy (E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode); electrolysis uses electricity to drive non-spontaneous reactions (Faraday’s laws).

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Method

Galvanic-cell and electrolysis problems

  1. Write both half-reactions as reductions with their E° values.
  2. The one with the more positive E° is the cathode (reduction); reverse the other for the anode (oxidation).
  3. E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode. Positive means spontaneous.
  4. Balance electrons (multiply half-reactions — without changing E°), add, and cancel; n is the number of electrons transferred.
  5. For electrolysis: Q = It, n(e⁻) = Q/F, then the mole ratio from the half-reaction, then mass.

Key formulas

  • E°cell = E°cathode − E°anodeStandard cell potential — Both E° values are reduction potentials from the table; do not reverse the sign of the anode value and then also subtract it.
  • ΔG° = −nFE°cellSpontaneity — E° > 0 ⇔ ΔG° < 0 ⇔ spontaneous as written (under standard conditions).
  • E = E° − 0.0592 V / n log QNernst equation (25 °C) — T = 298 K; Q is the reaction quotient (products over reactants, with pure solids omitted).
  • n(e−) = It / F; n(product) = n(e−) ÷ (electrons per formula)Faraday’s law of electrolysis — Assumes 100% current efficiency; t in seconds.

Worked examples

Cell potential from two half-reactions

Problem. Cathode and anode reduction potentials are +0.50 V and −0.20 V. Find E°cell. Repeat for +0.80 V and −0.40 V.

  1. E°cell = 0.50 − (−0.20) = 0.70 V.

    Subtracting a negative potential adds its size.

  2. E°cell = 0.80 − (−0.40) = 1.20 V.

    Same rule.

Result: 0.70 V and 1.20 V.

What it means: Both positive: both reactions are spontaneous as written.

A silver–copper cell

Problem. Using the table, find E°cell and the overall reaction for a cell made from Ag⁺/Ag and Cu²⁺/Cu. Write the cell notation.

  1. Ag⁺/Ag (+0.80 V) is more positive: silver is the cathode. Copper is the anode.

    Compare E° values.

  2. E°cell = 0.80 − 0.34 = 0.46 V.

    Cathode minus anode.

  3. Balance electrons: 2Ag⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Ag (E° still +0.80 V); Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻.

    Multiply the silver half-reaction by 2; potential is unchanged.

  4. Overall: Cu(s) + 2Ag⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2Ag(s).

    Add and cancel electrons.

Result: E°cell = +0.46 V. Cell: Cu(s) | Cu²⁺(aq) || Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s).

What it means: Doubling the silver half-reaction’s potential to 1.60 V would be a mistake.

Gibbs energy from cell potential

Problem. Find ΔG° for the Daniell cell (E°cell = 1.10 V, n = 2).

  1. ΔG° = −nFE° = −2 × 96 485 × 1.10 = −2.12 × 105 J/mol.

    Units: C × V = J.

Result: ΔG° ≈ −212 kJ/mol.

What it means: The negative sign confirms spontaneity; this is the maximum electrical work per mole of reaction.

Electroplating copper

Problem. A current of 2.00 A passes through CuSO₄ solution for 30.0 min. What mass of copper is deposited? (M(Cu) = 63.55 g/mol)

  1. Q = It = 2.00 × 1800 = 3600 C.

    30.0 min = 1800 s.

  2. n(e−) = 3600 / 96 485 = 0.0373 mol.

    Faraday’s law.

  3. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu: n(Cu) = 0.0373/2 = 0.01866 mol.

    Two electrons per copper atom.

  4. m = 0.01866 × 63.55 = 1.19 g.

    Moles to mass.

Result: ≈ 1.19 g of copper.

What it means: Doubling the current or the time doubles the mass deposited.

Effect of concentration (Nernst)

Problem. In a Daniell cell at 25 °C, [Zn²⁺] = 1.0 M and [Cu²⁺] = 0.010 M. Find Ecell.

  1. Q = [Zn2+] / [Cu2+] = 1.0 / 0.010 = 100.

    Solids omitted.

  2. E = 1.10 − 0.0592 / 2 log 100 = 1.10 − 0.0296 × 2 = 1.04 V.

    Nernst equation, n = 2.

Result: Ecell ≈ 1.04 V.

What it means: As the cell discharges, Cu²⁺ is used up and the voltage falls gradually.

Where students go wrong

  • Misunderstanding: Multiplying E° when a half-reaction is multiplied.

    Correct idea: E° is intensive: it does not change with the coefficients.

  • Misunderstanding: Reversing the anode’s sign and then also subtracting it.

    Correct idea: Use E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode with table values, or add the oxidation potential — not both.

  • Misunderstanding: “Electrons flow through the salt bridge.”

    Correct idea: Electrons flow in the wire; ions move in the salt bridge and solutions.

  • Misunderstanding: Assuming the anode is always positive.

    Correct idea: In a galvanic cell the anode is negative; in an electrolytic cell it is positive. Oxidation happens at the anode in both.

  • Misunderstanding: Using minutes in Q = It.

    Correct idea: Time must be in seconds.

Classify the material before calculating

Meaning. A pure substance has a fixed chemical identity. Elements contain one kind of atom; compounds contain elements in a fixed ratio. Mixtures can vary in composition and are separated physically.

Choose the dominant intermolecular interaction

Situation. Boiling point, condensation, or miscibility is compared.

Meaning. All particles exhibit dispersion. Polar molecules can have dipole interactions; molecules with H bonded to N, O, or F can form hydrogen bonds with suitable partners. These attractions are between particles, not the covalent bonds inside them.

Between CH₄ and NH₃, which has the higher boiling point and what interaction matters?

NH₃ has the higher boiling point; N–H molecules can hydrogen-bond, while CH₄ relies mainly on dispersion.

Read heating and phase changes

Situation. A heating curve has slopes and plateaus.

Meaning. On a sloped segment, added energy changes temperature. On a plateau during a first-order phase change at constant pressure, energy changes phase while temperature stays approximately constant.

First move. Mark initial and final phase. Add mcΔT for temperature intervals and nΔH for phase intervals. Keep fusion and vaporization enthalpies distinct.

Worked case. Melting 2.0 mol ice at its melting temperature takes q=2.0ΔHfus without raising its temperature during the melt.

Additional worked example

A liquid at its boiling point absorbs heat and fully vaporizes. Does temperature rise during this idealized phase step?

No. Under constant pressure during the phase change, energy breaks intermolecular attractions and temperature remains at the boiling point.

Test spontaneity under stated conditions

Separate rate from energy barrier

Situation. Temperature or catalyst changes reaction speed.

Meaning. Increasing temperature makes a larger fraction of collisions energetic enough to cross an activation barrier. A catalyst supplies an alternative path with a lower barrier but does not change the overall reaction enthalpy or equilibrium constant.

First move. For a temperature comparison, use the Arrhenius relationship and absolute T. For a catalyst question, compare barriers and distinguish a faster arrival at equilibrium from a changed equilibrium composition.

Worked case. A catalyst lowers both forward and reverse kinetic barriers. Equilibrium is reached sooner, but K at the same temperature is unchanged.

Additional worked example

Can a catalyst turn an unfavorable equilibrium constant into a favorable one at fixed temperature?

No. It changes rates, not ΔG° or K; changing temperature may alter K.

Explanation. A faster reaction need not be more thermodynamically favorable.

Compare current composition with equilibrium

Situation. A reversible reaction gives concentrations before equilibrium.

Meaning. An equilibrium constant compares equilibrium activities in a specified reaction direction. A reaction quotient Q uses the same expression with the current composition.

First move. Write the balanced equation and its K expression, omitting pure solids and liquids. Compute Q; Q<K favors forward change, Q>K favors reverse change, and Q=K is equilibrium.

Worked case. For H₂+I₂⇌2HI with K=4, current [HI]=1, [H₂]=1, [I₂]=1 gives Q=1, so the net change initially proceeds toward HI.

Additional worked example

If Q=9 for a reaction with K=3, which net direction is favored?

Reverse: products are relatively too abundant compared with equilibrium.

Explanation. K says where equilibrium lies, not how fast it will be reached.

Predict a shift without claiming a new rate law

Situation. A system at equilibrium is stressed by concentration, pressure, or temperature.

Meaning. A disturbance changes Q immediately; the system then moves until Q equals the appropriate K. Only a temperature change generally changes K for a given reaction.

First move. Write the reaction and decide what the stress changes first. Use Q relative to K for concentration or pressure. Treat heat as reactant or product for temperature changes.

Worked case. For N₂+3H₂⇌2NH₃, adding H₂ lowers Q and favors product formation. Compressing an ideal-gas mixture favors the side with fewer gas moles.

Additional worked example

At equilibrium, an inert gas is added at fixed volume and temperature. Must the equilibrium composition shift?

No. The reactive gases retain their partial pressures at fixed volume and T, so Q and K do not change.

Explanation. “More pressure favors fewer moles” needs the compression condition; adding inert gas at fixed volume is different.

Read a titration by its chemical region

Situation. A titration curve or volume of titrant is given.

Meaning. Before equivalence, a weak-acid/strong-base titration contains both HA and A⁻. At half-equivalence pH≈pKa; at equivalence the conjugate base makes the solution basic. A strong-acid/strong-base equivalence is near pH 7 at 25 °C.

First move. Locate the initial, buffer, half-equivalence, equivalence, or excess-titrant region before choosing a pH method. Use mole balances to locate equivalence.

Worked case. 20.0 mL of 0.100 M HCl contains 0.00200 mol acid; a 0.200 M NaOH solution reaches 1:1 equivalence after 10.0 mL.

Additional worked example

At half-equivalence of a weak monoprotic acid titrated with strong base, how does pH compare with pKa?

They are approximately equal because the neutralization produces equal HA and A⁻ amounts.

Explanation. Equivalence and pH 7 coincide only for a strong-acid/strong-base titration under ideal standard conditions.

Use Ksp and Qsp for precipitation

Situation. A sparingly soluble salt meets dissolved ions.

Meaning. Ksp describes an equilibrium with the solid omitted from the expression. Qsp is calculated from current ion concentrations; Qsp>Ksp favors precipitation until equilibrium is restored.

First move. Write the dissolution equation first, then the ion-product expression with stoichiometric exponents. Include the dilution that occurs when solutions mix.

Worked case. For MX₂(s)⇌M²⁺+2X⁻, pure-water solubility s gives Ksp=s(2s)²=4s³.

Additional worked example

If Ksp=4.0×10⁻¹² for ideal MX₂ in pure water, estimate s.

4s³=4.0×10⁻¹², so s³=1.0×10⁻¹² and s=1.0×10⁻⁴ M.

Explanation. Comparing two salts by Ksp alone can misrank solubility when their dissociation stoichiometries differ.

Recognize a complex-ion side equilibrium

Situation. A metal ion binds ligands and apparent solubility changes.

Meaning. Ligand binding lowers free-metal-ion concentration. It can pull a dissolution equilibrium forward, so a salt may dissolve more in the presence of a suitable ligand.

First move. Write the metal–ligand formation reaction and its Kf separately from the salt’s Ksp. Use mass balances if a numerical total concentration is required.

Worked case. Ag⁺ binds two NH₃ molecules to form [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺. Reducing free [Ag⁺] can promote dissolution of an Ag-containing solid.

Additional worked example

If a ligand strongly removes free M²⁺ from a solution saturated with MX₂, which way does MX₂ dissolution shift?

Toward more dissolved ions, provided the complex forms and other conditions permit it.

Explanation. A measured total dissolved metal concentration is not the same as free-metal-ion concentration in Ksp.

Read radioactive change as a nuclear balance

Situation. A nuclide decays or a half-life is supplied.

Meaning. Nuclear equations conserve mass number A and atomic number Z. Half-life describes the time for the expected number of undecayed nuclei in a large sample to halve.

First move. For a decay equation, balance A and Z separately. For a half-life problem, count elapsed half-lives or use the exponential form.

Worked case. An 80 mg sample with a 5 day half-life has 40 mg after 5 days and 20 mg after 10 days.

Additional worked example

What fraction remains after three half-lives?

(1/2)³=1/8, or 12.5% of the original radioactive nuclei.

Explanation. Half-life does not mean that every nucleus decays at the same instant.

Recognize a functional group before naming details

Situation. Two organic structures share a formula but differ in behavior.

Meaning. Connectivity and functional groups often predict broad chemical behavior more reliably than a molecular formula alone. Hydroxyl, carbonyl, carboxyl, and amine groups have different bonding patterns.

First move. Count C and heteroatoms, then inspect bonds and group placement. Determine whether the structures are constitutional isomers or merely different drawings of the same connectivity.

Worked case. CH₃CH₂OH and CH₃OCH₃ both have C₂H₆O, but ethanol is an alcohol and dimethyl ether is an ether.

Additional worked example

Are CH₃CH₂CH₂OH and CH₃CHOHCH₃ identical molecules?

No. They are constitutional isomers: the OH attaches to a different carbon.

Explanation. A molecular formula does not uniquely identify a structure or its properties.

Balance charge in a coordination complex

Situation. A metal ion is surrounded by charged or neutral ligands.

Meaning. Coordination number counts donor atoms bound to the metal; oxidation state follows total complex charge. A bidentate ligand counts two donor atoms, even if it is one ligand molecule.

First move. Write each ligand charge and donor count. Solve the metal oxidation state from the overall charge, then sum donor atoms for coordination number.

Worked case. In [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺, NH₃ is neutral: Cu is +2 and four monodentate ligands give coordination number four.

Additional worked example

For [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ with each CN⁻ monodentate, find Fe oxidation state and coordination number.

Six CN⁻ contribute −6. Fe must be +2 to give −4 overall, and six donors give coordination number six.

Explanation. The ligand count is not always the coordination number when a ligand has multiple donor atoms.
