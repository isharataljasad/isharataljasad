Book · Chemistry

1 Matter, measurement, and atomic identity

Classify the sample

Principle. A pure substance has a fixed chemical identity. An element contains one atomic kind; a compound contains fixed ratios of elements. A mixture is a physical combination and may be homogeneous or heterogeneous.

Decision. Ask whether composition can vary and whether separation changes chemical identity.

Worked case. Air is a homogeneous mixture; liquid water is a compound.

Remember. A uniform appearance does not prove a pure substance.

Measure with honest precision

Principle. Measured digits include one estimated digit. Accuracy means closeness to the accepted value; precision means repeatability. Report a result to the precision justified by the least precise measurement.

Decision. For products or quotients, keep the fewest significant figures. For sums or differences, keep the fewest decimal places.

Worked case. 2.4 cm × 3.15 cm = 7.6 cm²; 2.4 + 3.15 = 5.6 cm.

Remember. Do not round intermediate values early.

Convert units as a chain

Principle. Multiply by conversion factors equal to one; cancel units before calculating. Density links mass and volume.

Decision. If the requested unit does not remain after cancellation, the factor is inverted.

Worked case. 25.0 mL × (1 L / 1000 mL) = 0.0250 L.

Remember. Temperature for gas laws must be in kelvin.

Identify atoms and ions

Principle. Atomic number Z counts protons; mass number A counts protons plus neutrons. An ion changes electron count, not proton count. Isotopes differ in neutrons.

Decision. For a cation of charge +q, electrons = Z − q; for an anion of charge −q, electrons = Z + q.

Worked case. ²⁷Al³⁺ has 13 protons, 14 neutrons, and 10 electrons.

Remember. Periodic-table atomic mass is an isotope-weighted average, not a mass number.

2 Formula, mole, and reaction arithmetic

Read a chemical formula

Principle. Subscripts count atoms in one formula unit; coefficients count whole formula units or moles. Ionic formulas must have zero net charge.

Decision. Balance ion charges before reducing subscripts; name transition-metal charge when variable.

Worked case. Al³⁺ and O²⁻ form Al₂O₃.

Remember. Never alter a subscript to balance a reaction.

Move among mass, moles, and particles

Principle. Molar mass converts grams to moles; Avogadro's constant converts moles to entities. The identity of the entity must be stated.

Decision. Use moles as the hub between different chemicals.

Worked case. 18.0 g H₂O ÷ 18.015 g mol⁻¹ ≈ 0.999 mol H₂O.

Remember. One mole of O₂ molecules contains two moles of O atoms.

Find an empirical formula

Principle. Turn each element's mass or percentage into moles, divide by the smallest mole amount, then multiply to clear near-integer fractions. Molecular formula is an integer multiple.

Decision. Check the final ratio against measured molar mass.

Worked case. 40.0% C, 6.7% H, 53.3% O gives CH₂O; a molar mass near 180 g mol⁻¹ gives C₆H₁₂O₆.

Remember. Do not divide percentages directly without first converting to moles.

Balance, then convert

Principle. A balanced equation conserves each atom and total charge. Coefficients supply mole ratios; mass ratios come only after molar-mass conversion.

Decision. Convert given mass → moles → coefficient ratio → desired unit.

Worked case. 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O: 3 mol O₂ could form 6 mol H₂O with enough H₂.

Remember. A coefficient never means a gram ratio.

Find the limiting reactant

Principle. Calculate the product possible from each reactant separately; the smaller product amount is the theoretical yield. Actual yield divided by theoretical yield gives percent yield.

Decision. Compare product moles, not raw reactant masses.

Worked case. N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃: 2 mol N₂ and 3 mol H₂ give at most 2 mol NH₃; H₂ limits.

Remember. Excess reagent can remain even when the reaction stops.

3 Solutions and aqueous reactions

Use molarity and dilution

Principle. Molarity is solute moles per liter of solution. Dilution changes volume and concentration while conserving solute moles if no reaction occurs.

Decision. Use C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ only for dilution of the same solute.

Worked case. 25.0 mL of 2.00 M stock diluted to 100.0 mL becomes 0.500 M.

Remember. Solution volume is not necessarily solvent volume.

Write a net ionic equation

Principle. Separate strong aqueous electrolytes into ions, preserve solids, liquids, gases, and weak electrolytes, then cancel unchanged spectator ions.

Decision. First predict whether a precipitate, gas, or weak electrolyte forms.

Worked case. Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).

Remember. Do not split a solid product into ions.

Track acid, base, and redox

Principle. A Brønsted acid donates H⁺ and a base accepts H⁺. Oxidation loses electrons; reduction gains electrons. Oxidation numbers expose the electron transfer.

Decision. Separate electron transfer from proton transfer before selecting a method.

Worked case. Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu: Zn oxidizes; Cu²⁺ reduces.

Remember. The oxidizing agent itself is reduced.

4 Electrons, periodicity, and bonding

Link light to electronic change

Principle. Photon energy rises with frequency and falls with wavelength. Atomic line spectra arise from discrete electronic energy changes.

Decision. Use wavelength in meters and check whether emission or absorption occurs.

Worked case. A shorter-wavelength photon has greater energy.

Remember. Electron orbitals are probability distributions, not little planetary paths.

Build an electron configuration

Principle. Fill lower-energy orbitals first, obey two electrons per orbital with opposite spins, and place single electrons across equal-energy orbitals before pairing.

Decision. For transition-metal cations, remove electrons from the highest principal shell first.

Worked case. Fe: [Ar] 4s²3d⁶; Fe²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁶.

Remember. A configuration predicts valence behavior; it is not a drawing of fixed electron locations.

Predict periodic trends

Principle. Across a period, effective nuclear attraction generally grows: atomic radius falls while first ionization energy generally rises. Down a group, added shells enlarge atoms.

Decision. Account for subshell and electron-pair exceptions rather than treating trends as exact laws.

Worked case. Na is larger than Cl; Cl attracts a bonding electron pair more strongly.

Remember. Ionic size must be compared at a specified charge and electron count.

Draw Lewis structures

Principle. Count valence electrons (adjust for ion charge), connect atoms, complete outer octets, then form multiple bonds when needed. Minimize unreasonable formal charges.

Decision. For each atom, formal charge = valence electrons − nonbonding electrons − half the bonding electrons.

Worked case. CO₂ has O=C=O and zero formal charges.

Remember. Resonance represents a delocalized structure, not atoms rapidly switching bonds.

Use electron regions to find shape

Principle. VSEPR first counts bonding regions and lone pairs around the central atom. Electron-region geometry includes both; molecular shape names positions of atoms only.

Decision. A double bond counts as one region; lone pairs often compress bond angles.

Worked case. CH₄: tetrahedral; NH₃: trigonal pyramidal; H₂O: bent.

Remember. Polar bonds can cancel in a symmetric molecule such as CO₂.

5 Energy, gases, and phases

Keep energy signs straight

Principle. For the system, heat absorbed is positive and heat released is negative. At constant pressure, reaction heat equals enthalpy change. Reverse a reaction to reverse ΔH.

Decision. Use a Hess-law sum only after every equation has been transformed to the desired net equation.

Worked case. If combustion releases 286 kJ per mole of product, ΔH = −286 kJ mol⁻¹.

Remember. A favorable enthalpy change alone does not establish reaction speed.

Solve calorimetry

Principle. The temperature change is final minus initial. For ideal insulation, heat lost by one part is gained by the other. Use mass of the heated material with its specific heat.

Decision. Check whether the calorimeter itself absorbs heat.

Worked case. 100 g water warmed 5.0 °C absorbs about 2.09 kJ using c = 4.184 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹.

Remember. Heat and temperature are different quantities.

Connect gas variables

Principle. Ideal gas behavior relates pressure, volume, moles, and absolute temperature. At fixed T and n, P is inverse to V; at fixed P and n, V follows T.

Decision. Convert °C to K and use a gas constant consistent with pressure and volume units.

Worked case. At 1.00 atm and 298 K, 1.00 mol ideal gas occupies about 24.5 L.

Remember. At high pressure or low temperature, intermolecular forces and finite particle size matter.

Read a phase diagram

Principle. Each region marks the stable phase at that pressure and temperature. A boundary marks coexistence; the triple point joins three boundaries; the critical point ends the liquid–gas boundary.

Decision. Trace the actual P–T path before naming a phase change.

Worked case. Heating at pressure below the triple point can cause solid → gas directly.

Remember. For water, the solid–liquid boundary has an unusual negative slope.

Connect forces to properties

Principle. Dispersion exists for all particles; dipole interactions require permanent molecular dipoles; hydrogen bonding needs H attached to N, O, or F and a suitable acceptor.

Decision. For comparable size and shape, stronger attractions usually raise boiling point and lower vapor pressure.

Worked case. Water hydrogen bonds; methane has only dispersion forces.

Remember. A polar bond does not automatically make an entire molecule polar.

6 Rates and equilibrium

Separate rate from extent

Principle. A rate describes how quickly concentration changes; an equilibrium constant describes composition at equilibrium. A catalyst lowers the activation barrier in both directions but does not change K.

Decision. Use a rate law only when experimentally established; do not copy exponents from an overall equation.

Worked case. If rate doubles when [A] doubles with other conditions fixed, the observed order in A is one.

Remember. Fast does not mean product-favored.

Use reaction quotient Q

Principle. Write the equilibrium expression from activities, often approximated by concentrations for dilute solutions; omit pure solids and liquids. Compare present Q with K.

Decision. Q < K drives forward; Q > K drives reverse; Q = K is equilibrium.

Worked case. For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, Kc = [NH₃]²/([N₂][H₂]³).

Remember. Changing concentration changes Q immediately, not K at fixed temperature.

Set up an ICE calculation

Principle. Write initial concentrations, coefficient-scaled changes, and equilibrium concentrations. Solve the resulting equation and reject roots that give negative concentrations.

Decision. Test any small-x approximation against the final value.

Worked case. A ⇌ B starting with 0.10 M A: equilibrium A = 0.10 − x, B = x.

Remember. Do not add a solid's concentration to K.

Predict a shift

Principle. A concentration or pressure disturbance changes Q; the system evolves until Q returns to K. Only a temperature change changes K.

Decision. For gas pressure effects, compare gaseous mole counts on both sides.

Worked case. Compressing N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ favors the side with fewer gas moles.

Remember. A catalyst changes arrival time, not equilibrium position.

7 Acid–base and solubility decisions

Translate pH

Principle. pH and pOH are negative base-10 logarithms of hydronium and hydroxide concentrations. At 25 °C, their sum is 14.00.

Decision. Use concentration units mol L⁻¹, and know that 14.00 is temperature dependent.

Worked case. [H₃O⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻³ M gives pH 3.00.

Remember. A one-unit pH change is a tenfold concentration change.

Solve a weak-acid equilibrium

Principle. For HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, Ka measures ionization. With initial HA concentration C and small ionization, [H⁺] ≈ √(KaC); verify x is small relative to C.

Decision. If the approximation fails, solve the full quadratic.

Worked case. Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, C = 0.100 M gives pH ≈ 2.87.

Remember. A weak acid need not have a high pH if it is concentrated.

Recognize a buffer

Principle. A buffer contains a weak acid and its conjugate base in appreciable amounts. The ratio sets pH; total concentration sets how much added acid or base it can withstand.

Decision. Use Henderson–Hasselbalch only when both partners remain after reaction stoichiometry.

Worked case. Equal HA and A⁻ concentrations give pH = pKa.

Remember. At equivalence in a weak-acid/strong-base titration, the buffer pair is gone.

Read a titration curve

Principle. Before equivalence, account for remaining acid/base and formed conjugate; at equivalence, identify the salt's hydrolysis; after equivalence, excess titrant dominates.

Decision. Half-equivalence for a weak acid gives pH = pKa.

Worked case. A weak-acid/strong-base titration has equivalence pH above 7 at 25 °C.

Remember. The steepest point locates equivalence, not necessarily pH 7.

Test precipitation

Principle. Ksp is the equilibrium product of dissolved ion activities raised to stoichiometric powers. Compare the current ion product Qsp with Ksp.

Decision. Qsp > Ksp implies precipitation is favored until equilibrium is reached.

Worked case. For AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺ + Cl⁻, Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻].

Remember. Common-ion addition generally lowers the salt's molar solubility.

8 Spontaneity and electron transfer

Connect ΔG to equilibrium

Principle. At fixed temperature and pressure, negative ΔG favors forward progress. Standard ΔG° reflects the standard state; actual ΔG depends on composition through Q.

Decision. At equilibrium ΔG = 0 and Q = K; a negative ΔG° implies K > 1.

Worked case. ΔG° = −RT ln K.

Remember. A spontaneous reaction can be kinetically slow.

Construct a galvanic cell

Principle. Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode. Electrons travel externally from anode to cathode; ions migrate through the salt bridge to maintain charge balance.

Decision. Compute E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode using reduction potentials; never multiply a potential by stoichiometric coefficients.

Worked case. Cu|Cu²⁺ || Ag⁺|Ag has E° = 0.80 − 0.34 = 0.46 V.

Remember. A galvanic cell has positive Ecell for the spontaneous written reaction.

Understand electrolysis

Principle. An external power supply drives an otherwise nonspontaneous redox change. The electrode names still follow oxidation at anode and reduction at cathode.

Decision. Charge in coulombs divided by Faraday's constant gives moles of electrons.

Worked case. 2 F of charge can deposit 1 mol Cu from Cu²⁺ if current efficiency is 100%.

Remember. Electrode polarity differs between galvanic and electrolytic setups.
