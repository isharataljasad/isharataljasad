Atomic structure

Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons. Z (protons) fixes the element; A counts protons plus neutrons; the charge counts missing or extra electrons.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Definitions

Atomic number Z
Number of protons in the nucleus; identifies the element.
Mass number A
Number of protons + neutrons (a whole number). Neutrons n = A − Z.
Isotopes
Atoms with the same Z but different A (different numbers of neutrons).
Ion
An atom or group of atoms with a net charge due to gained or lost electrons. Cation: positive; anion: negative.
Atomic mass unit (u)
1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom, 1.6605 × 10−27 kg. Proton and neutron masses are each about 1 u; an electron is about 1/1836 u.
Average atomic mass
Abundance-weighted average of isotope masses, as printed on the periodic table.

Formulas and their conditions

Neutrons

n = A − Z

Conditions and limits: A is the mass number of a specific isotope, not the average atomic mass.

Electrons in an ion

electrons = Z − q

Conditions and limits: q is the charge with its sign: Mg2+ (q = +2) has 12 − 2 = 10; Cl− (q = −1) has 17 + 1 = 18.

Average atomic mass

Mavg = Σ (fractional abundance × isotopic mass)

Conditions and limits: Abundances as fractions that add to 1 (divide percentages by 100).

Why chlorine’s atomic mass is 35.45 u

Natural chlorine is 75.78% chlorine-35 (34.969 u) and 24.22% chlorine-37 (36.966 u).

  1. 0.7578 × 34.969 = 26.50 u.

    Contribution of Cl-35.

  2. 0.2422 × 36.966 = 8.953 u.

    Contribution of Cl-37.

  3. Sum: 26.50 + 8.953 = 35.45 u.

    The weighted average.

The average lies closer to 35 because Cl-35 is about three times as abundant. No individual chlorine atom has mass 35.45 u.

Worked example

Neutrons in an isotope

Problem. An atom has mass number 23 and atomic number 11. How many neutrons does it have, and what element is it?

  1. n = A − Z = 23 − 11 = 12.

    Mass number counts protons and neutrons.

  2. Z = 11 is sodium.

    The proton number identifies the element.

Result: 12 neutrons; the atom is sodium-23.

What it means: Any atom with 11 protons is sodium, whatever its neutron number.

Moles, formula reading and composition

The mole connects the particles in an atomic model to the mass measured on a balance. Start by identifying what is being counted: atoms, molecules, ions or formula units. The same number of moles can have different masses because the particles have different masses.

One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 1023 specified entities. This number is the Avogadro constant NA in mol−1. An entity can be an atom, a molecule, an ion or a formula unit; always name it. One mole of O₂ molecules contains two moles of O atoms. One mole of NaCl formula units represents one mole each of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in the ionic lattice.

A subscript belongs to the symbol immediately before it; a subscript outside parentheses multiplies everything inside. Ca(NO₃)₂ contains one Ca, two N and six O atoms per formula unit. A coefficient multiplies the whole formula: 3 Ca(NO₃)₂ represents three formula units, so the atom counts become 3, 6 and 18. Changing a coefficient changes the amount; changing a subscript changes the substance.

Molar mass M is mass per mole, usually expressed in g/mol. Add the listed atomic molar masses, with each multiplied by its atom count. Formula mass in u describes one formula unit; molar mass in g/mol describes a mole. Their numerical values agree to the precision used in introductory chemistry, but their units and meanings differ. Use your course’s periodic-table values consistently.

An empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. A molecular formula gives the actual atom counts in one molecule. C₆H₁₂O₆ has empirical formula CH₂O. Ionic formulas describe neutral ratios in a lattice, not separate molecules. A measured percentage composition gives an atom ratio only after masses have been divided by the corresponding atomic molar masses.

Worked examples for this background

Mass to moles to molecules

Problem. Find the amount and number of molecules in 9.008 g of water. Use H = 1.008 and O = 16.00 g/mol.

  1. M(H₂O) = 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.016 g/mol.

    The subscript 2 applies to H only.

  2. n = 9.008/18.016 = 0.5000 mol.

    Grams divided by grams per mole leaves moles.

  3. N = 0.5000 × 6.02214076 × 1023 = 3.011 × 1023 molecules.

    Multiply by the entity count per mole.

  4. O atoms = N; H atoms = 2N = 6.022 × 1023.

    Every water molecule contains one O and two H atoms.

Result: 0.5000 mol H₂O; 3.011 × 1023 molecules.

What it means: The number of hydrogen atoms is twice the number of water molecules, not the same number.

Parentheses and ionic formula units

Problem. Find the molar mass of calcium nitrate, Ca(NO₃)₂, and the amount of oxygen atoms in 0.250 mol of the compound. Use Ca = 40.08, N = 14.01 and O = 16.00 g/mol.

  1. Counts: Ca = 1, N = 2, O = 6.

    The outside 2 multiplies the complete nitrate group.

  2. M = 40.08 + 2(14.01) + 6(16.00) = 164.10 g/mol.

    Add every atom’s contribution.

  3. Amount of oxygen atoms = 6 × 0.250 = 1.50 mol.

    Each formula unit contains six O atoms.

Result: M = 164.10 g/mol; 1.50 mol of oxygen atoms.

What it means: This counts oxygen atoms chemically present in nitrate; it does not mean 1.50 mol of free O₂ gas is produced.

An empirical formula from percentage composition

Problem. A compound contains 40.00% C, 6.71% H and 53.29% O by mass. Find its empirical formula using C = 12.01, H = 1.008 and O = 16.00 g/mol.

  1. Assume a 100.00 g sample: 40.00 g C, 6.71 g H and 53.29 g O.

    Percentages then become convenient masses without changing the ratio.

  2. Amounts: C = 40.00/12.01 = 3.331 mol; H = 6.71/1.008 = 6.657 mol; O = 53.29/16.00 = 3.331 mol.

    Divide each mass by its own atomic molar mass.

  3. Divide by the smallest amount: C:H:O ≈ 1:2:1.

    Mole ratios equal atom-number ratios. The small mismatch comes from rounded percentages.

Result: Empirical formula CH₂O.

What it means: Do not use the mass ratio 40:6.71:53.29 as subscripts. For a ratio near 1:1.5, multiply all ratios by 2; do not round 1.5 to 2.

From empirical formula to molecular formula

Problem. A molecular compound has empirical formula CH₂O and measured molar mass about 180.16 g/mol. Find its molecular formula using the same atomic masses.

  1. Empirical molar mass = 12.01 + 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 30.026 g/mol.

    Use one empirical formula unit.

  2. k = 180.16/30.026 ≈ 6.000.

    The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.

  3. Multiply every subscript by 6: C₆H₁₂O₆.

    Preserve the 1:2:1 ratio.

Result: Molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆.

What it means: Composition and molar mass identify a molecular formula, not a unique structure. Different compounds can share that formula.

Mass fraction from a formula

Problem. Find the oxygen mass percentage in water using the atomic masses above.

  1. One mole of water contains 16.00 g oxygen in 18.016 g total.

    There is one O atom per water molecule.

  2. Oxygen mass percentage = (16.00/18.016) × 100% = 88.81%.

    Compare masses, not the 1:2 oxygen-to-hydrogen atom ratio.

Result: Water is about 88.81% oxygen by mass with these atomic masses.

What it means: Hydrogen contributes two thirds of the atoms but only about 11.19% of the mass.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: Using an average atomic mass as a neutron count.

    Correct idea: Average atomic mass is used for molar-mass calculations; neutron counts require the mass number of a specific isotope.

  • Misunderstanding: Calling a mole a mass.

    Correct idea: The mole measures amount. The molar mass connects that amount to grams.

  • Misunderstanding: Rounding every empirical ratio immediately.

    Correct idea: Keep several digits, identify a common multiplier, and check against the original composition.

  • Misunderstanding: Changing subscripts to balance a reaction.

    Correct idea: Change coefficients; the formula of each substance stays fixed.

Identify atoms and ions

Principle. Atomic number Z counts protons; mass number A counts protons plus neutrons. An ion changes electron count, not proton count. Isotopes differ in neutrons.

Decision. For a cation of charge +q, electrons = Z − q; for an anion of charge −q, electrons = Z + q.

Worked case. ²⁷Al³⁺ has 13 protons, 14 neutrons, and 10 electrons.

Remember. Periodic-table atomic mass is an isotope-weighted average, not a mass number.

Quantum theory

Light comes in photons with energy E = hν. Electrons in atoms can only have certain energies, so atoms absorb and emit light only at particular wavelengths. Electrons are described by orbitals labelled by quantum numbers.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Definitions

Wavelength λ, frequency ν
λ: distance between wave crests (m); ν: number of waves per second (Hz = s−1).
Photon
A quantum (packet) of light energy, E = hν.
Ground and excited states
The lowest-energy arrangement (n = 1 for hydrogen) and any higher-energy arrangement.
Emission / absorption
An electron dropping to a lower level emits a photon; a photon of exactly the right energy can raise an electron to a higher level.
Orbital
A region of space described by a wavefunction where an electron of given energy is likely to be found; it holds at most two electrons of opposite spin.
Quantum numbers
n = 1, 2, 3, …; l = 0 to n − 1 (s, p, d, f for l = 0, 1, 2, 3); ml = −l to +l; ms = ±½.

Formulas and their conditions

Wave relation

c = λν

Conditions and limits: All electromagnetic radiation in vacuum.

Photon energy

E = hν = hc / λ

Conditions and limits: Energy of one photon. Multiply by NA for a mole of photons.

Hydrogen energy levels (Bohr)

En = −2.18 × 10−18 J / n2

Conditions and limits: Hydrogen atom (one electron) only. Multi-electron atoms need the full quantum model.

Transition energy

ΔE = 2.18 × 10−18 J (1 / nf2 − 1 / ni2) ; |ΔE| = hc / λ

Conditions and limits: Hydrogen. ΔE < 0 for emission (n_i > n_f).

de Broglie wavelength

λ = h / mv

Conditions and limits: Any moving particle; significant only for very small masses such as electrons.

Orbital counts

n2 orbitals and 2n2 electrons per shell; 2l + 1 orbitals per subshell

Conditions and limits: s: 1 orbital, p: 3, d: 5, f: 7.

The red line of hydrogen from the energy levels

Predict the wavelength emitted when an electron drops from n = 3 to n = 2.

  1. ΔE = 2.18 × 10−18(1 / 4 − 1 / 9) = 2.18 × 10−18 × 0.1389 = 3.03 × 10−19 J.

    Energy released (magnitude).

  2. λ = hc / ΔE = (6.626 × 10−34)(2.998 × 108) / 3.03 × 10−19 = 6.56 × 10−7 m.

    Photon energy relation.

  3. = 656 nm, red light.

    1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m.

This matches the measured red line of hydrogen (656.3 nm) — strong evidence that energies are quantised.

Worked example

Energy of a photon

Problem. Find the energy of one photon of green light (λ = 500 nm) and of one mole of such photons.

  1. E = hc / λ = (6.626 × 10−34)(2.998 × 108) / 500 × 10−9 = 3.97 × 10−19 J.

    Convert nm to m first.

  2. Per mole: 3.97 × 10−19 × 6.022 × 1023 = 2.39 × 105 J/mol = 239 kJ/mol.

    Multiply by Avogadro’s number.

Result: 3.97 × 10−19 J per photon; 239 kJ/mol.

What it means: This is comparable to chemical bond energies, which is why visible and UV light can drive chemical reactions (photosynthesis, fading of dyes).

  • Wavefunction mathematics (Schrödinger equation) is described qualitatively only.

Link light to electronic change

Principle. Photon energy rises with frequency and falls with wavelength. Atomic line spectra arise from discrete electronic energy changes.

Decision. Use wavelength in meters and check whether emission or absorption occurs.

Worked case. A shorter-wavelength photon has greater energy.

Remember. Electron orbitals are probability distributions, not little planetary paths.

Build an electron configuration

Principle. Fill lower-energy orbitals first, obey two electrons per orbital with opposite spins, and place single electrons across equal-energy orbitals before pairing.

Decision. For transition-metal cations, remove electrons from the highest principal shell first.

Worked case. Fe: [Ar] 4s²3d⁶; Fe²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁶.

Remember. A configuration predicts valence behavior; it is not a drawing of fixed electron locations.

Periodic table

Electron configurations follow from filling orbitals in order of energy. The periodic table groups elements with similar outer electrons, and effective nuclear charge explains the trends in size, ionisation energy and electronegativity.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Definitions

Electron configuration
The distribution of electrons among orbitals, e.g. O: 1s22s22p4.
Valence electrons
Electrons in the outermost shell (for main-group elements: the ns and np electrons).
Effective nuclear charge Zeff
The net positive charge felt by an electron after shielding by other electrons; roughly Z − (number of core electrons) for valence electrons.
Ionisation energy (IE)
Energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion: X(g) → X+(g) + e−. Successive IEs increase.
Electronegativity
The ability of an atom in a molecule to attract bonding electrons (Pauling scale: F = 3.98 highest).
Paramagnetic / diamagnetic
Having unpaired electrons (attracted by a magnetic field) / all electrons paired (weakly repelled).

Why a large jump in ionisation energy reveals the valence electrons

Magnesium ([Ne]3s²) has IE₁ = 738, IE₂ = 1451 and IE₃ = 7733 kJ/mol.

  1. IE₁ and IE₂ remove the two 3s valence electrons.

    Each removal is harder because the ion becomes more positive.

  2. IE₃ must remove an electron from the 2p core, much closer to the nucleus with far less shielding.

    A new, inner shell.

  3. So IE₃ is about five times IE₂.

    The jump marks the end of the valence shell.

This is why magnesium forms Mg²⁺ but never Mg³⁺ in chemical reactions.

Worked example

Configuration and unpaired electrons of oxygen

Problem. Write the ground-state configuration of oxygen (Z = 8) and find the number of unpaired electrons.

  1. 1s2 2s2 2p4.

    Fill 1s, 2s, then 2p with the remaining four electrons.

  2. 2p has three orbitals: put one electron in each, then pair the fourth: ↑↓ ↑ ↑.

    Hund’s rule.

Result: 1s²2s²2p⁴, with 2 unpaired electrons (oxygen atoms are paramagnetic).

What it means: A 2p³ configuration (nitrogen) would have 3 unpaired electrons.

Scope

  • Quantitative shielding (Slater’s rules), lanthanides and actinides, and detailed transition-metal chemistry are beyond this topic.

Predict periodic trends

Principle. Across a period, effective nuclear attraction generally grows: atomic radius falls while first ionization energy generally rises. Down a group, added shells enlarge atoms.

Decision. Account for subshell and electron-pair exceptions rather than treating trends as exact laws.

Worked case. Na is larger than Cl; Cl attracts a bonding electron pair more strongly.

Remember. Ionic size must be compared at a specified charge and electron count.

Chemical bonding

Atoms bond by transferring electrons (ionic) or sharing them (covalent). Lewis structures count the electrons; VSEPR turns electron domains into shapes; shape and electronegativity decide polarity.

Compare this topic:BookPearsonEducatorFull guide

Definitions

Ionic bond
Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, typically metal cations and non-metal anions, in a lattice.
Covalent bond
A shared pair (single), two pairs (double) or three pairs (triple) of electrons between two atoms.
Polar covalent bond
Unequal sharing due to an electronegativity difference; roughly 0.4–1.8 on the Pauling scale (larger differences are mostly ionic). The cut-offs are only a guide.
Lewis structure
A diagram showing all valence electrons as bonding pairs and lone pairs.
Formal charge
FC = (valence electrons) − (lone-pair electrons) − (number of bonds).
Electron domain
A region of electron density around a central atom: a lone pair or a bond (single, double or triple counts once).
Dipole moment
A measure of charge separation. A molecule with a net dipole is polar.

Formulas and their conditions

Total valence electrons

Σ(valence electrons of atoms) − (ion charge)

Conditions and limits: For anions the charge is negative, so electrons are added: CO₃²⁻ has 4 + 3(6) + 2 = 24.

Formal charge

FC = V − L − B

Conditions and limits: V = valence electrons of the free atom; L = electrons in lone pairs on the atom; B = number of bonds (shared pairs). The formal charges add up to the overall charge.

Ionic formula

total positive charge = total negative charge

Conditions and limits: Ionic compounds are neutral: Al³⁺ and O²⁻ give Al₂O₃ (2 × 3 = 3 × 2).

VSEPR geometry

2 domains: linear 180°; 3: trigonal planar 120°; 4: tetrahedral 109.5°; 5: trigonal bipyramidal; 6: octahedral

Conditions and limits: Domains around the central atom; lone pairs compress angles slightly.

Building the Lewis structure of CO₂

Carbon has 4 valence electrons and each oxygen 6.

  1. Total: 4 + 2(6) = 16 electrons (8 pairs).

    Count first.

  2. Skeleton O–C–O uses 2 pairs; complete the oxygen octets with 3 lone pairs each: 2 + 6 = 8 pairs used.

    Outer atoms first.

  3. Carbon now has only 4 electrons. Move one lone pair from each O into a bond: O=C=O.

    Form multiple bonds to complete the central octet.

  4. Formal charges: C: 4 − 0 − 4 = 0; each O: 6 − 4 − 2 = 0.

    All zero: the best structure.

Two electron domains on carbon (two double bonds) make CO₂ linear, 180°.

Worked example

Counting valence electrons

Problem. How many valence electrons are in CO₂, H₂O and the nitrate ion NO₃⁻?

  1. CO₂: 4 + 2 × 6 = 16.

    C has 4, each O 6.

  2. H₂O: 2 × 1 + 6 = 8.

    Each H has 1.

  3. NO₃⁻: 5 + 3 × 6 + 1 = 24.

    Add one electron for the 1− charge.

Result: 16, 8 and 24.

What it means: Every structure you draw must use exactly this many electrons.

Writing formulas and naming compounds

A name and a formula describe the same chemical composition in different ways. Decide first whether the substance is ionic or molecular; the naming rules depend on that choice. The aim is to account for both atom counts and electric charge.

Ionic compounds contain cations and anions in a ratio with total charge zero. Write the cation first. Use the lowest whole-number ratio of complete ions that gives neutrality; keep every polyatomic ion intact. Calcium Ca²⁺ and chloride Cl⁻ give CaCl₂. Calcium and nitrate NO₃⁻ give Ca(NO₃)₂: parentheses show two whole nitrate ions. Do not reduce the internal subscripts of an ion such as peroxide O₂²⁻.

For an ionic name, name the cation followed by the anion. A one-element anion ends in -ide: chloride, oxide, sulfide. A polyatomic ion keeps its established name: nitrate, sulfate, hydroxide. Metals with more than one common charge need a Roman numeral identifying the charge: iron(II) means Fe²⁺; iron(III) means Fe³⁺. The numeral is not an atom count. Metals with a usual fixed charge, such as sodium or calcium, ordinarily do not need it.

Binary molecular compounds made from two nonmetals usually use prefixes to state atom counts: mono 1, di 2, tri 3, tetra 4, penta 5, hexa 6. The first element normally omits mono; the second ends in -ide and includes its prefix. Standard vowel contractions give monoxide and pentoxide. CO is carbon monoxide; CO₂ is carbon dioxide. A molecular formula is not reduced to its empirical ratio.

Names must also preserve the chemical context. HCl(g) is hydrogen chloride; HCl(aq) is hydrochloric acid. Common introductory oxyacid names follow the anion: nitrate → nitric acid, nitrite → nitrous acid, sulfate → sulfuric acid. These patterns supplement a table of known ions; they do not let you invent an unknown ion’s formula.

Worked examples for this background

Write a formula from two ionic charges

Problem. Write the formula and name for a compound containing Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻.

  1. Two Al³⁺ ions give +6; three SO₄²⁻ ions give −6.

    The smallest common charge magnitude is 6.

  2. Write Al₂(SO₄)₃.

    Three sulfate groups require parentheses around the complete ion.

  3. Name: aluminium sulfate.

    Aluminium has its usual +3 charge; sulfate retains its ion name.

Result: Al₂(SO₄)₃, aluminium sulfate.

What it means: It contains two Al, three S and twelve O atoms per formula unit. AlSO₄ would carry a net +1 charge and is not the neutral formula.

Recover a metal charge from a formula

Problem. Name FeCl₂ and FeCl₃.

  1. Each chloride is Cl⁻. In FeCl₂, two chlorides contribute −2, so Fe is +2.

    The total must be zero.

  2. In FeCl₃, three chlorides contribute −3, so Fe is +3.

    The number of Fe ions is one in both formulas.

  3. Use iron(II) chloride and iron(III) chloride.

    The Roman numeral distinguishes two different metal-ion charges.

Result: FeCl₂: iron(II) chloride. FeCl₃: iron(III) chloride.

What it means: Iron trichloride can occur in other naming conventions, but the charge-based ionic convention used here clearly identifies Fe³⁺.

Preserve a polyatomic cation

Problem. Write the formula of ammonium carbonate.

  1. Ammonium is NH₄⁺; carbonate is CO₃²⁻.

    Retrieve each complete ion and its charge.

  2. Two ammonium ions balance one carbonate: 2(+1) + (−2) = 0.

    Use the smallest neutral ratio of ions.

  3. Write (NH₄)₂CO₃.

    The outside 2 multiplies both N and H within ammonium.

Result: (NH₄)₂CO₃.

What it means: There are 2 N, 8 H, 1 C and 3 O atoms per formula unit. The subscript does not apply only to H.

Molecular prefixes preserve the actual formula

Problem. Name N₂O₄ and write the formula for carbon dioxide.

  1. N₂O₄ has two N and four O atoms: dinitrogen tetroxide.

    The prefix tetra contracts before oxide in the conventional name.

  2. Carbon dioxide has one C and two O atoms: CO₂.

    No mono is normally used for the first element.

Problem. Distinguish HCl(g), HCl(aq) and HNO₃(aq).

  2. HNO₃(aq) is nitric acid; the associated anion is nitrate, NO₃⁻.

Intermolecular forces, phases and solubility

A covalent bond holds atoms together inside a molecule. Intermolecular attractions act between molecules. This distinction explains why a liquid can boil without changing each molecule’s chemical formula.

London dispersion forces arise from fluctuating electron distributions and induced dipoles. They occur between all atoms and molecules, including nonpolar ones. Greater polarizability and more effective contact between molecules can make these attractions stronger. Molar mass is a useful comparison within some related families, but it is not a universal rule.

Dipole–dipole attractions occur between molecules with permanent dipoles. All such molecules also have dispersion forces. Shape matters: CO₂ contains polar C=O bonds, but its bond dipoles cancel, so an isolated CO₂ molecule has no permanent net dipole. Do not assign a molecule’s polarity from one bond alone.

In the usual introductory model, hydrogen bonding needs a donor H covalently bonded to N, O or F and a suitable lone pair on an acceptor, commonly N, O or F. Water can both donate and accept. An ether can accept a hydrogen bond from water but cannot donate one because its hydrogens are bonded to carbon. Hydrogen bonds between molecules are not the covalent O–H bonds inside those molecules.

Ion–dipole attractions occur between an ion and a polar molecule. Water’s partially negative oxygen points towards a cation; its partially positive hydrogens point towards an anion. This helps stabilize dissolved ions. Dissolution still depends on the balance of separating the original particles, forming new interactions and the entropy change. Hydration alone does not guarantee that every salt is soluble.

For comparable molecular substances, stronger net intermolecular attractions often mean higher boiling temperature, lower equilibrium vapor pressure at the same temperature, and larger energy required for vaporization. Comparisons need the same external pressure for boiling points. Molecular size, shape and the set of interactions all matter; there is no fixed ranking that works for every pair of substances.

A liquid boils when its equilibrium vapor pressure equals the external pressure. Lower external pressure allows boiling at a lower temperature. Evaporation can occur below the boiling temperature. In vaporizing molecular water, molecules become widely separated but remain H₂O; making H₂ and O₂ would be a chemical reaction with a different energy requirement.

Worked examples for this background

Compare two molecules with the same molecular formula

Problem. Ethanol CH₃CH₂OH and dimethyl ether CH₃OCH₃ both have formula C₂H₆O. Explain why ethanol is expected to have the higher boiling point at the same pressure.

  1. Both have similar molar mass and both have dispersion forces. Both molecules are polar.

    A fair comparison includes the interactions shared by the substances.

  2. Ethanol has an O–H donor and oxygen lone pairs, so neighboring ethanol molecules can hydrogen-bond.

    The molecular structure supplies both a donor and an acceptor.

  3. Dimethyl ether has oxygen lone pairs but no O–H bond, so pure ether cannot form the same donor–acceptor network.

    An acceptor alone does not supply a hydrogen-bond donor.

Result: Ethanol is expected to boil at the higher temperature because its molecules have additional hydrogen-bonding interactions.

What it means: Dimethyl ether can still accept hydrogen bonds from water. The statement about the pure substance does not prohibit interactions in mixtures.

Explain solubility without an absolute rule

Problem. Explain why water generally mixes more favorably with ethanol than with a nonpolar hydrocarbon such as hexane.

  1. Water molecules form an extensive hydrogen-bond network.

    Separating water molecules changes those interactions.

  2. Ethanol’s OH group can form hydrogen bonds with water.

    New solute–solvent interactions help compensate for the interactions disrupted on mixing.

  3. Hexane cannot form hydrogen bonds with water, although dispersion forces still act between them.

    The full energetic and entropic balance is less favorable for extensive mixing.

Result: Polarity and hydrogen-bond compatibility help explain the difference; “like dissolves like” is a guide, not a complete law.

What it means: Actual solubility depends on temperature and the free-energy balance; do not predict exact solubility from one interaction label.

Vaporize a stated amount of liquid water

Problem. At its boiling temperature under the stated conditions, 0.250 mol of liquid water vaporizes. Take ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol. Find the heat absorbed.

  1. The water is already at the phase-change temperature, so q = nΔHvap.

    No preliminary warming is included in this problem.

  2. q = 0.250 mol × 40.7 kJ/mol = 10.175 kJ.

    Moles cancel, leaving energy.

  3. Report 10.2 kJ absorbed to three significant figures.

    Vaporization requires energy input.

Result: q = +10.2 kJ for the water.

What it means: H₂O molecules remain H₂O in the vapor. For condensation of the same amount under the same conditions, q has the opposite sign.

Changing the pressure changes the boiling temperature

Problem. A liquid boils in a vessel. Explain what happens to its boiling temperature if the external pressure is lowered.

  1. At boiling, the liquid’s vapor pressure equals the external pressure.

    This is the condition to compare.

  2. A lower external pressure can be matched at a lower temperature on the same vapor-pressure curve.

    Equilibrium vapor pressure increases with temperature along the liquid–vapor curve.

Result: The boiling temperature falls when external pressure is reduced.

What it means: This explains vacuum distillation. It does not mean that the substance’s covalent bonds have become weaker.

Common misunderstandings

  • Misunderstanding: Boiling breaks each molecule into atoms.

    Correct idea: Boiling a molecular liquid separates molecules; it normally preserves the covalent bonds within them.

  • Misunderstanding: Every substance containing H can hydrogen-bond to itself.

    Correct idea: Check for an appropriate donor and acceptor; a C–H bond does not supply the usual introductory donor.

  • Misunderstanding: Dispersion acts only in nonpolar molecules.

    Correct idea: Dispersion is present in all atoms and molecules, alongside any other interactions.

  • Misunderstanding: A stronger named force always guarantees a higher boiling point.

    Correct idea: Compare the whole molecules: size, polarizability, shape and all available interactions.

From a molecular equation to a net ionic equation

Silver nitrate solution is mixed with sodium chloride solution.

  1. Molecular: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq).

  2. Complete ionic: Ag⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) + Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) + Na⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq).

    They appear unchanged on both sides.

Where Boyle’s law comes from

Start from PV = nRT and hold n and T constant.

  1. The right side nRT is then a constant, call it k.

    Nothing on the right changes.

  2. PV = k, so P = k/V: pressure is inversely proportional to volume.

    Rearrange.

  3. For two states of the same sample: P1V1 = P2V2.

    Both equal k.

Molecular picture: in half the volume the molecules hit each square metre of wall twice as often, so the pressure doubles.

    n and T constant.

Result: 4.0 bar.

What it means: Halving the volume doubled the pressure.

  2. Cancel CO, which appears on both sides: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g).

    CO is an intermediate.

    Add the enthalpy changes.

Definitions

Half-reaction
The oxidation or reduction part of a redox reaction written separately, showing electrons.
Anode / cathode
Electrode where oxidation / reduction occurs (in every kind of cell).
Galvanic cell
A cell in which a spontaneous redox reaction produces electrical energy.
Electrolytic cell
A cell in which electrical energy drives a non-spontaneous reaction.
Standard reduction potential E°
The potential of a reduction half-reaction relative to the standard hydrogen electrode, with all species in standard states.
Cell notation
Anode on the left, cathode on the right: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s); | is a phase boundary, || the salt bridge.
Faraday constant F
Charge of 1 mol of electrons: 96 485 C/mol.

Formulas and their conditions

Standard cell potential

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

Conditions and limits: Both E° values are reduction potentials from the table; do not reverse the sign of the anode value and then also subtract it.

Spontaneity

ΔG° = −nFE°cell

Conditions and limits: E° > 0 ⇔ ΔG° < 0 ⇔ spontaneous as written (under standard conditions).

Nernst equation (25 °C)

E = E° − 0.0592 V / n log Q

Conditions and limits: T = 298 K; Q is the reaction quotient (products over reactants, with pure solids omitted).

Faraday’s law of electrolysis

n(e−) = It / F; n(product) = n(e−) ÷ (electrons per formula)

Conditions and limits: Assumes 100% current efficiency; t in seconds.

The Daniell cell voltage from the table

Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn has E° = −0.76 V; Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu has E° = +0.34 V.

  1. Copper has the more positive E°, so Cu²⁺ is reduced: copper is the cathode.

    The stronger oxidising agent is reduced.

  2. Zinc is oxidised: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ at the anode.

    The other half-reaction runs in reverse.

  3. E°cell = +0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V.

    Cathode minus anode, both as reduction potentials.

  4. Overall: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s), n = 2.

    Electrons cancel.

ΔG° = −2 × 96 485 × 1.10 = −212 kJ/mol: strongly spontaneous. Zinc metal dropped into copper sulfate solution becomes coated with copper — the same reaction without the wire.

Worked example

Cell potential from two half-reactions

Problem. Cathode and anode reduction potentials are +0.50 V and −0.20 V. Find E°cell. Repeat for +0.80 V and −0.40 V.

  1. E°cell = 0.50 − (−0.20) = 0.70 V.

    Subtracting a negative potential adds its size.

  2. E°cell = 0.80 − (−0.40) = 1.20 V.

    Same rule.

Result: 0.70 V and 1.20 V.

What it means: Both positive: both reactions are spontaneous as written.

Short study notes from the original Book collection for this topic.

Construct a galvanic cell

Principle. Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode. Electrons travel externally from anode to cathode; ions migrate through the salt bridge to maintain charge balance.

Decision. Compute E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode using reduction potentials; never multiply a potential by stoichiometric coefficients.

Worked case. Cu|Cu²⁺ || Ag⁺|Ag has E° = 0.80 − 0.34 = 0.46 V.

Remember. A galvanic cell has positive Ecell for the spontaneous written reaction.

Understand electrolysis

Principle. An external power supply drives an otherwise nonspontaneous redox change. The electrode names still follow oxidation at anode and reduction at cathode.

Decision. Charge in coulombs divided by Faraday's constant gives moles of electrons.

Worked case. 2 F of charge can deposit 1 mol Cu from Cu²⁺ if current efficiency is 100%.

Remember. Electrode polarity differs between galvanic and electrolytic setups.

Background: matter, measurement, formulas and moles

The quantitative foundation that reactions in solution, solutions and gases rely on: classification, measurement, unit conversion, formulas and the mole.

Classify the sample

Principle. A pure substance has a fixed chemical identity. An element contains one atomic kind; a compound contains fixed ratios of elements. A mixture is a physical combination and may be homogeneous or heterogeneous.

Decision. Ask whether composition can vary and whether separation changes chemical identity.

Worked case. Air is a homogeneous mixture; liquid water is a compound.

Remember. A uniform appearance does not prove a pure substance.

Measure with honest precision

Principle. Measured digits include one estimated digit. Accuracy means closeness to the accepted value; precision means repeatability. Report a result to the precision justified by the least precise measurement.

Decision. For products or quotients, keep the fewest significant figures. For sums or differences, keep the fewest decimal places.

Worked case. 2.4 cm × 3.15 cm = 7.6 cm²; 2.4 + 3.15 = 5.6 cm.

Remember. Do not round intermediate values early.

Convert units as a chain

Principle. Multiply by conversion factors equal to one; cancel units before calculating. Density links mass and volume.

Decision. If the requested unit does not remain after cancellation, the factor is inverted.

Worked case. 25.0 mL × (1 L / 1000 mL) = 0.0250 L.

Remember. Temperature for gas laws must be in kelvin.

Read a chemical formula

Principle. Subscripts count atoms in one formula unit; coefficients count whole formula units or moles. Ionic formulas must have zero net charge.

Decision. Balance ion charges before reducing subscripts; name transition-metal charge when variable.

Worked case. Al³⁺ and O²⁻ form Al₂O₃.

Remember. Never alter a subscript to balance a reaction.

Move among mass, moles, and particles

Principle. Molar mass converts grams to moles; Avogadro's constant converts moles to entities. The identity of the entity must be stated.

Decision. Use moles as the hub between different chemicals.

Worked case. 18.0 g H₂O ÷ 18.015 g mol⁻¹ ≈ 0.999 mol H₂O.

Remember. One mole of O₂ molecules contains two moles of O atoms.

Find an empirical formula

Principle. Turn each element's mass or percentage into moles, divide by the smallest mole amount, then multiply to clear near-integer fractions. Molecular formula is an integer multiple.

Decision. Check the final ratio against measured molar mass.

Worked case. 40.0% C, 6.7% H, 53.3% O gives CH₂O; a molar mass near 180 g mol⁻¹ gives C₆H₁₂O₆.

Remember. Do not divide percentages directly without first converting to moles.

Read a phase diagram

Principle. Each region marks the stable phase at that pressure and temperature. A boundary marks coexistence; the triple point joins three boundaries; the critical point ends the liquid–gas boundary.

Decision. Trace the actual P–T path before naming a phase change.

Worked case. Heating at pressure below the triple point can cause solid → gas directly.

Remember. For water, the solid–liquid boundary has an unusual negative slope.

Connect ΔG to equilibrium

Principle. At fixed temperature and pressure, negative ΔG favors forward progress. Standard ΔG° reflects the standard state; actual ΔG depends on composition through Q.

Decision. At equilibrium ΔG = 0 and Q = K; a negative ΔG° implies K > 1.

Worked case. ΔG° = −RT ln K.

Remember. A spontaneous reaction can be kinetically slow.

Separate rate from extent

Principle. A rate describes how quickly concentration changes; an equilibrium constant describes composition at equilibrium. A catalyst lowers the activation barrier in both directions but does not change K.

Decision. Use a rate law only when experimentally established; do not copy exponents from an overall equation.

Worked case. If rate doubles when [A] doubles with other conditions fixed, the observed order in A is one.

Remember. Fast does not mean product-favored.

Use reaction quotient Q

Principle. Write the equilibrium expression from activities, often approximated by concentrations for dilute solutions; omit pure solids and liquids. Compare present Q with K.

Decision. Q < K drives forward; Q > K drives reverse; Q = K is equilibrium.

Worked case. For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, Kc = [NH₃]²/([N₂][H₂]³).

Remember. Changing concentration changes Q immediately, not K at fixed temperature.

Set up an ICE calculation

Principle. Write initial concentrations, coefficient-scaled changes, and equilibrium concentrations. Solve the resulting equation and reject roots that give negative concentrations.

Decision. Test any small-x approximation against the final value.

Worked case. A ⇌ B starting with 0.10 M A: equilibrium A = 0.10 − x, B = x.

Remember. Do not add a solid's concentration to K.

Predict a shift

Principle. A concentration or pressure disturbance changes Q; the system evolves until Q returns to K. Only a temperature change changes K.

Decision. For gas pressure effects, compare gaseous mole counts on both sides.

Worked case. Compressing N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ favors the side with fewer gas moles.

Remember. A catalyst changes arrival time, not equilibrium position.

Translate pH

Principle. pH and pOH are negative base-10 logarithms of hydronium and hydroxide concentrations. At 25 °C, their sum is 14.00.

Decision. Use concentration units mol L⁻¹, and know that 14.00 is temperature dependent.

Worked case. [H₃O⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻³ M gives pH 3.00.

Remember. A one-unit pH change is a tenfold concentration change.

Solve a weak-acid equilibrium

Principle. For HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, Ka measures ionization. With initial HA concentration C and small ionization, [H⁺] ≈ √(KaC); verify x is small relative to C.

Decision. If the approximation fails, solve the full quadratic.

Worked case. Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, C = 0.100 M gives pH ≈ 2.87.

Remember. A weak acid need not have a high pH if it is concentrated.

Recognize a buffer

Principle. A buffer contains a weak acid and its conjugate base in appreciable amounts. The ratio sets pH; total concentration sets how much added acid or base it can withstand.

Decision. Use Henderson–Hasselbalch only when both partners remain after reaction stoichiometry.

Worked case. Equal HA and A⁻ concentrations give pH = pKa.

Remember. At equivalence in a weak-acid/strong-base titration, the buffer pair is gone.

Read a titration curve

Principle. Before equivalence, account for remaining acid/base and formed conjugate; at equivalence, identify the salt's hydrolysis; after equivalence, excess titrant dominates.

Decision. Half-equivalence for a weak acid gives pH = pKa.

Worked case. A weak-acid/strong-base titration has equivalence pH above 7 at 25 °C.

Remember. The steepest point locates equivalence, not necessarily pH 7.

Test precipitation

Principle. Ksp is the equilibrium product of dissolved ion activities raised to stoichiometric powers. Compare the current ion product Qsp with Ksp.

Decision. Qsp > Ksp implies precipitation is favored until equilibrium is reached.

Worked case. For AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺ + Cl⁻, Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻].

Remember. Common-ion addition generally lowers the salt's molar solubility.
