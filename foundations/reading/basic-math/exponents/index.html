What this lesson will settle

An exponent is a compact instruction for repeated multiplication. You will learn to read the base and exponent, expand a power, calculate it, reverse the process when the factors or value are given, and notice when parentheses change the base. This chapter also answers the natural follow-up questions about powers of one and zero. The next chapter shows where powers fit in a longer calculation.

1. The base is the factor; the exponent counts its copies

In 6³, six is the base and three is the exponent. Read it as “six cubed” or “six to the third power.” Its expanded form is 6 × 6 × 6 = 216. Three counts the copies of six; it is not another factor. So 6³ is not 6 × 3.

The same rule works for a letter: x⁴ = x × x × x × x. Until x has a value, that expanded product is already a valid description. An expression such as 3x⁴ has a coefficient as well: it means 3 × x × x × x × x, not four copies of 3x. Parentheses can change that meaning: (3x)⁴ has four copies of the entire product 3x.

The names “squared” and “cubed” are used for exponents two and three. Every positive whole-number exponent follows the same counting rule, whether or not it has a special name.

2. Expand first when the notation feels uncertain

To find 3⁴, write four factors: 3 × 3 × 3 × 3. Pairing them gives (3 × 3)(3 × 3) = 9 × 9 = 81. The grouping helps arithmetic, but does not alter the four factors. To find 4⁵, group five fours: (4 × 4)(4 × 4)4 = 16 × 16 × 4 = 1024.

Going the other way, 7 × 7 × 7 × 7 becomes 7⁴: the repeated factor is the base, and the number of factors is the exponent. 7 × 7 × 5 cannot become a single power of seven because one factor is different; it can be written 7² × 5.

You can check a result's size before calculating. For a base greater than one, adding one more positive exponent multiplies by that base again. Thus 4⁵ must be four times 4⁴, and certainly larger than 4 × 5.

3. Recover the exponent from a value

Suppose a number is 125 and you are required to write it with base five. Divide repeatedly by five, or build powers: 5¹ = 5, 5² = 25, and 5³ = 125. Therefore 125 = 5³. The question specifies the base; without it, there may be another power description. For example, 81 = 3⁴ = 9².

This reverse task is different from evaluating 5³. Evaluation starts with the exponent and finds the value; recovering the exponent starts with the value and a chosen base. A later lesson on prime factorization gives a systematic way to inspect the factors of a larger whole number.

4. Parentheses decide whether a sign belongs to the base

(-2)⁴ means four factors of negative two, so it equals 16. In -2⁴, the power applies to 2 and the leading minus is applied afterward, so the value is -16. If the exponent is three, (-2)³ = -8 because an odd number of negative factors leaves one negative sign. Writing the base inside parentheses removes the ambiguity.

The same idea matters when you substitute a negative number for a variable. If x = -2, then x² = (-2)² = 4. Substitute the whole value inside parentheses before applying the exponent.

5. The edge cases: one factor and no factors

a¹ = a: one copy of the base is simply the base. For any nonzero base, a⁰ = 1. The zero exponent is not saying “multiply zero copies and get zero.” It preserves the pattern a³ ÷ a = a², a² ÷ a = a¹, then a¹ ÷ a = a⁰ = 1. This argument requires a ≠ 0 because division by zero is forbidden. The expression 0⁰ needs a separate convention in advanced contexts and is not assigned a value in this chapter.

Powers can carry units. If a square has side length 3 cm, its area is (3 cm)² = 9 cm²: both the number and its unit are squared. By comparison, 3² cm means 9 cm, a length, not an area. Parentheses again tell us what is raised to the power.

Coverage before questions

You have met the base, exponent, expanded form, numerical value, reverse conversion from repeated factors or a known value, coefficients, signed bases, parentheses, exponents one and zero, and units. These are distinct decisions. Read the relevant section again if a question below depends on one that is still unclear.

Seven question fingerprints

  1. Identify and expand: In 8³, name the base and exponent, then write the equal-factor product.
  2. Evaluate: Find 4⁵ without multiplying four by five.
  3. Compress: Write 6 × 6 × 6 × 2 using a power where possible. Why is it not 6⁴?
  4. Recover: Write 125 as a power with base five; explain how you found the exponent.
  5. Distinguish signs: Calculate both (-3)² and -3².
  6. Substitute: Evaluate 2x³ when x = -2; show the parentheses in the substitution.
  7. Check a boundary and a unit: What are 12⁰ and (4 m)²? Explain why the first rule does not settle 0⁰.

Answers with reasons

  1. The base is eight and the exponent is three. 8³ = 8 × 8 × 8 = 512.
  2. 4⁵ = 4 × 4 × 4 × 4 × 4 = 1024. The exponent counts five factors.
  3. 6³ × 2. Only three factors are six; the final factor is two.
  4. 125 = 5³ because 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. Three copies of five determine the exponent.
  5. (-3)² = (-3)(-3) = 9; -3² = -(3 × 3) = -9. The parentheses decide the base.
  6. 2(-2)³ = 2(-8) = -16. The coefficient two is outside the power.
  7. 12⁰ = 1 because the base is nonzero. (4 m)² = 16 m². The pattern for a zero exponent uses division by the base and cannot divide by zero.
