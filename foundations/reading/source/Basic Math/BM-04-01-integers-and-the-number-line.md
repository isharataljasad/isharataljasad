# Integers and the number line

**Basic Math, episode 04.1 | Early reading edition**

## What this lesson will settle

Counting numbers run one way only. Many real quantities run both ways from a natural zero: a temperature above or below freezing, a depth above or below sea level, money owed or held. Integers extend the counting numbers to describe both directions, and the number line is the picture that makes their order obvious.

By the end you will be able to place an integer on a line, decide which of two integers is larger, name an opposite, read absolute value as a distance, and say why `−8` is smaller than `−3` even though eight is larger than three.

Assumed before this: whole numbers, and [reading and evaluating an expression](BM-01-01-expressions-and-variables.md). Not covered here: adding, subtracting, multiplying or dividing integers, which are the next four episodes. This lesson is about what integers *are* and how they compare.

## 1. An integer is a whole number with a direction

The **integers** are the whole numbers together with their negatives:

`…, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …`

They continue without end in both directions. There is no largest integer and no smallest one. `0` is an integer; it is neither positive nor negative.

A number written without a sign is positive: `7` and `+7` are the same integer. A number written with a minus sign in front, such as `−7`, is negative.

Integers do **not** include values between the whole numbers. `2.5` and `−1/2` are numbers, but they are not integers.

### The minus sign does two different jobs

This is the first place notation causes trouble. The same symbol means two things:

- In `−7`, it is part of the name of a number: negative seven.
- In `12 − 7`, it is an operation: subtract seven from twelve.

You can usually tell from position. A sign directly in front of a number with nothing to its left is part of the number. A sign between two numbers is an operation. When both readings are possible, brackets settle it: `12 + (−7)` adds a negative number, and the bracket exists to keep the two jobs apart.

## 2. The number line orders integers by position

Draw a line, mark a point for `0`, and choose a spacing. Step right by that spacing for `1`, `2`, `3`, and so on; step left the same distance for `−1`, `−2`, `−3`.

Two rules follow from the picture, and they are the whole of ordering:

- Every integer has exactly one position.
- Of any two integers, the one **further right is larger**.

That second rule is the definition of "larger" for integers. It is not a separate fact to memorise, and it never has exceptions.

| Comparison | Reading | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `3 > −8` | three is greater than negative eight | `3` lies right of `−8` |
| `−3 > −8` | negative three is greater than negative eight | `−3` lies right of `−8` |
| `−8 < 0` | negative eight is less than zero | every negative lies left of `0` |
| `0 < 1` | zero is less than one | `0` lies left of `1` |

Every negative integer is less than every positive integer, and less than `0`. No comparison between a negative and a positive ever needs thought beyond that.

## 3. The most common mistake, and why it happens

Many people first read `−8 > −3` because eight is bigger than three. The digits are being compared instead of the positions.

Going left makes a number **smaller**, and `−8` is five steps further left than `−3`. So `−8 < −3`.

A situation makes this concrete. If the temperature is `−3` degrees and it falls by five degrees, it becomes `−8` degrees. It got colder, so the number got smaller. A debt of 8 riyals leaves you worse off than a debt of 3 riyals; `−8` is the smaller amount of money to have.

Whenever a negative comparison feels wrong, place both numbers on a line and look. The picture decides it, not the digits.

## 4. Opposites face each other across zero

The **opposite** of an integer is the integer the same distance from `0` on the other side. The opposite of `5` is `−5`; the opposite of `−5` is `5`. The opposite of `0` is `0`, since `0` is its own reflection.

Writing a minus sign in front of a number means "the opposite of", which is why `−(−3) = 3`. The opposite of negative three is positive three. Read it as an instruction to cross to the other side of zero, and the double sign stops being mysterious.

A pair of opposites always sums to zero: `5 + (−5) = 0`. That is what "same distance, other side" means.

## 5. Absolute value is a distance, so it is never negative

The **absolute value** of an integer is its distance from `0`, written with vertical bars.

`|5| = 5` and `|−5| = 5`, because both are five steps from zero.

A distance has no direction, so an absolute value is never negative. `|0| = 0`.

It is tempting to describe `| |` as "remove the minus sign". That description gives the right answer for `|−5|` and the wrong idea everywhere else. Once you meet `|x − 3|`, there is no minus sign to remove; there is a distance to measure. Learn it as distance from the start.

Two integers that are opposites have the same absolute value and different positions. So absolute value alone never tells you which of two integers is larger: `|−8| > |−3|`, yet `−8 < −3`.

## 6. Reading integers from a described situation

Integers earn their place when a quantity has a natural zero and runs both ways. Choosing which direction is positive is a decision you make and then keep.

| Situation | Natural zero | Sensible choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature in °C | freezing point of water | above zero positive |
| Elevation | sea level | above sea level positive |
| Bank balance | owing nothing | money held positive, money owed negative |
| Time around an event | the event itself | after positive, before negative |

The choice is a convention, not a fact about the world. If you decide depth below the surface is positive, a diver at `30` is thirty metres down, and you must use that meaning consistently for the whole problem. Stating the convention before calculating prevents most sign errors in engineering work.

## Coverage before questions

The explanation has covered what an integer is, the two jobs of the minus sign, how the number line fixes order, why comparing digits misleads for negatives, opposites and double signs, absolute value as distance, and how to choose a sign convention from a situation. The questions below test different parts of that coverage; they are not all the same kind.

## Five question fingerprints

1. **Classify:** Which of `−4`, `0`, `2.5`, `7`, `−1/2` are integers? State why each excluded value fails.
2. **Order:** Arrange `−7`, `3`, `0`, `−2`, `6` from least to greatest, and say which rule decided each comparison.
3. **Opposite and double sign:** Write the opposite of `−12`. Then evaluate `−(−12)` and explain why the two answers match.
4. **Absolute value versus order:** Compare `|−9|` with `|4|`, then compare `−9` with `4`. Explain why the two comparisons point opposite ways.
5. **Apply a convention:** A store's freezer must stay below `−18` °C. A reading of `−15` °C is recorded. Is the freezer within its limit? Justify using position on the line.

:::answer Answers with reasons
(1) Integers: `−4`, `0`, `7`. `2.5` and `−1/2` lie between whole numbers, and integers include no values between consecutive whole numbers.

(2) `−7 < −2 < 0 < 3 < 6`. Each comparison used the same rule: the number further right on the line is larger. `−7` is furthest left, and every negative is left of `0`, which is left of every positive.

(3) The opposite of `−12` is `12`. `−(−12)` reads "the opposite of negative twelve", which is the same instruction, so it is also `12`.

(4) `|−9| = 9` and `|4| = 4`, so `|−9| > |4|`: nine steps from zero is further than four. But `−9 < 4`, because `−9` lies left of `4`. Absolute value measures distance and ignores side; order depends on side. They answer different questions, so they can disagree.

(5) No, it is not within the limit. "Below `−18`" means to the left of `−18` on the line. `−15` lies to the right of `−18`, so `−15 > −18`: the freezer is warmer than its limit, not colder. Comparing the digits 15 and 18 would give the wrong answer here, which is exactly the mistake section 3 describes.
:::
