# Points, lines, and planes

**Geometry, episode 02 | Early reading edition**

## What this lesson will settle

Geometry uses a small vocabulary to describe location and shape. The drawn mark is always a **model**; the mathematical object may have no size or may extend beyond the page. Once that distinction is clear, you can name objects correctly, read their relationships, and turn a statement into a sketch.

## 1. Three objects, three dimensions

| Object | Mathematical meaning | What a drawing hides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Point `A` | An exact location with no length, width, or thickness | The ink dot has visible size. |
| Line through `A` and `B` | A straight, one-dimensional set extending without end both ways | The page shows only a short part; arrows indicate continuation. |
| Plane `M` | A perfectly flat, two-dimensional set extending in every direction | A slanted four-sided patch is a symbol, not the plane's edge. |

Two **distinct** points determine one line. The line contains infinitely many more points. Three **noncollinear** points determine one plane. “Noncollinear” means the three do not all lie on one line; if they did, many different planes could contain them.

A desk corner, a taut thread, and a tabletop can help us picture a point, part of a line, and part of a plane. Real objects have thickness and finite extent, so they are approximations, not the definitions.

## 2. Names must fit positions

Imagine a line `g` drawn in plane `M`. Points `A`, `B`, and `D` lie on `g`; point `C` lies in `M` but off `g`.

The line can be named **line g**, **line AB**, **line BA**, or **line AD**. Reversing two letters does not change the whole line. The plane can be named **plane M**, **plane ABC**, or **plane ACD**. It cannot be identified as “plane ABD”: those three points lie on one line and fail to select one unique plane.

Do not confuse a line with a segment. The **segment AB** stops at its endpoints; the **line AB** continues in both directions. This distinction matters whenever a physical mark, such as a 12 cm scratch, is used to illustrate an ideal line.

## 3. Being in a plane differs from passing through it

A point can lie on a line or off it, and it can lie in a plane or outside it. A line can have three important positions relative to a plane:

| Position of line `h` relative to plane `M` | Shared points |
| --- | --- |
| `h` lies in `M` | Every point of `h`; the intersection is the whole line. |
| `h` pierces `M` at point `T` | Exactly one point, `T`. |
| `h` is parallel to `M` and outside it | No points. |

Picture a pencil resting flat on a sheet versus a pencil pushed through it. The first models a line contained in the plane; the second models a line meeting the plane once. A drawing may show a crossing without clearly showing depth, so rely on the stated relationship when the picture is ambiguous.

## 4. Intersections are complete shared sets

Two distinct lines in one plane may cross at one point or be parallel and share none. In three dimensions, two lines may also be **skew**: they do not meet and are not parallel because they are not in one plane. Two distinct planes can be parallel or, if they meet, share an **entire line**. Coincident planes share the whole plane.

Thus “two planes meet in a line” needs the conditions **distinct** and **nonparallel**. If distinct planes `M` and `N` both contain distinct points `A` and `B`, their intersection is the full line AB, not merely the two named points.

## 5. From words to sketch, then back to words

Consider: “`A`, `B`, and `C` are collinear in plane `M`; `D` lies in `M` but off their line; line `h` pierces `M` at `D`.” Draw a finite patch labeled `M`. Draw an arrowed line inside it and place `A`, `B`, `C` on that line. Place `D` elsewhere on the patch. Draw `h` crossing the patch at `D` and extending to both sides.

Read your sketch back: the first line lies in `M`; `h` shares only `D` with `M`. Plane ABD is a valid three-point name because `D` is off the first line, while plane ABC is not a valid three-point name because all three are collinear. Never infer a right angle, equal lengths, or an unstated intersection just because a sketch looks that way.

## Coverage before questions

The explanation has covered the mathematical objects and their physical models, valid naming, collinearity, line versus segment, points on/off, line-plane positions, line-line and plane-plane intersections, and drawing from a sentence. The questions now test different parts of that coverage.

## Four question fingerprints

1. **Model versus object:** A finite straight ink mark lies on a tabletop. Is the mark literally a mathematical line? What do line arrows represent?
2. **Naming condition:** `A`, `B`, and `D` lie on line `g`; `C` is off `g` but in the same plane. Give two point names for `g`, two three-point names for the plane, and explain why `ABD` fails as a plane name.
3. **Incidence:** Line `h` pierces plane `M` at `T`; line `g` lies in `M`; line `k` is parallel to `M` outside it. State the complete shared set for each line with `M`.
4. **Plane intersection:** Distinct nonparallel planes `M` and `N` both contain `A` and `B`. What is their intersection? Why is “only `A`” incomplete?

## Answers with reasons

1. The finite mark is best modeled as a segment. An ideal line has no thickness and continues without end; the arrows indicate that continuation.
2. Examples for the line are AB and BD. Examples for the plane are ABC and ACD. ABD is collinear and does not identify one unique plane.
3. `h` shares point `T`; `g` shares all of line `g`; `k` shares no points. “Pierces” and “lies in” cannot have the same intersection.
4. Their intersection is the **entire line AB**. A and B are just two named points among infinitely many points on that common line.
