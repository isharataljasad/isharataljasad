# Angles

## What this route covers

This is the practice treatment. One welded joint is set up below and every family asks something about the same three arms, so the naming, the measuring and the reasoning all refer to one object. Commit to an answer before opening each reply. The replies name the error, and most of the errors here come from trusting the drawing instead of the marks on it.

If a ruling surprises you, the [curriculum reference](/foundations/concepts/angles-geometry/approach-1/) states it as a rule, and the [written guide](/foundations/reading/geometry/angles/) explains it at length.

## The one joint used throughout

Three steel arms are welded at a single point `B`. They run out to points `A`, `D` and `C`, with `D` between the other two.

| Given | Value |
| --- | --- |
| `m∠ABD` | `35°` |
| `m∠DBC` | `55°` |
| arm `BA` on the drawing | `40 mm` long |
| arm `BC` on the drawing | `90 mm` long |
| `D` | lies in the interior of `∠ABC` |

The arm lengths are how long they are **drawn**, not a property of the angles.

## Family 1 · Is this name legal?

### Decide: may this joint's angles be called `∠B`?

### Then decide: name all three angles at the joint legally, and say whether `∠ABD` and `∠DBA` are the same angle

:::answer Which names survive, and the letter that decides
`∠B` is **not** legal here. Three rays leave `B`, so `∠B` could mean any of three different angles, and an ambiguous name is not a name.

The three angles are `∠ABD`, `∠DBC` and `∠ABC`. Each needs three letters, and the middle letter of each is `B`, the vertex.

`∠ABD` and `∠DBA` are the same angle. Reversing the outer letters swaps which arm you named first, and the angle between two arms does not depend on the order you mention them.

But `∠BAD` is a different angle entirely — vertex `A`, not `B`. Moving the middle letter moves the vertex, which is the one change that matters.

The error worth naming: reading three-letter names as a longer label for the same thing. The middle letter is doing work; the outer two are interchangeable.
:::

## Family 2 · Does the drawing length matter?

### Decide: arm `BC` is drawn more than twice as long as arm `BA`. Is `∠DBC` therefore larger than `∠ABD`?

Answer from the given measures, then say what the arm lengths contribute.

### Then decide: the fabricator redraws arm `BA` at `90 mm`. What is the new `m∠ABD`?

:::answer What changed, and what did not
`m∠DBC = 55°` and `m∠ABD = 35°`, so `∠DBC` is indeed the larger angle — but **not because of the arm lengths**. It is larger because the given measures say so. The drawing lengths are irrelevant to the comparison, and had the measures been the other way round the longer arm would have belonged to the smaller angle.

After redrawing arm `BA` at `90 mm`, `m∠ABD` is still `35°`. Nothing about the angle changed. The arms are rays, already endless; the page simply shows more of one of them.

This is worth holding onto because diagrams actively mislead here. A long-armed shallow angle looks substantial, and a short-armed wide angle looks minor.

The error worth naming: ranking angles by how much ink they occupy. The measure records the turn between the arms, and a turn has no length.
:::

## Family 3 · Add the measures, and check the precondition

### Decide: find `m∠ABC`, and classify it

### Then decide: which given fact licensed the addition, and what would follow if it were removed?

:::answer The measure, and the phrase that permits the arithmetic
`m∠ABC = m∠ABD + m∠DBC = 35° + 55° = 90°`, so `∠ABC` is a **right angle**.

The addition was licensed by "`D` lies in the interior of `∠ABC`". Without it, the three rays could sit in another arrangement — `D` outside the angle, for instance — and the two measures would not combine to give the third. The relationship would become a claim to test rather than a rule to apply, exactly as with segment addition.

Note what this makes the right angle: a **consequence of arithmetic**, not an observation about the sketch. The joint is square because `35 + 55 = 90`.

The error worth naming: applying the addition because the picture shows one ray sitting between two others. The interior condition is a given in the problem, and a sketch cannot establish it.
:::

## Family 4 · What may you take from the drawing?

The fabrication drawing shows `∠ABC` with no square at the vertex and no stated measure. On the page it looks square.

### Decide: may you use `90°` for it in a strength calculation?

### Then decide: in this problem, is `∠ABC` a right angle — and how do you know?

:::answer Two questions that look the same and are not
You may **not** take `90°` from the drawing. An unmarked angle asserts nothing, however convincing it looks. Only a small square at the vertex, or a stated measure, or a value you derived, may be used.

And yet `∠ABC` **is** a right angle in this problem — because the two given measures add to `90°`. The justification comes from the givens, not from the page.

So the two questions separate cleanly. "Does it look right-angled?" is not evidence. "Do the givens force `90°`?" is. Here the answer happens to be yes, which is the more instructive case: the conclusion is correct and the drawing was still not what established it.

The error worth naming: treating a correct conclusion as a vindication of how you reached it. The same trust in the sketch fails the next time the drawing is a little off.
:::

## What to be able to do before moving on

1. Decide whether a single-letter angle name is legal, and name every angle at a shared vertex unambiguously.
2. State what changes when an arm is redrawn longer, and rank two angles by measure rather than by appearance.
3. Add two measures, classify the result, and name the given that licensed the addition.
4. Say what a diagram asserts and what it does not, and justify a right angle from the givens rather than from the page.
